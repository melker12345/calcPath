# Statistical Inference: Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is a formal framework for making decisions from data. You start with a default assumption (the null hypothesis $H_0$) and ask: is the evidence strong enough to reject it?

The $p$-value quantifies the strength of evidence against $H_0$: the probability of seeing results as extreme as yours, assuming $H_0$ is true. Small $p$-values mean the data is surprising under $H_0$.

Understanding the logic of hypothesis testing — including Type I and Type II errors — is essential for interpreting any scientific study that reports 'statistical significance.'

## The Logic of Hypothesis Testing

<!-- section: z-test -->

<!-- section: basic -->

:::definition[Null and alternative hypotheses]{#hypotheses}
The null hypothesis $H_0$ is the default claim — no effect, no difference, status quo. The alternative $H_a$ is the claim the investigator wants to support.
:::

We begin by assuming $H_0$ is true and ask: how surprising is the observed data under this assumption?

:::definition[Test statistic for a mean]
The test statistic measures how many standard errors the sample result is from the value specified by $H_0$:

$$z = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \ \ (\sigma \text{ known}), \qquad t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \ \ (\sigma \text{ estimated}).$$
:::

Dividing by the standard error puts the result on a universal scale, so a value of $2$ always means "two standard errors away" regardless of the original units. Both formulas assume the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately normal — guaranteed if the population is normal, or by the Central Limit Theorem when $n$ is large (a common rule of thumb is $n \geq 30$).

:::definition[p-value]{#p-value-def}
The $p$-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours, assuming $H_0$ is true:

$$p = P(X \geq x_\text{obs} \mid H_0).$$
:::

Small $p$-values ($p \leq \alpha$) lead to rejection of $H_0$; large $p$-values do not.

One-sided vs. two-sided tests: $H_a: \mu > \mu_0$ is right-sided (one-tailed, $p = P(Z > z_\text{obs})$); $H_a: \mu < \mu_0$ is left-sided; $H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0$ is two-sided ($p = 2P(Z > |z_\text{obs}|)$). Choose the direction of $H_a$ before collecting data.

:::recipe[Testing a hypothesis about a mean]
1. State $H_0$ and $H_a$, and fix the significance level $\alpha$, before looking at the data.
2. Check the conditions that make the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ approximately normal.
3. Compute the test statistic, $z$ or $t$ according to whether $\sigma$ is known or estimated.
4. Convert it to a $p$-value in the tail (or tails) named by $H_a$, or compare it with the critical value at level $\alpha$.
5. Reject $H_0$ when $p \leq \alpha$; otherwise fail to reject. State the conclusion in the language of the original problem.
:::

:::proposition[Tests and confidence intervals are dual]
Reject $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ at level $\alpha$ (two-sided) if and only if the $(1-\alpha)\times 100\%$ confidence interval for $\mu$ does not contain $\mu_0$.
:::

:::intuition
Hypothesis testing is like a criminal trial. $H_0$ is 'innocent until proven guilty.' The $p$-value is the probability of seeing evidence this strong (or stronger) if the defendant really is innocent. If that probability is very small — below $\alpha$ — you convict (reject $H_0$). If not, you acquit — but that doesn't mean the defendant is innocent, just that the evidence wasn't strong enough.
:::

:::example[One-sample z-test (two-sided)]
A machine fills bottles. Claimed mean: $\mu_0=500$ ml, known $\sigma=12$ ml. Sample of $n=36$ gives $\bar{x}=496$ ml. Test $H_0: \mu=500$ vs $H_a: \mu\neq 500$ at $\alpha=0.05$.
:::

:::solution
1. $z = (496-500)/(12/\sqrt{36}) = -4/2 = -2$.
2. $p$-value $= 2P(Z < -2) = 2(0.0228) = 0.0456$.
3. Since $0.0456 < 0.05$, reject $H_0$. Evidence suggests the machine is not filling to $500$ ml.
4. CI check: $496 \pm 1.96(2) = (492.1,\, 499.9)$. Does not contain $500$ — consistent with rejection.
:::

## Type I and Type II Errors

<!-- section: errors -->

<!-- section: errors -->

:::definition[Type I and Type II errors]{#errors-def}
A Type I error is rejecting $H_0$ when it is true (false positive); its probability is $\alpha$. A Type II error is failing to reject $H_0$ when it is false (false negative); its probability is $\beta$.
:::

By choosing $\alpha$ you directly control the probability of a Type I error; common choices are $\alpha = 0.05$, $0.01$, or $0.10$ depending on the consequences of false rejection. In contrast $\beta$ is not directly controlled by $\alpha$ — it depends on $n$, the true effect size, and $\sigma$.

:::definition[Power]{#power-def}
The power of a test is

$$1 - \beta,$$

the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when it exists.
:::

Power increases with larger $n$, larger true effect size, larger $\alpha$, and smaller $\sigma$. The conventional target is $80\%$ power.

The $\alpha$-$\beta$ trade-off: for fixed $n$, lowering $\alpha$ (stricter rejection) increases $\beta$ (more missed effects). Increasing $n$ is the only way to improve power without inflating $\alpha$.

:::proposition[Sample size for a target power]
For a one-sample $z$-test at significance $\alpha$ and power $1-\beta$, the sample size needed to detect a difference $\delta = \mu_a - \mu_0$ is

$$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2}.$$
:::

Here $z_{\alpha/2}$ and $z_\beta$ are the upper-tail critical values: $z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96$ for $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-sided), and $z_\beta = 0.842$ for $80\%$ power ($\beta = 0.20$). Always round the result up, since a fractional sample size does not achieve the target power.

:::intuition
Think of a smoke alarm. Type I error: it goes off when there is no fire (annoying false alarm). Type II error: there is a fire but it stays silent (catastrophic miss). You want both errors to be rare. Making the alarm more sensitive ($\alpha$ larger) catches more fires but also triggers more false alarms. The only way to get both right is a better alarm with more sensors ($n$ larger).
:::

:::example[Sample size for a target power]
A researcher wants to detect a shift of $\delta = 5$ units in a mean, using a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $80\%$ power. The population standard deviation is $\sigma = 10$.
:::

:::solution
1. Look up the critical values: $z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = 1.96$ and $z_\beta = z_{0.20} = 0.842$.
2. Apply the formula: $n = \dfrac{(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)^2\sigma^2}{\delta^2} = \dfrac{(1.96+0.842)^2\,(10)^2}{5^2} = \dfrac{(2.802)^2 \cdot 100}{25}$.
3. Compute the numerator: $(2.802)^2 = 7.851$, so $7.851 \times 100 = 785.1$, and $785.1 / 25 = 31.4$.
4. Round up: $n = 32$. You need at least $32$ observations.
5. Sanity check: halving the detectable difference $\delta$ would quarter the fraction (since $\delta$ is squared), so a harder-to-detect effect demands a much larger sample — as it should.
:::

:::example[Which error, and computing power qualitatively]
A drug truly lowers blood pressure, but a small trial fails to reach $p \leq \alpha$ and concludes "no significant effect." Because $H_0$ (no effect) is false yet was not rejected, this is a Type II error.
:::

:::solution
1. If the trial reported $\beta = 0.35$, its power is $1 - \beta = 1 - 0.35 = 0.65$, i.e. only a $65\%$ chance of catching a real effect this size — below the conventional $80\%$ target.
2. Remedy: increase $n$ (or use a more precise measurement to shrink $\sigma$). Lowering $\alpha$ would only make $\beta$ worse.
:::

## The t-Test Family

<!-- section: t-test -->

<!-- section: t-test -->

:::definition[One-sample t-test]{#one-sample-t}
To test $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ when $\sigma$ is unknown, use

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1} \quad \text{under } H_0.$$
:::

This is the workhorse, because $\sigma$ is unknown essentially always.

:::definition[Two-sample Welch t-test]
To compare $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ from two independent samples without assuming equal variances, use

$$t = \frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}},$$

with degrees of freedom estimated by the Welch-Satterthwaite formula.
:::

:::definition[Paired t-test]
For matched pairs $(x_i, y_i)$, form the differences $d_i = x_i - y_i$ and apply a one-sample $t$-test to them.
:::

The paired test is more powerful than the two-sample test when pairs are correlated — it removes between-subject variability.

:::note
All three $t$-tests share the same conditions: the data (or the differences, for a paired test) should come from an approximately normal population, or the sample should be large enough for the Central Limit Theorem to make $\bar{x}$ approximately normal. Observations must be independent within each group.
:::

The $z$-test uses $\sigma$ known; the $t$-test estimates $\sigma$ with $s$. For $n \geq 30$ the difference is negligible in practice. For small $n$, the heavier tails of the $t$-distribution properly account for the additional uncertainty in estimating $\sigma$.

:::intuition
One-sample $t$: 'Is my sample's mean different from a specific value?' Two-sample $t$: 'Do these two groups have different means?' Paired $t$: 'Are before/after measurements different?' Use paired when you can link observations one-to-one — it gives more statistical power. The heavier tails of the $t$-distribution are like adding a safety margin because you had to *guess* the spread ($s$) instead of knowing it ($\sigma$); with more data the guess is trustworthy and the margin shrinks toward the normal curve.
:::

:::example[One-sample t-test]
A supplier claims parts weigh $\mu_0 = 50$ g. A sample of $n = 16$ gives $\bar{x} = 52$ g with sample SD $s = 4$ g. Test $H_0: \mu = 50$ vs $H_a: \mu \neq 50$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.
:::

:::solution
1. Standard error: $SE = s/\sqrt{n} = 4/\sqrt{16} = 4/4 = 1$.
2. Test statistic: $t = (\bar{x}-\mu_0)/SE = (52-50)/1 = 2$, with $df = n-1 = 15$.
3. Critical value for a two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$ with $15$ df is $t_{0.025,15} \approx 2.13$. Since $|2| < 2.13$, we fail to reject $H_0$.
4. Sanity check: had we (wrongly) used a $z$-test, the cutoff would be $1.96$ and we would have rejected. The $t$-test's wider cutoff reflects the extra uncertainty from estimating $\sigma$ — exactly why it is the correct tool here.
:::

:::example[Welch's two-sample t-test]
Group 1: $\bar{x}_1 = 50$, $s_1 = 4$, $n_1 = 16$. Group 2: $\bar{x}_2 = 45$, $s_2 = 6$, $n_2 = 25$. Test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ (unequal variances not assumed).
:::

:::solution
1. Standard error: $SE = \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2} = \sqrt{16/16 + 36/25} = \sqrt{1 + 1.44} = \sqrt{2.44} \approx 1.562$.
2. Test statistic: $t = (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)/SE = (50-45)/1.562 \approx 3.20$.
3. This exceeds the $\approx 2$ cutoff for any reasonable df, so we reject $H_0$: strong evidence the group means differ.
4. Sanity check: the numerator is $5$ and $SE \approx 1.56$, so $t \approx 3.2$ — the means are about three standard errors apart, which is convincingly large.
:::

:::example[Paired t-test]
Blood pressure before and after treatment for $3$ patients. Differences $d_i = x_{\text{after}} - x_{\text{before}}$: $-3, -5, -4$.
:::

:::solution
1. Mean difference: $\bar{d} = (-3-5-4)/3 = -12/3 = -4$.
2. Sample SD of differences: deviations from $\bar{d}$ are $1, -1, 0$, so $s_d = \sqrt{(1^2+(-1)^2+0^2)/(3-1)} = \sqrt{2/2} = 1$.
3. Test statistic: $t = \bar{d}/(s_d/\sqrt{n}) = -4/(1/\sqrt{3}) = -4\sqrt{3} \approx -6.93$, with $df = n-1 = 2$.
4. With $df=2$, the two-sided cutoff at $\alpha=0.05$ is $t_{0.025,2} \approx 4.30$; since $|-6.93| > 4.30$, $p < 0.05$. Strong evidence treatment lowers blood pressure.
:::

## Chi-Square Test for a Proportion and Goodness-of-Fit

<!-- section: chi-square -->

The chi-square goodness-of-fit test checks whether observed category counts match a hypothesised distribution.

:::definition[Chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic]{#chi-square-stat}
With $k$ categories, observed counts $O_i$ and expected counts $E_i$ under $H_0$,

$$\chi^2 = \sum_i \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i},$$

compared against the chi-square distribution with $k-1$ degrees of freedom.
:::

:::note
The chi-square approximation for goodness-of-fit requires each expected count $E_i \geq 5$; if some categories are too sparse, merge them before testing.
:::

:::definition[One-sample proportion z-test]
To test $H_0: p = p_0$, use

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}},$$

valid when $np_0 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) \geq 10$.
:::

:::definition[Two-proportion z-test]
To test $H_0: p_1=p_2$, use

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2}{SE}, \qquad SE = \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)},$$

with the pooled estimate $\hat{p} = (x_1+x_2)/(n_1+n_2)$.
:::

Pooling is used only because $H_0$ says both groups share one common proportion, so the best estimate of it combines all the data.

:::intuition
The proportion $z$-test asks: 'Is the fraction of successes in my sample far enough from my hypothesised value $p_0$ to be surprising?' A $z$-score of $-2$ means the observed proportion is $2$ standard errors below $p_0$ — which, under a normal approximation, happens only $2.3\%$ of the time by chance.

The goodness-of-fit test is a "tally checker." You have a theory that predicts how many things should land in each bin (a fair die predicts $10$ of each face in $60$ rolls). You count how far each observed tally strays from its prediction, square it so overshoots and undershoots both count, scale by the prediction, and add them up. A big total $\chi^2$ means reality and theory disagree.
:::

:::example[Chi-square goodness-of-fit (fair die)]
Roll a die $n = 60$ times to test $H_0$: the die is fair (each face equally likely). Observed counts: $8, 12, 9, 11, 14, 6$.
:::

:::solution
1. Under $H_0$, each of the $k = 6$ faces is expected $E_i = 60 \times \tfrac{1}{6} = 10$ times. Each $E_i = 10 \geq 5$, so the approximation is valid.
2. Contributions $(O_i - E_i)^2/E_i$: $\tfrac{(8-10)^2}{10}=0.4$, $\tfrac{(12-10)^2}{10}=0.4$, $\tfrac{(9-10)^2}{10}=0.1$, $\tfrac{(11-10)^2}{10}=0.1$, $\tfrac{(14-10)^2}{10}=1.6$, $\tfrac{(6-10)^2}{10}=1.6$.
3. Sum: $\chi^2 = 0.4+0.4+0.1+0.1+1.6+1.6 = 4.2$, with $df = k-1 = 5$.
4. The critical value is $\chi^2_{0.05,\,5} \approx 11.07$. Since $4.2 < 11.07$, we fail to reject $H_0$ — no evidence the die is unfair.
5. Sanity check: the observed counts sum to $8+12+9+11+14+6 = 60 = n$, as they must.
:::

:::example[One-sample proportion z-test]
A candidate claims $50\%$ support. In a poll of $n = 400$, a fraction $\hat{p} = 0.55$ support them. Test $H_0: p = 0.5$ vs $H_a: p \neq 0.5$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.
:::

:::solution
1. Check conditions: $np_0 = 400(0.5) = 200 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p_0) = 200 \geq 10$, so the normal approximation is valid.
2. Standard error under $H_0$: $SE = \sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n} = \sqrt{0.5\times 0.5/400} = \sqrt{0.000625} = 0.025$.
3. Test statistic: $z = (\hat{p}-p_0)/SE = (0.55-0.5)/0.025 = 2$.
4. Two-sided $p$-value $= 2P(Z > 2) = 2(0.0228) = 0.0456 < 0.05$, so reject $H_0$: evidence support differs from $50\%$.
:::

## Multiple Testing and p-Hacking

<!-- section: p-value -->

:::proposition[Family-wise error rate]
If you run $m$ independent tests each at level $\alpha$, the probability of at least one false positive is

$$1 - (1-\alpha)^m.$$
:::

For $m=20$ and $\alpha=0.05$, this is $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64$ — a $64\%$ chance of at least one spurious significant result.

:::definition[Bonferroni correction]
To keep the family-wise error rate at $\alpha$ across $m$ tests, run each individual test at level

$$\frac{\alpha}{m}.$$
:::

Bonferroni is conservative: it is too strict when the tests are positively correlated. The False Discovery Rate (FDR) is the alternative: the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controls the expected fraction of false rejections among all rejections, is less conservative than Bonferroni, and is preferred for exploratory work.

:::pitfall
p-hacking: running many analyses and selectively reporting significant ones. It inflates the Type I error rate and is a major cause of the replication crisis in science.
:::

:::intuition
If you test $20$ hypotheses at $\alpha=0.05$, on average one will be 'significant' just by chance even if none of them are really true. Multiple testing correction is the fix — you demand stronger evidence for each test to keep the overall false-alarm rate at $5\%$.

Think of it like buying lottery tickets. Any single ticket almost never wins ($\alpha = 5\%$). But buy $20$ tickets and your chance of *at least one* win jumps to nearly two-in-three. Bonferroni is the rule "to keep my overall win chance at $5\%$, each ticket must be $20\times$ harder to win."
:::

:::example[Family-wise error and Bonferroni]
Suppose $H_0$ is actually true for all $m = 20$ independent tests, each run at $\alpha = 0.05$.
:::

:::solution
1. Probability a single test does *not* give a false positive: $1 - 0.05 = 0.95$. For all $20$ to avoid it: $0.95^{20} \approx 0.358$.
2. So the probability of *at least one* false positive is $1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 1 - 0.358 = 0.642$ — about a $64\%$ chance of a spurious "significant" result.
3. Bonferroni fix: use per-test level $\alpha/m = 0.05/20 = 0.0025$. Now the family-wise error is $1 - (1-0.0025)^{20} \approx 1 - 0.951 = 0.049 \approx 0.05$, back to the intended $5\%$.
4. Sanity check: with the tighter threshold the chance of at least one false alarm dropped from $64\%$ to $5\%$, exactly the goal of the correction.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Interpreting 'fail to reject $H_0$' as evidence that $H_0$ is true. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- Reporting only the $p$-value without an effect size or confidence interval. A $p$-value of $0.001$ tells you the effect is real; a confidence interval tells you how large it is.
- Using a $z$-test when $\sigma$ is unknown. Use the $t$-distribution whenever $\sigma$ is estimated from the data.
- Choosing the direction of $H_a$ after seeing the data ('data snooping'). The alternative hypothesis must be specified before data collection.
- Running many tests without correction and cherry-picking significant results (p-hacking). Apply Bonferroni or FDR correction for multiple comparisons.
