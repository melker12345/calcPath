# Bayesian Inference

Bayesian inference treats unknown parameters as random variables and updates beliefs using Bayes' theorem as new data arrives. It provides a coherent way to combine prior knowledge with observed data.

The output is a full posterior distribution over possible parameter values rather than a single point estimate. This naturally gives uncertainty quantification and allows direct probability statements about parameters (e.g., 'the probability that the treatment effect is positive is 0.94').

While computationally more demanding than classical methods, modern tools (MCMC, variational inference, Stan, PyMC) have made Bayesian modeling practical for a wide range of problems in science, medicine, and business.

## Bayes' Theorem Revisited

<!-- section: bayes -->

Bayes' theorem tells us how to update the probability of a hypothesis $H$ given data $D$: $P(H|D) = \frac{P(D|H) P(H)}{P(D)}$.

- $P(H)$ is the prior probability — what we believed before seeing the data.
- $P(D|H)$ is the likelihood — how probable the observed data is under hypothesis $H$.
- $P(H|D)$ is the posterior probability — our updated belief after seeing the data.
- $P(D)$ is the marginal likelihood (evidence), which acts as a normalizing constant.

The denominator $P(D)$ is not a separate mystery quantity: when the hypotheses $H_1, \dots, H_k$ are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, the law of total probability gives $P(D) = \sum_i P(D|H_i)\,P(H_i)$. So $P(D)$ is just the sum of the numerator over every hypothesis — exactly what is needed to make the posteriors sum to $1$. This is why we often write posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior and only normalize at the end.

**ELI5**

- You have two coins: one fair, one double-headed. You pick one at random and flip it — heads. Bayes' theorem tells you the probability you picked the double-headed coin. Your prior was 50/50. The likelihood of heads is 1 for the double-headed coin and 0.5 for the fair coin. After seeing heads, your posterior belief shifts toward the double-headed coin.

**Worked Example: two coins**

- Setup: prior $P(\text{DH}) = P(\text{fair}) = 0.5$. Likelihoods for the observed heads: $P(H|\text{DH}) = 1$, $P(H|\text{fair}) = 0.5$.
- Step 1 — evidence (denominator): $P(H) = P(H|\text{DH})P(\text{DH}) + P(H|\text{fair})P(\text{fair}) = 1(0.5) + 0.5(0.5) = 0.75$.
- Step 2 — posterior: $P(\text{DH}|H) = \dfrac{P(H|\text{DH})P(\text{DH})}{P(H)} = \dfrac{1 \times 0.5}{0.75} = \dfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$.
- Sanity check: the other posterior is $P(\text{fair}|H) = \dfrac{0.5 \times 0.5}{0.75} = \dfrac{1}{3}$, and $\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = 1$ ✓. Seeing heads moved our belief in the double-headed coin from $0.5$ up to $0.667$, as expected.

**Worked Example: a rare-disease test**

- Setup: a disease has prevalence $P(D) = 0.01$. A test has sensitivity $P(+|D) = 0.99$ and false-positive rate $P(+|\text{no }D) = 0.05$. You test positive — what is $P(D|+)$?
- Step 1 — evidence: $P(+) = P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|\text{no }D)P(\text{no }D) = 0.99(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$.
- Step 2 — posterior: $P(D|+) = \dfrac{P(+|D)P(D)}{P(+)} = \dfrac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$.
- Sanity check: despite a positive result on a $99\%$-sensitive test, there is only a $\approx 17\%$ chance of having the disease. The rare prior ($1\%$) dominates because false positives among the many healthy people outnumber true positives. This is the classic base-rate lesson — the prior cannot be ignored.

## Prior, Likelihood, Posterior

<!-- section: prior -->

The prior encodes knowledge or beliefs before seeing the data. It can be informative (based on previous studies) or non-informative (trying to let the data speak for itself). A prior is *proper* if it integrates to $1$ (a genuine probability distribution); it is *improper* if it does not (e.g. a "flat" prior $p(\theta) \propto 1$ over an unbounded range). Improper priors are sometimes used to represent ignorance, but they are only safe if the resulting posterior is itself proper — you must check this.

Conjugate priors are mathematically convenient because the posterior is in the same family as the prior, so updating just changes the parameters instead of requiring an integral. The classic example: a Beta prior with a binomial likelihood gives a Beta posterior. Concretely, with a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior and $s$ successes in $n$ trials, the posterior is $\text{Beta}(\alpha + s,\; \beta + n - s)$ — you simply add the observed successes to $\alpha$ and the failures to $\beta$. This makes $\alpha$ and $\beta$ interpretable as "prior pseudo-counts" of successes and failures.

The choice of prior matters when data is scarce; with lots of data the posterior is dominated by the likelihood. This is because the prior contributes a fixed number of pseudo-counts while the data contributes $n$ real counts — as $n$ grows, the data swamps the prior.

Sensitivity analysis (trying different reasonable priors) is good practice in serious Bayesian work.

**ELI5**

- The prior is your starting bet before the game; the likelihood is what the game just showed you; the posterior is your updated bet. A conjugate prior is like keeping score with the same scoreboard the whole time: a Beta prior says "I've imagined $\alpha$ heads and $\beta$ tails already," and each real flip just adds a tally mark to the same scoreboard.

**Worked Example: updating a Beta prior**

- Setup: you suspect a coin is roughly fair, so you use a mildly informative $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ prior (equivalent to having "seen" 1 head and 1 tail already; its mean is $2/(2+2) = 0.5$). You then flip $n = 5$ times and get $s = 4$ heads.
- Step 1 — update the parameters: posterior $= \text{Beta}(\alpha + s,\ \beta + n - s) = \text{Beta}(2 + 4,\ 2 + 1) = \text{Beta}(6, 3)$.
- Step 2 — posterior mean: $\dfrac{6}{6 + 3} = \dfrac{6}{9} \approx 0.667$.
- Sanity check: the raw data proportion is $4/5 = 0.8$, and the prior mean is $0.5$. The posterior mean $0.667$ sits between them — pulled away from the data toward the prior because the prior's 2 pseudo-heads and 2 pseudo-tails still carry weight against only 5 real flips. With more data, the posterior mean would move closer to $0.8$ ✓.

## Bayesian Estimation and Credible Intervals

<!-- section: estimation -->

Instead of a single point estimate, Bayesian inference gives the entire posterior distribution. Common summaries are the posterior mean, median, or mode (MAP).

A 95% credible interval is an interval that contains 95% of the posterior probability mass. Unlike a frequentist confidence interval, you can say 'there is 95% probability that the true parameter lies in this interval' (given the model and data). The frequentist interval, by contrast, makes a statement about the long-run coverage of the *procedure*, not about the single interval you computed.

There is more than one way to pick a 95% interval from the same posterior. The *equal-tailed* interval chops off 2.5% of probability from each tail (using the posterior quantiles). The *Highest Posterior Density* (HPD) interval is the shortest interval containing 95% probability; every point inside it has higher posterior density than every point outside. For a symmetric posterior the two coincide, but for a skewed posterior the HPD interval is shorter and shifted toward the mode.

**ELI5**

- A point estimate is like saying "the treasure is at step 40." A credible interval is saying "I'm 95% sure the treasure is between steps 30 and 55" — and unlike the frequentist version, that 95% really is your probability that *this* range holds the treasure. The HPD interval is the smallest such range you can draw around the spots where the treasure is most likely to be buried.

**Worked Example: Bayesian coin flip**
- You flip a coin 10 times and get 8 heads. You want to estimate the probability $p$ of heads.
- Step 1 — choose a prior: use a $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ prior (uniform on $[0,1]$), meaning "any value of $p$ is equally plausible a priori."
- Step 2 — update: with $s = 8$ successes and $n - s = 2$ failures, the posterior is $\text{Beta}(1 + 8,\ 1 + 2) = \text{Beta}(9, 3)$.
- Step 3 — summarize: posterior mean $= \dfrac{9}{9 + 3} = \dfrac{9}{12} = 0.75$. The posterior mode (MAP) is $\dfrac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} = \dfrac{8}{10} = 0.8$, matching the raw data proportion because the uniform prior adds no pseudo-counts.
- Step 4 — 95% equal-tailed credible interval: the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of $\text{Beta}(9,3)$ are approximately $(0.48,\ 0.94)$.
- Step 5 — a direct probability statement: $P(p > 0.5)$ is the integral of the $\text{Beta}(9,3)$ density from $0.5$ to $1$, which is $\approx 0.97$.
- Sanity check: the mean $0.75$ lies comfortably inside the interval $(0.48, 0.94)$, and since almost all the interval sits above $0.5$ it is consistent with $P(p > 0.5) \approx 0.97$ ✓.

**Worked Example: Normal–Normal shrinkage**
- Setup: you want to estimate a student's true ability $\mu$. Prior belief: $\mu \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_0 = 100,\ \sigma_0 = 15)$ (the population IQ distribution). You observe $n = 4$ independent test scores with sample mean $\bar{x} = 112$, and the measurement noise has known SD $\sigma = 10$.
- Step 1 — work in *precisions* (precision $=$ 1/variance), because Normal–Normal updating is a precision-weighted average. Prior precision $= \dfrac{1}{\sigma_0^2} = \dfrac{1}{225} \approx 0.00444$. Data precision $= \dfrac{n}{\sigma^2} = \dfrac{4}{100} = 0.04$.
- Step 2 — posterior precision is the sum: $0.00444 + 0.04 = 0.04444$, so posterior variance $= \dfrac{1}{0.04444} = 22.5$ and posterior SD $= \sqrt{22.5} \approx 4.74$.
- Step 3 — posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of prior mean and data mean: $\mu_{\text{post}} = \dfrac{0.00444(100) + 0.04(112)}{0.04444} = \dfrac{0.444 + 4.48}{0.04444} \approx 110.8$.
- Sanity check: the posterior mean $110.8$ lies between the prior mean $100$ and the data mean $112$, but much closer to the data because the data precision ($0.04$) is about $9\times$ the prior precision ($0.00444$). This "shrinkage toward the prior" is the same mechanism behind question `bayes-est-2`: had the data been weaker (fewer scores or noisier tests), the estimate would sit closer to $100$ ✓.

## Bayesian Hypothesis Testing and Model Comparison

<!-- section: testing -->

Bayes factors compare the evidence for two competing models: $BF_{12} = P(\text{data} \mid M_1) / P(\text{data} \mid M_2)$. Here each $P(\text{data} \mid M_i)$ is the *marginal* likelihood — the likelihood integrated over that model's prior, $P(\text{data} \mid M_i) = \int P(\text{data} \mid \theta, M_i)\,P(\theta \mid M_i)\,d\theta$ — not the maximized likelihood. Because it averages rather than maximizes, the marginal likelihood automatically penalizes models that are more flexible than the data warrant (an automatic Occam's razor). A consequence is that Bayes factors require *proper* priors on the parameters being tested; an arbitrary improper prior leaves $BF$ undefined up to a constant.

The Bayes factor connects priors to posteriors through the odds form of Bayes' theorem:

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(M_1 \mid \text{data})}{P(M_2 \mid \text{data})}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(\text{data} \mid M_1)}{P(\text{data} \mid M_2)}}_{BF_{12}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(M_1)}{P(M_2)}}_{\text{prior odds}}.$$

So $BF_{12} > 1$ means the data shift the odds toward $M_1$; a common reading (Jeffreys' scale) is that $BF > 3$ is "substantial," $> 10$ is "strong," and $> 100$ is "decisive" evidence, with the reciprocals favoring $M_2$.

Unlike p-values, Bayes factors can provide evidence *for* the null hypothesis as well as against it — a p-value can only fail to reject.

Bayesian model averaging accounts for model uncertainty by weighting predictions by the posterior probability of each model.

Posterior probabilities of hypotheses can be computed directly when using proper priors.

**ELI5**

- Two weather apps each predicted today. The Bayes factor asks: "How much more probable was what actually happened under app 1's forecast than under app 2's?" If today's weather was 3 times as likely under app 1, that's a Bayes factor of 3 in app 1's favor — and you multiply that by how much you trusted each app beforehand to get how much you trust them now.

**Worked Example: comparing two models with a Bayes factor**
- Setup: you test whether a coin is fair. $M_1$: "biased, $p = 0.8$." $M_2$: "fair, $p = 0.5$." You observe $8$ heads in $10$ flips. Assume equal prior odds, $P(M_1) = P(M_2) = 0.5$.
- Step 1 — likelihood under $M_1$: $P(\text{data} \mid M_1) = \binom{10}{8}(0.8)^8(0.2)^2 = 45 \times 0.16777 \times 0.04 \approx 0.3020$.
- Step 2 — likelihood under $M_2$: $P(\text{data} \mid M_2) = \binom{10}{8}(0.5)^8(0.5)^2 = 45 \times (0.5)^{10} = 45 \times 0.0009766 \approx 0.0439$.
- Step 3 — Bayes factor: $BF_{12} = \dfrac{0.3020}{0.0439} \approx 6.9$.
- Step 4 — posterior odds: with equal prior odds, posterior odds $= BF_{12} \times 1 \approx 6.9$, so $P(M_1 \mid \text{data}) = \dfrac{6.9}{6.9 + 1} \approx 0.87$.
- Sanity check: $BF \approx 6.9$ falls in the "substantial-to-strong" range, and $8/10$ heads is indeed closer to $0.8$ than to $0.5$, so favoring $M_1$ is the expected direction ✓.

## Computational Methods

<!-- section: computation -->

For all but the simplest models, the posterior cannot be computed in closed form — the obstacle is almost always the normalizing constant $P(D) = \int P(D|\theta)P(\theta)\,d\theta$, a high-dimensional integral with no analytic solution. We use numerical methods instead.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs sampling, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) generate samples from the posterior. Their key trick is that they need only the *unnormalized* posterior $P(D|\theta)P(\theta)$ — because they work with *ratios* of the target density at two points, the intractable constant $P(D)$ cancels. Once you have samples, any posterior summary (mean, credible interval, $P(\theta > 0)$) is just a summary of the samples.

Modern probabilistic programming languages (Stan, PyMC, Turing.jl) let you specify models in a high-level language and automatically run efficient sampling.

Variational inference approximates the posterior with a simpler distribution that is easier to optimize — useful for very large datasets. It trades exactness for speed: MCMC is asymptotically exact but can be slow, while variational inference is fast but only approximate.

**ELI5**

- The posterior is a mountain range whose exact height you cannot compute because you never know the total "amount of mountain" (the normalizing constant). MCMC is a hiker who wanders the range, preferring to step uphill but occasionally stepping down, and simply records where they stand. Spend long enough and the hiker's footprints pile up in proportion to the height of the terrain — so counting footprints reconstructs the shape without ever measuring the whole mountain.

**Worked Example: one Metropolis step**
- Setup: the current sample is $\theta_{\text{old}}$ where the unnormalized posterior is $\tilde{P}(\theta_{\text{old}}) = 0.20$. You propose a new point $\theta_{\text{new}}$ (using a symmetric proposal) where $\tilde{P}(\theta_{\text{new}}) = 0.30$.
- Step 1 — acceptance ratio: $r = \dfrac{\tilde{P}(\theta_{\text{new}})}{\tilde{P}(\theta_{\text{old}})} = \dfrac{0.30}{0.20} = 1.5$. Note the normalizing constant cancels because it appears in both numerator and denominator.
- Step 2 — acceptance probability: $\alpha = \min(1, r) = \min(1, 1.5) = 1$. The proposal moves uphill, so it is always accepted; the chain moves to $\theta_{\text{new}}$.
- Step 3 — the reverse move: had we instead been at $\theta_{\text{new}}$ and proposed $\theta_{\text{old}}$, then $r = 0.20/0.30 \approx 0.667$, so $\alpha \approx 0.667$ — a downhill move is accepted only about two times in three. Draw a uniform $u \sim U(0,1)$; accept if $u < 0.667$.
- Sanity check: uphill moves are certain, downhill moves are only sometimes taken, and the acceptance never exceeds $1$. Over many steps the chain lingers where $\tilde{P}$ is large, so the sample histogram reproduces the posterior shape — exactly the "footprints pile up by height" idea above ✓.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating the prior as 'just a regularizer' without thinking about what information it actually encodes.
- Using an improper prior without checking that the posterior is proper.
- Interpreting a 95% credible interval as a frequentist confidence interval (or vice versa).
- Ignoring sensitivity to prior choice when sample size is small.
- Thinking Bayesian methods are always 'better' — they require more computational effort and careful prior specification.
