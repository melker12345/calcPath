# Continuous Probability Distributions

Continuous random variables can take any value in an interval. Their distributions are described by probability density functions (PDFs): probabilities are areas under the curve, never the height at a point.

The normal distribution is the cornerstone of statistics. Its bell-curve shape emerges naturally when many small independent effects are added, and it underpins virtually all classical inference methods.

The exponential and uniform distributions complete the toolkit for modeling waiting times, lifetimes, and situations where all outcomes are equally likely.

## PDFs and CDFs

<!-- section: pdf -->

A probability density function (PDF) $f(x)$ satisfies $f(x) \geq 0$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$. Probabilities are areas under the curve: $P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. The PDF height is not a probability — it is a density (probability per unit length).

For a continuous distribution, $P(X = c) = \int_c^c f(x)\,dx = 0$ for any single point $c$. This is why $P(X \leq b) = P(X < b)$ for continuous distributions: adding or removing a single endpoint changes nothing.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is $F(x) = P(X \leq x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$. It is non-decreasing from $0$ to $1$, right-continuous, and gives the probability of being at or below $x$.

The relationship between PDF and CDF: $f(x) = F'(x)$ (derivative of CDF is PDF) and $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$ (antiderivative of PDF is CDF).

Quantiles and percentiles: the $p$-th quantile $x_p$ satisfies $F(x_p) = p$. For the standard normal, $x_{0.975} = 1.96$ — this is where $97.5\%$ of the distribution lies below.

**ELI5**

- A PDF is like a topographic map of probability. The height at any point shows how densely packed the probability is there. But the actual probability of a region is the volume (area) under the curve over that region — not the height at a single point.
- The CDF is the running total: $F(x)$ tells you the total probability that has accumulated by the point $x$. It starts at $0$ (nothing accumulated yet) and ends at $1$ (all probability accounted for).

**Worked Example: Finding a normalising constant, then a probability**
- Suppose $f(x) = c\,x^2$ on $[0, 3]$ and $f(x) = 0$ elsewhere. Find $c$, then compute $P(1 \leq X \leq 2)$.
- Step 1 — impose the total-area condition. A valid PDF must integrate to $1$ over its domain: $\int_0^3 c\,x^2\,dx = 1$.
- Step 2 — evaluate the integral: $\int_0^3 c\,x^2\,dx = c\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3 = c\cdot\frac{27}{3} = 9c$.
- Step 3 — solve: $9c = 1 \Rightarrow c = \frac{1}{9} \approx 0.111$. Note $f(x) = x^2/9 \geq 0$ on $[0,3]$, so the non-negativity condition also holds — this is a valid PDF.
- Step 4 — now find the probability by integrating the density over the interval: $P(1 \leq X \leq 2) = \int_1^2 \frac{x^2}{9}\,dx = \frac{1}{9}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^2 = \frac{1}{9}\cdot\frac{8-1}{3} = \frac{7}{27} \approx 0.259$.
- Sanity check: $[1,2]$ is one-third of the domain's width, so a uniform density would assign it $1/3 \approx 0.333$. But $f(x) = x^2/9$ grows toward $x=3$, piling most mass to the right of our interval, so a probability below $1/3$ is exactly what we expect. And integrating over the full domain returns $\int_0^3 x^2/9\,dx = 1$ ✓.

## The Normal Distribution

<!-- section: normal -->

The normal (Gaussian) distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has PDF $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$. It is symmetric about $\mu$; the inflection points are at $\mu \pm \sigma$.

The empirical (68-95-99.7) rule: $P(\mu-\sigma \leq X \leq \mu+\sigma) \approx 0.6827$; $P(\mu-2\sigma \leq X \leq \mu+2\sigma) \approx 0.9545$; $P(\mu-3\sigma \leq X \leq \mu+3\sigma) \approx 0.9973$.

Standardisation: any $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is transformed to $Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)$. The idea is to measure how many standard deviations $x$ sits above or below the mean; once expressed in those units, every normal curve looks identical, so a single table (the standard normal) serves them all. Standard normal probabilities are read from $z$-tables or computed with software. Because tables list left-tail values $\Phi(z) = P(Z \leq z)$, use $P(Z > z) = 1 - \Phi(z)$ for right tails and $\Phi(z_2) - \Phi(z_1)$ for an interval.

More generally, any linear transformation of a normal is normal: if $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ then $aX + b \sim N(a\mu + b,\ a^2\sigma^2)$ (the variance scales by $a^2$, never by $a$). Standardisation is the special case $a = 1/\sigma$, $b = -\mu/\sigma$.

The normal distribution is additive: if $X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then $X+Y\sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$. Variances add only because $X$ and $Y$ are independent; the sum stays exactly normal (not merely approximately). By induction, a sum of $n$ independent $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ variables is $N(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2)$.

Why normal? By the Central Limit Theorem, the sum (or average) of many independent, identically distributed random variables is approximately normal regardless of the underlying distribution. This explains the normal's ubiquity in nature and statistics.

**ELI5**

- The bell curve is the shape that emerges whenever many small, independent random factors add together. Heights, measurement errors, test scores — they all pile up near the average and thin out toward the extremes. That's the normal distribution, and it appears everywhere precisely because of this 'sum of many small effects' mechanism.

**Worked Example: Normal probability using z-scores**
- Exam scores: $X\sim N(70, 10^2)$. Find $P(60 \leq X \leq 85)$.
- Step 1 — standardise each endpoint: $z_1 = (60-70)/10 = -1$, $z_2 = (85-70)/10 = 1.5$.
- Step 2 — write the interval probability as a difference of left-tails: $P(-1 \leq Z \leq 1.5) = \Phi(1.5) - \Phi(-1)$.
- Step 3 — look up the values ($\Phi(-1) = 1 - \Phi(1) = 1 - 0.8413 = 0.1587$): $0.9332 - 0.1587 = 0.7745$.
- About $77.5\%$ of students score between $60$ and $85$.
- Sanity check: $[60, 85]$ spans from $\mu - \sigma$ to $\mu + 1.5\sigma$, which is a bit wider than the central $\pm 1\sigma$ band (about $68\%$) but narrower than $\pm 2\sigma$ ($95\%$), so $0.77$ sits sensibly between them ✓.

**Worked Example: Percentile-to-score and summing normals**
- IQ scores are $X\sim N(100, 15^2)$. (a) What score marks the $97.5$th percentile? (b) If $4$ people are chosen independently, what is the distribution of their total IQ?
- Part (a) — invert the standardisation. We need $x$ with $F(x) = 0.975$, i.e. the $z$ with $\Phi(z) = 0.975$; that is $z = 1.96$. Then $x = \mu + z\sigma = 100 + 1.96 \times 15 = 100 + 29.4 = 129.4$.
- Part (b) — apply additivity. The sum $S = X_1 + X_2 + X_3 + X_4$ of $4$ independent $N(100, 225)$ variables is $N(4\times 100,\ 4\times 225) = N(400,\ 900)$, so the total has mean $400$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{900} = 30$.
- Sanity check (a): the $97.5$th percentile lies to the right of the mean, so a score above $100$ is expected — and $129.4$ is just under $2\sigma$ above, matching the $\approx 95\%$-within-$2\sigma$ rule ✓. Sanity check (b): the total's SD $30$ is $2\times 15$, not $4\times 15$, because standard deviations grow like $\sqrt{n}$, not $n$ ✓.

## The Exponential and Uniform Distributions

<!-- section: exponential -->

The exponential distribution $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \geq 0$ (and $0$ for $x < 0$). Mean $= 1/\lambda$, variance $= 1/\lambda^2$. It models the waiting time between events in a Poisson process at rate $\lambda$. Its CDF is $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \geq 0$, so the survival probability is the clean $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$ — the workhorse formula for exponential problems.

Memoryless property: $P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$ for $s, t \geq 0$. The past waiting time provides no information about the future wait. This is the continuous analogue of the geometric distribution's memorylessness, and the exponential is the *only* continuous distribution with this property. It follows directly from the survival formula: $\dfrac{P(X > s+t)}{P(X > s)} = \dfrac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t)$.

The uniform distribution $U(a,b)$: $f(x) = 1/(b-a)$ for $a \leq x \leq b$. Mean $= (a+b)/2$, variance $= (b-a)^2/12$. Every sub-interval of equal length is equally probable.

The chi-squared distribution $\chi^2_k$ is the sum of $k$ squared independent standard normals: $\chi^2_k = Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2$ (so in particular $Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$). It is defined only for $x \geq 0$, is right-skewed, and is used in goodness-of-fit tests and inference about variances. Mean $= k$, variance $= 2k$.

The $t$-distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom is the ratio $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ where $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $V\sim\chi^2_\nu$ independently. It is bell-shaped and symmetric about $0$ but heavier-tailed than the normal (the random denominator adds extra spread), converging to $N(0,1)$ as $\nu\to\infty$.

**ELI5**

- Exponential: you're waiting for a bus that comes randomly at an average rate of once every $10$ minutes. The exponential distribution models how long you wait. The memoryless property says: having already waited $5$ minutes tells you nothing — the next bus is still just as far away as when you started.
- Uniform: you pick a random number between $0$ and $1$. Every tiny interval of the same width has the same probability. Completely flat.
- Chi-squared: throw $k$ independent standard-normal darts, square each miss, and add them up — that total is $\chi^2_k$. Squaring forces it non-negative, and adding $k$ of them makes the typical size grow, which is why its mean is exactly $k$.
- $t$-distribution: a normal that has to *estimate* its own spread from a small sample. Because the estimated spread wobbles, the curve is a little shorter and its tails a little fatter than the true normal — and the more data you have (larger $\nu$), the closer it snaps back to $N(0,1)$.

**Worked Example: Exponential waiting time and median**
- Calls arrive at rate $\lambda = 2$ per minute. Find $P(\text{wait} > 1\text{ min})$ and the median wait.
- Step 1 — survival probability. Using $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$: $P(X > 1) = e^{-2\times 1} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$. About a $13.5\%$ chance of waiting more than $1$ minute.
- Step 2 — median $m$ solves $F(m) = 0.5$, i.e. $1 - e^{-2m} = 0.5 \Rightarrow e^{-2m} = 0.5 \Rightarrow -2m = \ln 0.5 \Rightarrow m = \dfrac{\ln 2}{2} \approx \dfrac{0.693}{2} \approx 0.347$ min.
- Sanity check: the mean wait is $1/\lambda = 0.5$ min, and for a right-skewed distribution the median should sit below the mean — indeed $0.347 < 0.5$ ✓.

**Worked Example: Uniform probability and spread**
- Wait time at a crossing is $X\sim U(0, 10)$ seconds. Find $P(3 \leq X \leq 7)$, the mean, and the variance.
- Step 1 — the density is constant: $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{b-a} = \dfrac{1}{10-0} = 0.1$ on $[0,10]$.
- Step 2 — probability of an interval is height $\times$ width: $P(3 \leq X \leq 7) = 0.1 \times (7 - 3) = 0.4$.
- Step 3 — mean $= \dfrac{a+b}{2} = \dfrac{0+10}{2} = 5$; variance $= \dfrac{(b-a)^2}{12} = \dfrac{100}{12} \approx 8.33$.
- Sanity check: $[3,7]$ is $4$ of the $10$ units of width, so it should hold $4/10 = 0.4$ of the (flat) probability — matches ✓.

## Normal Approximation to the Binomial

<!-- section: approximation -->

When $n$ is large and $p$ is not extreme, $X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)$ can be approximated by $N(np,\, np(1-p))$.

Validity condition: $np \geq 10$ and $n(1-p) \geq 10$. When $p$ is close to $0$ or $1$, use the Poisson approximation instead.

Continuity correction: because the binomial is discrete, $P(X = k)$ is approximated by $P(k-0.5 \leq X \leq k+0.5)$ using the normal. For $P(X \leq k)$, use $P(Z \leq (k+0.5-np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)})$.

**ELI5**

- For large $n$, a binomial histogram starts to look like a smooth bell curve. The normal approximation exploits this: instead of summing many binomial terms, you compute one area under the normal curve. The continuity correction patches up the gap between the 'staircase' (binomial) and the 'smooth curve' (normal): each discrete bar for $X = k$ is really a rectangle spanning $[k-0.5,\ k+0.5]$, so to catch its whole area you widen the endpoint by $0.5$ before switching to the normal.

**Worked Example: Normal approximation with continuity correction**
- $X\sim\text{Binomial}(100, 0.5)$. Approximate $P(X \leq 55)$.
- Step 1 — check validity. $np = 100 \times 0.5 = 50 \geq 10$ and $n(1-p) = 50 \geq 10$, so the normal approximation is appropriate.
- Step 2 — match the normal. Use $Y\sim N(np,\ np(1-p)) = N(50,\ 25)$, so $\mu = 50$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
- Step 3 — apply the continuity correction. Since $X \leq 55$ includes the whole bar at $55$, extend the boundary to $55.5$: $P(X \leq 55) \approx P(Y \leq 55.5)$.
- Step 4 — standardise and read the table: $z = \dfrac{55.5 - 50}{5} = 1.1$, so $P(Y \leq 55.5) = \Phi(1.1) \approx 0.8643$.
- Sanity check: $55$ lies just above the mean $50$, so the accumulated probability should be a bit more than $0.5$ — and $0.86$ is above $0.5$ as expected ✓. (Skipping the correction would give $z = 1.0$, $\Phi(1.0) = 0.8413$; the $0.5$ nudge accounts for the missing half-bar.)

## Common Mistakes

- Computing $P(X = c)$ for a continuous random variable. Point probabilities are always zero. Always specify an interval.
- Reading the PDF height as a probability. $f(x)$ can exceed $1$; it is a density, not a probability.
- Applying the normal approximation when $np < 10$ or $n(1-p) < 10$. Use exact binomial or Poisson instead.
- Forgetting the $z$-table gives left-tail probabilities. For $P(X > x)$, use $1 - \Phi(z)$. For $P(a < X < b)$, use $\Phi(z_b) - \Phi(z_a)$.
- Confusing the rate $\lambda$ with the mean in the exponential distribution. Mean $= 1/\lambda$, not $\lambda$.
