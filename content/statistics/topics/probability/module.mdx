# Foundations of Probability

Probability quantifies uncertainty. It assigns a number between $0$ and $1$ to each possible outcome, where $0$ means impossible and $1$ means certain.

Probability theory gives us the language to reason about randomness, and it is the foundation that all of statistical inference is built upon.

We start with basic rules, then build up to conditional probability, independence, and Bayes' theorem — tools used in everything from medical testing to spam filtering.

## Basic Probability Rules

<!-- section: basic -->

:::definition[Sample space and event]
The sample space $S$ is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. An event $A$ is any subset of $S$ — a collection of outcomes we care about.
:::

:::definition[The probability axioms]{#probability-axioms}
A probability $P$ assigns a number to each event so that

- $0 \leq P(A) \leq 1$ (probabilities are fractions of certainty),
- $P(S) = 1$ (some outcome must occur), and
- for **mutually exclusive** events (no shared outcomes),

$$P(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots) = P(A_1)+P(A_2)+\cdots.$$
:::

Every other rule below is derived from these three.

For equally likely outcomes,

$$P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|S|},$$

the number of outcomes in $A$ divided by the total number of outcomes. This shortcut applies *only* when the outcomes are genuinely equally likely (a fair die, a well-shuffled deck); it is wrong for, say, the sum of two dice, where the outcomes $2, 3, \ldots, 12$ are not equally likely.

:::proposition[Complement and addition rules]
For any events $A$ and $B$,

$$P(A^c) = 1 - P(A), \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).$$
:::

The complement rule is often the easier route: compute what you do not want and subtract. In the addition rule, subtracting the intersection corrects the double-counting; for mutually exclusive events there is nothing to subtract and $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

:::intuition
Probability is like assigning percentages to outcomes that must all add up to 100%. If 30% chance of rain, 70% chance of no rain. The addition rule corrects for double-counting: if 40% like cats, 30% like dogs, 10% like both, then cats or dogs = 40+30-10=60%.
:::

:::example[Addition rule with overlap]
One card is drawn from a standard $52$-card deck. Find $P(\text{heart or face card})$, where a face card is a Jack, Queen, or King.
:::

:::solution
1. $P(\text{heart}) = 13/52$, since there are $13$ hearts.
2. $P(\text{face}) = 12/52$, since there are $3$ face cards in each of the $4$ suits, $3 \times 4 = 12$.
3. The overlap "heart AND face card" is the Jack, Queen, and King of hearts, so $P(\text{heart} \cap \text{face}) = 3/52$.
4. Addition rule: $P = \dfrac{13}{52} + \dfrac{12}{52} - \dfrac{3}{52} = \dfrac{22}{52} = \dfrac{11}{26} \approx 0.423$.
5. Sanity check: without subtracting the overlap we'd get $25/52$, double-counting the $3$ cards that are both — the intersection must be removed exactly once ✓.
:::

## Conditional Probability and Independence

<!-- section: conditional -->

Before the formula, the intuition: conditioning means we've been told $B$ happened, so we throw away every outcome outside $B$ and treat $B$ as our new, smaller sample space.

:::definition[Conditional probability]{#conditional-probability}
For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}.$$
:::

The ratio measures how much of that shrunken world is also in $A$. It is defined only when $P(B) > 0$ — you cannot condition on an event that never happens.

:::definition[Independence]{#independence}
Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B).$$
:::

When $P(B) > 0$ this is the same as saying $P(A|B) = P(A)$: knowing $B$ gives no information about $A$. The product form is the one to state, because it stays valid even when $P(B) = 0$ and it makes the roles of $A$ and $B$ interchangeable. Independence is a property you must *verify* or *justify from the setup* (like separate coin flips) — never assume it.

For dependent events (e.g. cards drawn without replacement), rearrange the definition of conditional probability into the multiplication rule.

:::proposition[Multiplication rule]
If $P(A) > 0$, then

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A).$$
:::

:::intuition
Conditional probability is "narrowing the world". $P(\text{rain}\mid\text{cloudy})$ asks: among cloudy days only, what fraction had rain? You ignore all the sunny days entirely. Independence means the world doesn't narrow at all — coin flips have no memory, so hearing "the last three were heads" tells you nothing about the next flip; it's still $1/2$.
:::

:::example[Cards without replacement]
Draw two cards from a standard deck without replacement. Find $P(\text{both hearts})$.
:::

:::solution
1. Let $A$ = "first is a heart", $B$ = "second is a heart". These are *dependent*: taking a heart changes what's left.
2. $P(A) = 13/52 = 1/4$, since $13$ of the $52$ cards are hearts.
3. Given the first card was a heart, $12$ hearts remain among $51$ cards, so $P(B|A) = 12/51$.
4. Multiplication rule: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B|A) = \dfrac{13}{52}\cdot\dfrac{12}{51} = \dfrac{156}{2652} \approx 0.059$.
5. Sanity check: with replacement the two draws would be independent and give $(1/4)^2 = 0.0625$; removing a heart makes the second heart slightly less likely, so $0.059 < 0.0625$ as expected.
:::

## Bayes' Theorem

<!-- section: bayes -->

Often we know $P(A|B)$ but actually want $P(B|A)$ — the conditioning points the wrong way. A test tells us $P(\text{positive}|\text{disease})$, but a patient cares about $P(\text{disease}|\text{positive})$. Bayes' theorem flips the direction of conditioning.

:::theorem[Bayes' theorem]{#bayes-theorem}
If $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$, then

$$P(B|A) = \frac{P(A|B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)}.$$
:::

:::proof
Write $P(A \cap B)$ two ways using the definition of conditional probability: $P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A)$. Dividing by $P(A)$, which is legitimate because $P(A) > 0$, gives the stated formula.
:::

:::notation
- $P(B)$ = prior (belief before seeing the data)
- $P(A|B)$ = likelihood (probability of the data if $B$ is true)
- $P(B|A)$ = posterior (updated belief after the data)
:::

The denominator $P(A)$ is computed with the law of total probability (see below), typically $P(A) = P(A|B)P(B) + P(A|B^c)P(B^c)$.

:::remark
Even a very accurate test can have low positive predictive value for a rare disease, because when almost nobody is sick, the few false positives among the huge healthy population can outnumber the true positives.
:::

:::intuition
Bayes answers "given this evidence, how should I update my belief?" Imagine screening $10{,}000$ people for a rare disease. Only about $100$ are actually sick, but the test's small error rate misfires on a slice of the $9{,}900$ healthy people — and that slice can be bigger than $100$. So a positive result, on its own, is often a false alarm. Bayes bakes in "how rare is it to begin with" instead of trusting the test blindly.
:::

:::example[Medical test PPV]
A disease has prevalence $P(D)=0.01$. The test has sensitivity $P(+|D)=0.99$ and false-positive rate $P(+|D^c)=0.05$. Find $P(D|+)$, the chance a person who tests positive is truly sick.
:::

:::solution
1. Step 1 (prior and complement): $P(D)=0.01$, so $P(D^c)=1-0.01=0.99$.
2. Step 2 (denominator via total probability): $P(+) = P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|D^c)P(D^c) = 0.99\times0.01 + 0.05\times0.99 = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$.
3. Step 3 (Bayes): $P(D|+) = \dfrac{P(+|D)P(D)}{P(+)} = \dfrac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167$.
4. Interpretation and sanity check: only about $17\%$ of positive tests are true positives. This feels surprising, but of the $0.0594$ positive rate, $0.0495$ (most of it) comes from healthy people — exactly the "false positives dominate" effect. As a check, the true and false positive pieces sum to the denominator: $0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594$ ✓.
:::

## Counting Principles

<!-- section: counting -->

We often need to count outcomes before we can divide to get a probability. The workhorse is the multiplication principle.

:::proposition[Multiplication principle]
If a process has independent steps, the total number of outcomes is the product of the number of choices at each step.
:::

For example, $3$ shirts and $4$ pants give $3 \times 4 = 12$ outfits. From that principle, two named formulas handle "pick $k$ of $n$ distinct items".

:::definition[Permutations and combinations]{#permutations-combinations}
For $0 \le k \le n$, the number of ordered selections of $k$ items from $n$ distinct items is

$$P(n,k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!},$$

and the number of unordered selections is

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}.$$
:::

The permutation count is just the multiplication principle applied to $k$ distinct positions: $n$ choices for the first, $n-1$ for the second, down to $n-k+1$ for the $k$-th. The combination count then divides by $k!$, because each unordered group of $k$ items was counted $k!$ times, once per ordering.

:::intuition
Permutations are for a race podium — gold, silver, bronze are different jobs, so the *order* of the three finishers matters. Combinations are for picking committee members — once you're on the committee, it doesn't matter whether you were named first or last. Same people, one committee. Dividing by $k!$ erases the ordering you don't care about.
:::

The binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$ also appears in binomial probability, where it counts the number of ways $k$ successes can be arranged among $n$ trials.

:::example[Ordered vs. unordered from the same pool]
A club of $10$ people must (a) elect a president, vice-president, and treasurer (three distinct offices), and (b) separately pick a $3$-person social committee (no offices).
:::

:::solution
1. Part (a) — order matters, use permutations: $P(10,3) = \dfrac{10!}{7!} = 10 \times 9 \times 8 = 720$.
2. Part (b) — order does not matter, use combinations: $\binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10!}{3!\,7!} = \dfrac{720}{6} = 120$.
3. Sanity check: the only difference between the two questions is whether the $3! = 6$ orderings of a chosen trio count as different outcomes. Indeed $720 / 6 = 120$ ✓, so the committee count is exactly the office count divided by $3!$.
:::

## Law of Total Probability

<!-- section: total -->

Sometimes $P(A)$ is hard to find directly but easy *within each of several scenarios*.

:::definition[Partition of the sample space]
Events $B_1, B_2, \ldots$ form a partition of the sample space when they are mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, and each has $P(B_i) > 0$.
:::

:::theorem[Law of total probability]{#total-probability}
If the events $B_i$ form a partition of the sample space, then for any event $A$,

$$P(A) = \sum_i P(A|B_i)\, P(B_i).$$
:::

You compute $A$'s probability inside each case and take a weighted average, where each case's weight is how likely that case is. This sum is the key denominator for Bayes' theorem.

:::intuition
To get the overall chance of $A$, split the world into non-overlapping cases that cover everything, find the chance of $A$ in each case, then blend them by how likely each case is — exactly like computing a course grade as a weighted average of homework, midterm, and final. The weights (the $P(B_i)$) must add to $1$.
:::

:::example[Two machines, one defect rate]
A factory runs two machines. Machine $A$ makes $60\%$ of parts with a $3\%$ defect rate; machine $B$ makes $40\%$ with a $5\%$ defect rate. What fraction of all parts are defective?
Step 1 (identify the partition): every part comes from $A$ or $B$, and no part comes from both, so $\{A, B\}$ partitions the sample space with $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.4$ (and $0.6+0.4=1$ ✓).
Step 2 (conditional defect rates): $P(D|A)=0.03$, $P(D|B)=0.05$.
Step 3 (total probability): $P(D) = P(D|A)P(A) + P(D|B)P(B) = 0.03\times0.6 + 0.05\times0.4 = 0.018 + 0.020 = 0.038$.
Sanity check: the answer $0.038$ (i.e. $3.8\%$) lies between the two per-machine rates $3\%$ and $5\%$, as any weighted average must, and it sits closer to $3\%$ because machine $A$ carries the larger weight ✓.
:::

:::solution
**Common Mistakes**
Forgetting to subtract the overlap in $P(A \cup B)$ when events overlap.
Confusing mutually exclusive with independent (mutually exclusive events with P>0 cannot be independent).
Swapping $P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$ — Bayes is exactly for converting between them.
Applying independence multiplication rule without verifying independence.
Using permutations when combinations needed (ask: does order matter?).
:::
