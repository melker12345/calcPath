# Sequences & Series

Convergence of sequences, Cauchy sequences, infinite series, absolute convergence, and power series.

A sequence is simply an infinite ordered list of numbers: a₁, a₂, a₃, … . In real analysis sequences are the basic objects whose limiting behavior encodes continuity, derivatives, integrals, and completeness.

We write $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ or simply $(a_n)$. The index $n$ runs through the positive integers.

## Definition and Notation

<!-- section: definition -->

A sequence in $\mathbb{R}$ is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$. We usually denote the value at $n$ by $a_n$ rather than $a(n)$.

The sequence is determined by the rule that produces the $n$-th term. Examples:
- $a_n = 1/n$
- $a_n = (-1)^n$
- $a_n = n^2 / (n + 1)$

The range of the sequence is the set of values that appear; the sequence itself keeps track of order and repetitions.

**ELI5**

- A sequence is like a film strip: each frame $n$ shows the number $a_n$. Even if the picture repeats, the position in time (the index) matters.
- Constant sequence $a_n = 5$ for all $n$ is a perfectly valid (boring) sequence.

**Worked Example:**

The sequence $a_n = n / (n+1)$ has first few terms $1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, \ldots$. Its range is $\{k/(k+1) \mid k \in \mathbb{N}\}$, which approaches but never reaches 1.

## Common Types of Sequences

<!-- section: types-examples -->

- Arithmetic: $a_n = a + (n-1)d$ (constant difference $d$).
- Geometric: $a_n = a r^{n-1}$ (constant ratio $r$).
- Monotonic: either non-decreasing ($a_n \leq a_{n+1}$ for all $n$) or non-increasing ($a_n \geq a_{n+1}$ for all $n$).
- Bounded: there exists $M$ such that $|a_n| \leq M$ for all $n$.
- Eventually constant or periodic.

Many important sequences are defined recursively (Fibonacci, Newton’s method) rather than by a closed formula.

**ELI5**

- Arithmetic = evenly spaced steps on the number line (like 2,5,8,11,…).
- Geometric = each term multiplies the previous by a fixed factor (1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8,…).
- Monotonic sequences only move one way: always up or always down (or stay flat).

**Worked Example:**

Take the geometric sequence with first term $a = 3$ and ratio $r = 1/2$, so $a_n = 3 \cdot (1/2)^{n-1}$: the terms are $3, 1.5, 0.75, 0.375, \ldots$.

- It is monotonic (decreasing), since each term is half the previous: $a_{n+1}/a_n = 1/2 < 1$.
- It is bounded: every term satisfies $0 < a_n \leq 3$.

The partial sums $s_N = 3 + 1.5 + \cdots$ form a *second* sequence. Using the finite geometric-sum formula $s_N = a\,\dfrac{1 - r^N}{1 - r} = 3 \cdot \dfrac{1 - (1/2)^N}{1 - 1/2} = 6\bigl(1 - (1/2)^N\bigr)$. As $N \to \infty$, $(1/2)^N \to 0$, so $s_N \to 6$.

Sanity check: $s_1 = 3$, $s_2 = 4.5$, $s_3 = 5.25$, $s_4 = 5.625$ — creeping up toward but never exceeding $6$. $\checkmark$

## Convergence and Divergence — First Look

<!-- section: convergence-first-look -->

We say the sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ (written $a_n \to L$ or $\lim a_n = L$) when the terms eventually get and stay arbitrarily close to $L$.

If no such $L$ exists the sequence diverges. Classic divergent examples: $a_n = n$ (goes to $+\infty$), $a_n = (-1)^n$ (oscillates), $a_n = n \sin n$ (unbounded oscillation).

A sequence can diverge even if it is bounded (oscillation).

**ELI5**

- Convergence = the numbers on the film strip eventually settle down near one fixed target and never wander far again.
- Divergence by oscillation = the film keeps flipping between two distant pictures forever.

**Worked Example:**

$a_n = 1/n \to 0$: given any tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$ (say $0.001$), after $N = \lceil 1/\varepsilon \rceil$ all later frames have $|a_n| < \varepsilon$. The terms get visibly smaller on the number line.

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing the sequence with its range set**: The set $\{1, 1/2, 1/3, \ldots\}$ is the same for $a_n = 1/n$ and for a rearranged listing; the sequence remembers the order of appearance.
- **Thinking every sequence has a limit**: Bounded sequences need not converge (oscillating ones); unbounded ones usually diverge to ±∞.
- **Ignoring the 'eventually' quantifier**: Early wild terms do not prevent convergence; only the tail matters.
- **Writing $\lim a_n = \infty$ as if $\infty$ is a real number**: In real analysis we say the sequence diverges to $+\infty$; the limit does not exist in $\mathbb{R}$.
- **Forgetting that constant sequences converge**: They converge (trivially) to their constant value.

The notion of the limit of a sequence is the precise mathematical embodiment of the idea that the terms "eventually get and stay close" to a number L. It is the foundation on which continuity, derivatives, integrals, and infinite series are built.

The ε-N definition replaces vague phrases such as "approaches" or "gets closer" with a clear, quantifier-driven statement that can be used in proofs.

## The ε-N Definition

<!-- section: epsilon-definition -->

We say that the sequence $(a_n)$ converges to $L$ ($\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = L$) if:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a natural number $N$ such that for all $n > N$ we have $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$.

L is called the limit. If no such L exists the sequence diverges.

The definition only cares about the tail of the sequence; any finite number of initial terms can be ignored.

**ELI5**

- ε is your "tolerance" or "how close is close enough". N tells you "after this frame in the movie, every subsequent frame is inside your tolerance of L".
- The quantifiers are: no matter which tolerance ε you name (even a tiny one), I can name a moment N after which the promise holds forever.

**Worked Example:**

Prove that $a_n = 2 + 1/n \to 2$.

- **Setup.** We must show: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an $N$ with $|a_n - 2| < \varepsilon$ for all $n > N$.
- **Simplify the target.** $|a_n - 2| = |(2 + 1/n) - 2| = 1/n$. So we need $1/n < \varepsilon$, i.e. $n > 1/\varepsilon$.
- **Choose $N$.** Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given and set $N = \lceil 1/\varepsilon \rceil$ (any integer $\geq 1/\varepsilon$).
- **Verify.** For any $n > N$ we have $n > 1/\varepsilon$, hence $1/n < \varepsilon$, hence $|a_n - 2| = 1/n < \varepsilon$. $\checkmark$

Concrete check: for $\varepsilon = 0.01$ we take $N = 100$; then $a_{101} = 2 + 1/101 \approx 2.0099$, and indeed $|a_{101} - 2| \approx 0.0099 < 0.01$. $\checkmark$

## Uniqueness of Limits and Basic Properties

<!-- section: uniqueness-algebra -->

If a sequence converges, its limit is unique. (Proof: suppose two limits L and M, L ≠ M. Take ε = |L−M|/2; the sequence cannot be eventually inside both ε-balls.)

Algebra of limits: if $a_n \to L$ and $b_n \to M$ then $a_n + b_n \to L+M$, $c\,a_n \to cL$ for any constant $c$, $a_n b_n \to LM$, and (provided $M \neq 0$, and the terms $b_n$ are eventually nonzero) $a_n / b_n \to L/M$.

These rules apply *only after* you already know both individual limits $L$ and $M$ exist; you cannot use them to conclude a limit exists.

Also constant sequences converge to their constant, and if $a_n \to L$ then $|a_n| \to |L|$.

**ELI5**

- A sequence cannot have two different "settling points"; if it did, the distance between those points would give a tolerance that the tail cannot satisfy for both.
- Limits respect the usual arithmetic operations (with the usual caveat about division by zero).

**Worked Example:**

Given $a_n \to 3$ and $b_n \to 4$, compute $\lim (a_n + 2 b_n)$.

- By the scalar-multiple rule, $2 b_n \to 2 \cdot 4 = 8$.
- By the sum rule, $a_n + 2 b_n \to 3 + 8 = 11$.

So $\lim (a_n + 2 b_n) = 11$. Note we could apply these rules precisely because both $\lim a_n$ and $\lim b_n$ were already known to exist.

A second example using the quotient rule: if $a_n = \dfrac{2n + 1}{n}$, divide top and bottom by $n$ to get $a_n = 2 + \dfrac{1}{n}$. Since the numerator interpretation $2 + 1/n \to 2$ and this uses only the sum rule with $1/n \to 0$, we get $a_n \to 2$. $\checkmark$

## Squeeze Theorem and Standard Limits

<!-- section: squeeze-standard-limits -->

Squeeze (sandwich) theorem: if $g_n \leq a_n \leq h_n$ for all large $n$ and both $g_n \to L$ and $h_n \to L$, then $a_n \to L$.

Classic limits:
- $1/n \to 0$
- $r^n \to 0$ when $|r| < 1$
- $n^{1/n} \to 1$
- $(1 + 1/n)^n \to e$

These are proved using binomial theorem, logs, or squeeze.

**ELI5**

- If a number is trapped between two other numbers that are both squeezing toward the same value, it has nowhere else to go.
- The squeeze theorem is the analysis version of "guilt by association."

**Worked Example:**

Show that $a_n = \sin(n)/n \to 0$. Because $-1 \leq \sin(n) \leq 1$, we have $-1/n \leq \sin(n)/n \leq 1/n$. Both $-1/n$ and $1/n \to 0$, therefore by squeeze $\sin(n)/n \to 0$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reversing the quantifiers**: Students often write 'there exists ε >0 such that for all N …' which is useless. The correct order is 'for all ε there exists N'.
- **Thinking the N depends on the term n**: N is chosen once for the given ε and then works for every larger n.
- **Forgetting to prove the limit exists before using algebra rules**: You may only apply limit laws after you already know the individual limits exist.
- **Writing $\lim a_n = \infty$ inside $\mathbb{R}$ proofs**: In the strict sense the limit does not exist; we say diverges to infinity.
- **Ignoring that the definition uses strict inequality $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$**: Using $\leq$ is harmless but the definition is stated with $<$.

A Cauchy sequence is one in which the terms eventually become arbitrarily close to each other, regardless of any candidate limit. The Cauchy criterion is often easier to check than producing a limit in advance, and in a complete space (such as ℝ) every Cauchy sequence converges.

This concept is central to the very definition of the real numbers via completion of the rationals.

## Definition

<!-- section: definition -->

A sequence $(a_n)$ is Cauchy if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $m, n > N$ we have $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$.

Note the quantifiers: m and n are both large, but they can be chosen independently of each other after N.

**ELI5**

- After a certain point in the movie, any two frames you pick are almost identical (within your tolerance ε); the picture has stopped changing appreciably.
- You do not need to know what the final settled picture looks like; you only observe that later frames look like each other.

**Worked Example:**

The sequence $a_n = 1/n$ is Cauchy. Given $\varepsilon > 0$ pick $N > 2/\varepsilon$. Then for $m, n > N$ we have $|1/m - 1/n| \leq 1/m + 1/n < 2/N < \varepsilon$.

## Cauchy Sequences in ℝ Converge (Cauchy Completeness)

<!-- section: cauchy-criterion-convergence -->

Theorem (Cauchy criterion for convergence in ℝ): A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is Cauchy.

One direction is easy (convergent ⇒ Cauchy) by triangle inequality: $|a_m - a_n| \leq |a_m - L| + |a_n - L|$.

The converse uses the least-upper-bound property: a Cauchy sequence is bounded, hence has a convergent subsequence by Bolzano-Weierstrass; the whole sequence then converges to the same limit.

**ELI5**

- In the reals, "terms get close to each other" automatically means "they get close to some definite number that is already in the system."
- This fails in the rationals: you can have a Cauchy sequence of rationals whose limit would be √2, which is missing from ℚ.

**Worked Example:**

The sequence defined by $a_1=1$, $a_{n+1} = (a_n + 2/a_n)/2$ is Cauchy (one shows $|a_{n+1} - a_n|$ decreases quadratically) and therefore converges in $\mathbb{R}$; its limit must satisfy $L = (L + 2/L)/2$, hence $L^2=2$, $L=\sqrt{2}$.

## Relation to Completeness; Non-Cauchy Examples

<!-- section: relation-completeness -->

The statement "every Cauchy sequence converges" is one of the equivalent ways to express the completeness of ℝ.

A sequence that is not Cauchy cannot converge (contrapositive of the easy direction). Classic non-Cauchy: $a_n = n$ (terms get farther apart), $a_n = (-1)^n$ (terms jump by 2 forever), harmonic series partial sums (grow like $\log n$, differences $1/n$ do not go to 0 uniformly).

**ELI5**

- If the terms keep jumping by a fixed amount no matter how far you go, they are not getting close to each other and therefore cannot be approaching any single target.
- Completeness of ℝ is exactly the guarantee that "getting close to each other" is enough to guarantee a limit exists inside the space.

**Worked Example:**

To show the partial sums of the harmonic series $s_n = \sum_{k=1}^n 1/k$ are not Cauchy: for any $N$, choose $m = N$, $n = 2N$. Then $s_n - s_m \geq \sum_{k=N+1}^{2N} 1/k > N \cdot (1/(2N)) = 1/2$. Thus $|s_n - s_m| > 1/2$ for arbitrarily large indices, so not Cauchy.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing 'for all $m,n > N$, $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$' but allowing $N$ to depend on $m$ or $n$**: The $N$ must be chosen first from $\varepsilon$ alone.
- **Confusing Cauchy with 'the terms get small'**: $a_n = n + 1/n$ is Cauchy? No, terms get large; differences $|a_{n+1}-a_n| \to 1$ not to $0$. $a_n = 5 + 1/n$ is Cauchy.
- **Thinking every bounded sequence is Cauchy**: Counter-example the oscillating (−1)^n.
- **Forgetting that in incomplete spaces Cauchy sequences may diverge**: The definition of completion of a metric space is precisely to add limits for all Cauchy sequences.
- **Misapplying the criterion to series**: For series we look at Cauchy on the partial sums; the terms $a_n$ themselves must go to 0 but that is not sufficient.

A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence: $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$. Because there are infinitely many terms, the sum is defined as the limit (if it exists) of the sequence of partial sums $s_N = a_1 + \cdots + a_N$.

The study of series is the study of when these infinite sums converge and what their values are.

## Partial Sums and Convergence of Series

<!-- section: partial-sums-convergence -->

The series $\sum a_n$ converges to $S$ if the sequence of partial sums $s_N \to S$ as $N \to \infty$.

If the partial sums diverge, the series diverges.

The terms $a_n$ themselves must tend to zero for the series to converge (necessary condition), but this is far from sufficient (harmonic series).

**ELI5**

- A series is like adding an infinite shopping list: you look at the running total after 1 item, after 2 items, after 10, … and ask whether those running totals settle down to a definite final bill.
- If the running totals keep growing without bound or keep oscillating, there is no final sum.

**Worked Example:**

Evaluate the geometric series $\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n$ for $|r| < 1$.

- **Partial sum.** Multiply $s_N = 1 + r + \cdots + r^N$ by $r$ to get $r s_N = r + r^2 + \cdots + r^{N+1}$. Subtracting, $s_N - r s_N = 1 - r^{N+1}$, so (for $r \neq 1$) $s_N = \dfrac{1 - r^{N+1}}{1 - r}$.
- **Take the limit.** When $|r| < 1$, $r^{N+1} \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, leaving $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty r^n = \frac{1}{1 - r}$.

Concrete case: $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots$ has $r = 1/2$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{1 - 1/2} = 2$.

Sanity check: $s_0 = 1$, $s_1 = 1.5$, $s_2 = 1.75$, $s_3 = 1.875$ — the partial sums close in on $2$. $\checkmark$ (If instead $|r| \geq 1$ the term $r^n$ does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges.)

## Necessary Condition $a_n \to 0$ and the Harmonic Series

<!-- section: necessary-condition-harmonic -->

If $\sum a_n$ converges then $a_n \to 0$. Proof: $s_n - s_{n-1} = a_n$ and both $s_n$ and $s_{n-1} \to S$, so $a_n \to 0$.

The harmonic series $\sum 1/n$ diverges (even though $1/n \to 0$) because its partial sums grow like $\ln N + \gamma$, where $\gamma \approx 0.577$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. The clean elementary proof groups terms in blocks of doubling length: $s_{2^k} > 1 + k/2 \to \infty$.

**ELI5**

- If the added items do not get smaller and smaller, the running total cannot settle.
- Even if items get smaller, if they shrink too slowly (like 1/n) the total still creeps to infinity, just very slowly.

**Worked Example:**

Show the harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty 1/n$ diverges by the doubling-block argument.

- Group the terms after the first: $\dfrac13 + \dfrac14 > \dfrac14 + \dfrac14 = \dfrac12$; then $\dfrac15 + \dfrac16 + \dfrac17 + \dfrac18 > 4 \cdot \dfrac18 = \dfrac12$; in general the block from $\dfrac{1}{2^{k-1}+1}$ to $\dfrac{1}{2^k}$ has $2^{k-1}$ terms each $\geq \dfrac{1}{2^k}$, so it sums to more than $\dfrac12$.
- Therefore $s_{2^k} \geq 1 + \dfrac{k}{2}$, which grows without bound. Hence the partial sums diverge to $+\infty$. $\checkmark$

Contrast: the *alternating* harmonic series $\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n$ converges (to $\ln 2$) even though the absolute series is this same divergent harmonic series. The alternating partial sums oscillate, but the amplitude of oscillation shrinks to $0$, so they settle. This is the prototype of a *conditionally* convergent series.

## Tests for Convergence (Brief Survey)

<!-- section: convergence-tests -->

- **Comparison test:** if $0 \leq a_n \leq b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges (and if $\sum a_n$ diverges then $\sum b_n$ diverges).
- **Limit comparison:** if $a_n, b_n > 0$ and $\lim a_n / b_n = c$ with $0 < c < \infty$, then $\sum a_n$ and $\sum b_n$ share the same fate.
- **Ratio test:** if $\lim |a_{n+1}/a_n| = L$, then $L < 1 \Rightarrow$ converges (absolutely), $L > 1 \Rightarrow$ diverges, and $L = 1 \Rightarrow$ inconclusive.
- **Root test:** the same verdicts using $L = \limsup |a_n|^{1/n}$.
- **Integral test:** for a positive, eventually decreasing $a_n = f(n)$, the series $\sum a_n$ and the integral $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converge or diverge together.
- **Alternating series (Leibniz) test:** if $b_n \geq 0$ decreases monotonically to $0$, then $\sum (-1)^n b_n$ converges (possibly only conditionally).

These tests let us decide convergence without computing the sum explicitly.

**ELI5**

- Comparison test: if your bills are each smaller than someone whose total is finite, your total is finite too; if they are each bigger than someone whose total is infinite, yours blows up as well.
- Ratio test: if each term is eventually less than 3/4 the size of the previous, the tail is dominated by a geometric series with ratio <1 and must converge.
- Integral test: a decreasing series is like a staircase; it converges exactly when the smooth ramp underneath it (the integral) has finite area.
- Alternating series with decreasing terms to 0: the error is at most the size of the first omitted term (like rounding error).

**Worked Example:**

**Ratio test on $\sum n / 2^n$.** With $a_n = n/2^n$,
$$\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \frac{(n+1)/2^{n+1}}{n/2^n} = \frac{n+1}{2n} = \frac12 \cdot \frac{n+1}{n} \longrightarrow \frac12 < 1,$$
so the series converges absolutely. Sanity check: the terms $1/2, 2/4, 3/8, 4/16, \ldots = 0.5, 0.5, 0.375, 0.25, \ldots$ eventually shrink fast, consistent with convergence. $\checkmark$

**Integral / p-series on $\sum 1/n^2$.** This is a $p$-series with $p = 2 > 1$, so it converges (the integral test compares it with $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1 < \infty$). The exact sum happens to be $\pi^2/6$ (the Basel problem), but a convergence test only needs to decide yes/no — it never has to find the value.

**Ratio test being inconclusive.** For the harmonic series $\sum 1/n$, $\left|a_{n+1}/a_n\right| = n/(n+1) \to 1$, so the ratio test gives $L = 1$ and says nothing — yet we know the series diverges. This is exactly why $L = 1$ is the "try another test" case.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking $a_n \to 0$ is also sufficient**: The harmonic series is the classic counter-example.
- **Confusing the series with the sequence of terms**: The series is the sequence of partial sums; the terms $a_n$ are only the differences.
- **Forgetting that conditional convergence is possible**: Rearrangements of conditionally convergent series can be made to converge to any number or diverge (Riemann rearrangement theorem).
- **Using the ratio test when the limit is 1**: The test is inconclusive; need another test (e.g. harmonic vs p-series).
- **Believing every convergent series has a 'nice' closed form**: Most convergent series do not; we study them for convergence alone.

A series $\sum a_n$ is said to converge absolutely if the series of absolute values $\sum |a_n|$ converges.

Absolute convergence is a stronger property than ordinary (conditional) convergence. If a series converges absolutely then it converges, and moreover any rearrangement of its terms converges to the same sum.

This is one of the most useful distinctions in the theory of infinite series.

## Definition and the Absolute Convergence Theorem

<!-- section: definition-absolute -->

$\sum a_n$ converges absolutely when $\sum |a_n| < \infty$ (the series of nonnegative terms converges).

Theorem: Absolute convergence ⇒ convergence.

Proof sketch: $0 \leq |a_n| - a_n \leq 2|a_n|$. If $\sum |a_n|$ converges then $\sum (|a_n| - a_n)$ converges (comparison), hence $\sum a_n = \sum (|a_n| - (|a_n| - a_n))$ converges as difference of two convergent series.

**ELI5**

- If the sum of the sizes of the terms is finite, then the signed sum cannot wander off to infinity; the positive and negative pieces cannot cancel in a way that breaks convergence.
- Absolute convergence is "robust convergence" — the sum does not rely on delicate cancellation.

**Worked Example:**

Classify the alternating harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$.

- **Does it converge?** The absolute terms $b_n = 1/n$ decrease monotonically to $0$, so by the alternating series test the signed series converges (to $\ln 2$).
- **Does it converge absolutely?** $\sum \left|\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\right| = \sum \dfrac{1}{n}$ is the harmonic series, which diverges.

Converges but not absolutely $\Rightarrow$ the convergence is **conditional**. $\checkmark$

## Rearrangements and Conditional Convergence

<!-- section: rearrangements-conditional -->

If $\sum a_n$ converges absolutely, then every rearrangement (permutation of the terms) converges to the same sum.

If a series converges only conditionally (converges but not absolutely), then by Riemann's rearrangement theorem one can rearrange the terms to converge to any prescribed real number, or to diverge to ±∞.

**ELI5**

- With absolute convergence the terms are "small enough in total size" that the order you add them does not matter; you always end up at the same place.
- With only conditional convergence the positive and negative pieces are both large (their absolutes diverge); by adding positives for a long time or negatives you can steer the partial sums wherever you want.

**Worked Example:**

Rearrange the alternating harmonic series to approach a target $M = 1.5$.

The positive terms are $1, \tfrac13, \tfrac15, \tfrac17, \ldots$ (their sum diverges), and the negative terms are $-\tfrac12, -\tfrac14, -\tfrac16, \ldots$ (their sum diverges too). The recipe:

- **Add positives until you first exceed $1.5$:** $1 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 = 1.533\ldots > 1.5$. Stop.
- **Add negatives until you first drop below $1.5$:** $1.533\ldots - \tfrac12 = 1.033\ldots < 1.5$. Stop.
- **Add more positives until you exceed $1.5$ again:** $1.033\ldots + \tfrac17 + \tfrac19 + \cdots$ until the running total passes $1.5$, then switch back to negatives, and so on.

Because both the leftover positive terms and the leftover negative terms still sum to $\pm\infty$, you can always overshoot and undershoot; and because the individual terms $\to 0$, each overshoot/undershoot is tinier than the last. The partial sums are therefore squeezed onto $M = 1.5$. The same recipe works for *any* real target — the essence of Riemann's rearrangement theorem.

## Tests That Give Absolute Convergence

<!-- section: tests-absolute -->

Most of the standard tests (ratio, root, comparison, integral, Weierstrass M-test for series of functions) actually prove absolute convergence when they succeed.

The alternating series test is the classic example that can give only conditional convergence.

**ELI5**

- When the ratio test or root test returns a limit <1, it is really saying the absolute series behaves like a convergent geometric series.
- You get absolute convergence "for free" from those tests.

**Worked Example:**

The series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \dfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2}$ converges absolutely: the absolute series is $\sum \left|\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\right| = \sum \dfrac{1}{n^2}$, a $p$-series with $p = 2 > 1$, which converges. Since the absolute series converges, so does the original (by the Absolute Convergence Theorem above), and it does so *absolutely*. $\checkmark$

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking absolute convergence is the same as convergence**: Many series converge conditionally.
- **Applying rearrangement carelessly**: You may change the sum (or destroy convergence) if you rearrange a conditionally convergent series.
- **Forgetting that absolute convergence allows term-by-term operations**: You can integrate or differentiate power series inside the radius of convergence precisely because of absolute convergence on compact subintervals.
- **Confusing absolute convergence of series with absolute value of the sum**: $|\sum a_n| \leq \sum |a_n|$ always holds when the right side converges (triangle inequality for infinite sums).
- **Using the alternating test when the terms do not decrease monotonically**: The Leibniz test requires $|a_n|$ decreasing to 0.

A power series is an expression of the form $\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n (x - a)^n$, where the coefficients $c_n$ are fixed numbers and $a$ is the center.

Power series are the infinite-degree analogues of polynomials. Inside their interval (or disk) of convergence they behave like very nice functions: they are infinitely differentiable, can be integrated and differentiated term by term, and equal their own Taylor series.

## Radius of Convergence

<!-- section: radius-convergence -->

For a power series there exists $R \in [0, \infty]$ (the radius of convergence) such that the series converges **absolutely** for $|x - a| < R$ and diverges for $|x - a| > R$. The two extreme cases are allowed: $R = 0$ means the series converges only at the center $x = a$, and $R = \infty$ means it converges for every real $x$.

At the endpoints x = a ± R (when R finite) convergence must be checked separately.

The radius can be found by the ratio test: $1/R = \lim |c_{n+1}/c_n|$ (when the limit exists), or by the root test $1/R = \limsup |c_n|^{1/n}$.

**ELI5**

- Around the center a the series converges inside a symmetric interval of "radius" R; outside that interval the terms eventually grow and the partial sums blow up.
- At exactly distance R the series may converge at one end, both, or neither (three separate cases to test).

**Worked Example:**

**Compute $R$ for $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{x^n}{n}$** (center $a = 0$, coefficients $c_n = 1/n$).

- Ratio test on the coefficients: $\dfrac{1}{R} = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|\dfrac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right| = \lim \dfrac{1/(n+1)}{1/n} = \lim \dfrac{n}{n+1} = 1$, so $R = 1$.
- So the series converges for $|x| < 1$ and diverges for $|x| > 1$.
- **Endpoints** (must be checked by hand, one at a time): at $x = 1$ we get $\sum 1/n$, the harmonic series, which **diverges**; at $x = -1$ we get $\sum (-1)^n/n$, the alternating harmonic series, which **converges** (conditionally). So the exact set of convergence is $[-1, 1)$ — the two ends behave differently, which the radius alone cannot tell you.

(The plain geometric series $\sum x^n$ has $R = 1$ as well, but there it *diverges at both* endpoints since $x^n \not\to 0$ when $|x| = 1$ — a reminder that endpoints are always a separate case.)

## Term-by-Term Differentiation and Integration

<!-- section: termwise-diff-int -->

Inside the open interval of convergence a power series may be differentiated and integrated term by term, and the resulting series have the same radius of convergence.

Consequently a power series defines a $C^\infty$ function on $(a-R, a+R)$ whose derivatives of all orders are given by the differentiated series.

**ELI5**

- You can treat the infinite polynomial almost exactly like a finite polynomial for calculus operations, provided you stay strictly inside the radius.
- The new series after differentiation still converges in the same open interval (though possibly not at the endpoints).

**Worked Example:**

Start with the geometric series, valid for $|x| < 1$:
$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}.$$

- **Differentiate term by term.** The left side gives $\sum_{n=1}^\infty n x^{n-1}$ (the $n=0$ term is constant, so it drops). The right side gives $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{1-x} = \dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}$. Hence $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty n x^{n-1} = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}$, still on $|x| < 1$.
- **Integrate term by term** (from $0$ to $x$). The left side gives $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}$. The right side gives $\int_0^x \dfrac{dt}{1-t} = -\ln(1-x)$. Hence $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} = -\ln(1-x)$ on $|x| < 1$.

Sanity check at $x = 1/2$: the differentiated identity predicts $\dfrac{1}{(1-1/2)^2} = 4$. Summing $\sum n (1/2)^{n-1} = 1 + 2(0.5) + 3(0.25) + 4(0.125) + \cdots = 1 + 1 + 0.75 + 0.5 + \cdots$, whose partial sums climb toward $4$. $\checkmark$ Both new series keep the radius $R = 1$.

## Taylor Series and Analytic Functions

<!-- section: taylor-analytic -->

Every power series is the Taylor series of the function it defines inside the radius: $c_n = f^{(n)}(a) / n!$.

A function is analytic at a point if it equals its Taylor series in some neighborhood of the point. Not every $C^\infty$ function is analytic: the classic counter-example is $f(x) = e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x \neq 0$ with $f(0) = 0$. One can show every derivative satisfies $f^{(n)}(0) = 0$, so its Taylor series at $0$ is the zero series — which converges to $0$, not to $f$ (which is positive for $x \neq 0$). So $f$ is $C^\infty$ but not analytic at $0$.

**ELI5**

- Inside the radius the power series "knows" all the derivatives of the function at the center; you can read the coefficients directly from the derivatives.
- Some very smooth functions are "flat" at a point (all derivatives zero) yet not zero; their Taylor series is the zero series and does not represent the function.

**Worked Example:**

The exponential series $\sum x^n / n!$ has $R=\infty$ and equals $e^x$ everywhere. All derivatives of $e^x$ are $e^x$, so the Taylor coefficients at 0 are exactly $1/n!$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to check the endpoints separately**: Radius tells you only the open interval; endpoints are case-by-case.
- **Thinking the radius formula always gives a number**: When $\lim |c_{n+1}/c_n|$ does not exist one must use $\limsup |c_n|^{1/n}$.
- **Differentiating or integrating at the endpoints without justification**: Term-by-term operations are guaranteed only inside the open disk/interval.
- **Assuming every smooth function equals its Taylor series**: Counter-examples exist that are C^∞ but not analytic.
- **Confusing power series in real vs complex variables**: In complex analysis the disk of convergence is fundamental and absolute convergence holds inside; on the real line one must still worry about conditional convergence at endpoints.
