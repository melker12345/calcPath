# Differentiation

The derivative, mean value theorem, Taylor approximations, and L'Hôpital's rule.

The derivative of f at a is the instantaneous rate of change of f at a, defined as the limit of the difference quotient [f(a+h) − f(a)] / h as h → 0 (when the limit exists).

Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line. The derivative is the central concept that turns calculus into a powerful tool for optimization, approximation, and modeling change.

## The Difference Quotient and Definition

<!-- section: definition-difference-quotient -->

f'(a) = lim_{h→0} [f(a + h) − f(a)] / h , provided the limit exists (as a real number).

Equivalently, f is differentiable at a if there exists a number L such that f(a + h) = f(a) + L h + o(h) as h → 0 (little-o notation: the error is negligible compared with h).

The tangent line is y = f(a) + f'(a)(x − a).

:::intuition
The difference quotient is the average slope over a small interval of width h. As you let the interval shrink to a point (h→0), if the average slopes settle to a definite number, that number is the instantaneous slope — the derivative.

The little-o form says the function is almost linear near a, with error much smaller than the distance |h|.
:::

:::example
For $f(x) = x^2$ at $a = 3$, form the difference quotient and simplify one step at a time:
:::

:::solution
$$\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h.$$

We may cancel $h$ because in the limit $h \to 0$ we only consider $h \neq 0$. As $h \to 0$ the expression $6 + h \to 6$, so $f'(3) = 6$. The tangent line is $y = 9 + 6(x - 3) = 6x - 9$.

Sanity check: the little-o form should hold with $L = 6$. Indeed $f(3 + h) = 9 + 6h + h^2 = f(3) + 6h + h^2$, and the error $h^2$ satisfies $h^2 / h = h \to 0$, so it is genuinely $o(h)$. The two definitions agree.
:::

## Differentiability Implies Continuity; Higher Derivatives

<!-- section: differentiability-implies-continuity -->

If f is differentiable at a then f is continuous at a (because f(a+h) − f(a) = h · (difference quotient) → 0 · L = 0).

The converse is false: |x| is continuous everywhere but not differentiable at 0.

If f' exists and is itself differentiable, we obtain f'', and so on. A C^∞ function has derivatives of all orders.

:::intuition
If the slope exists, the function cannot jump; the linear approximation forces continuity.

|x| has a sharp corner at 0; the left and right slopes differ (−1 and +1), so no single tangent line.
:::

:::example[(continuity is strictly weaker than differentiability):]
Take $f(x) = |x|$ at $a = 0$. The difference quotient is
:::

:::solution
$$\frac{f(0 + h) - f(0)}{h} = \frac{|h| - 0}{h} = \frac{|h|}{h} = \operatorname{sign}(h).$$

As $h \to 0^{+}$ this is $+1$; as $h \to 0^{-}$ it is $-1$. The one-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist and $f$ is **not** differentiable at $0$.

Continuity, however, does hold: $\lim_{h \to 0} f(0 + h) = \lim_{h \to 0} |h| = 0 = f(0)$. So $f$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there — a concrete witness that the implication "differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous" cannot be reversed.
:::

:::example[(a discontinuous second derivative):]
Let $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = 0$. From the definition, $f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h^2 \sin(1/h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} h \sin(1/h) = 0$ (squeeze, since $|\sin(1/h)| \le 1$). For $x \neq 0$, the product and chain rules give $f'(x) = 2x \sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$. As $x \to 0$ the $\cos(1/x)$ term oscillates and has no limit, so $f'$ exists everywhere but is **discontinuous** at $0$. This is the promised example behind "$f'$ can exist without being continuous."
:::

## Basic Differentiation Rules and the Chain Rule

<!-- section: rules-chain-rule -->

Going back to the limit definition for every derivative would be exhausting; the point of the differentiation rules is to build derivatives of complicated functions out of the derivatives of their pieces. Assuming $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at the point in question, one derives the usual rules directly from the definition:

- Sum: $(f + g)' = f' + g'$
- Constant multiple: $(cf)' = c\,f'$
- Product: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$
- Quotient: $\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$, valid wherever $g \neq 0$.

The chain rule handles compositions: if $g$ is differentiable at $a$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(a)$, then $f \circ g$ is differentiable at $a$ and

$$(f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a)) \cdot g'(a).$$

It is proved by writing the increments and using the little-o characterization (or careful limit manipulation), taking care at points where $g'(a) = 0$.

:::intuition
The rules let you differentiate "by algebra" without going back to the limit definition each time.

Chain rule: rates of change multiply when one quantity depends on another which depends on the input (like "speed of the shadow" = "speed of the ball" times "how shadow position depends on ball position").
:::

:::example[(chain rule, step by step):]
Differentiate $\sin(x^2)$. Write it as a composition $f \circ g$ with outer function $f(u) = \sin u$ and inner function $g(x) = x^2$, so that $f(g(x)) = \sin(x^2)$.
:::

:::solution
differentiate the outer function, keeping the inner one intact: $f'(u) = \cos u$, so $f'(g(x)) = \cos(x^2)$.
Step 2: differentiate the inner function: $g'(x) = 2x$.
Step 3: multiply, per $(f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2) = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x = 2x \cos(x^2).$$

Sanity check on the trap: it is $\cos(x^2) \cdot 2x$, **not** $\cos(2x)$ and **not** $2x\cos x$. You evaluate the outer derivative *at* $g(x) = x^2$, then multiply by $g'$.
:::

:::example[(quotient rule):]
Differentiate $h(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x + 1}$ on its domain $x \neq -1$. With $f = x^2$ (so $f' = 2x$) and $g = x + 1$ (so $g' = 1$), the quotient rule $\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$ gives
:::

:::solution
$$h'(x) = \frac{2x(x+1) - x^2 \cdot 1}{(x+1)^2} = \frac{2x^2 + 2x - x^2}{(x+1)^2} = \frac{x^2 + 2x}{(x+1)^2}.$$

Sanity check at $x = 1$: $h'(1) = \frac{1 + 2}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$. Numerically, $\frac{h(1.001) - h(0.999)}{0.002} \approx \frac{0.500750 - 0.499250}{0.002} = 0.75$, matching $\tfrac34$.
:::

## Mean Value Theorem Preview and Taylor

<!-- section: mvt-taylor-preview -->

The mean value theorem (next lesson) is the fundamental theorem relating derivatives to function values: if f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b) then there is c with f'(c) = [f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a).

As a corollary, if f' = 0 on an interval then f is constant. This is the basis for all the "increase/decrease" tests and for proving inequalities via derivatives.

:::intuition
Between any two points on the graph of a differentiable function there is a point where the tangent slope exactly equals the secant slope connecting the two points.

If the derivative is zero everywhere, the function is flat — constant.
:::

:::example[(an inequality from the sign of the derivative):]
To prove $\sin x \le x$ for all $x \ge 0$, define $g(x) = x - \sin x$ and show $g \ge 0$.
:::

:::solution
$g(0) = 0 - \sin 0 = 0$.
$g'(x) = 1 - \cos x$, and since $\cos x \le 1$ for every real $x$, we have $g'(x) \ge 0$ on $[0, \infty)$.
Because $g' \ge 0$, the corollary above makes $g$ non-decreasing on $[0, \infty)$. Hence for $x \ge 0$, $g(x) \ge g(0) = 0$, i.e. $x - \sin x \ge 0$, which is $\sin x \le x$.

Sanity check: at $x = \pi/2 \approx 1.571$, $\sin x = 1 \le 1.571$. The inequality is not tight except at $x = 0$, exactly where $g' = 1 - \cos 0 = 0$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing the derivative existing with the difference quotient being defined**: At a point the difference quotient must have a limit; for |x| it is defined for h≠0 but the limit fails.
- **Thinking differentiability on [a,b] requires one-sided derivatives at endpoints**: Usually we say differentiable on (a,b) and continuous up to the ends for MVT.
- **Forgetting that derivatives need not be continuous**: There exist functions differentiable everywhere with discontinuous derivative (Volterra's function or simpler examples).
- **Misapplying the chain rule**: (f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x)) g'(x), not f'(x) g'(x) or f(g'(x)).
- **Believing that if f' exists then f is C^1**: No; f' can exist and be discontinuous. C^1 means f' exists and is continuous.

The mean value theorem (MVT) is the fundamental link between the derivative (local rate of change) and the net change of the function over an interval.

It says that the average rate of change over [a,b] equals the instantaneous rate at some interior point. All the standard tests for monotonicity, convexity, and error bounds in approximations flow from MVT or its corollaries (Rolle's theorem, Taylor with remainder).

## Statement and Geometric Meaning

<!-- section: statement-geometric -->

Let f be continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b). Then there exists c ∈ (a,b) such that

f'(c) = [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a).

Geometrically: the slope of the secant line from (a,f(a)) to (b,f(b)) equals the slope of the tangent line at some point c in between.

:::intuition
The secant line is the "straight-line average speed" from a to b. MVT says that at some instant your instantaneous speed exactly matched that average speed.

You cannot go from height f(a) to f(b) in time b−a with a continuously changing speed without at some moment having speed exactly equal to the overall average.
:::

:::example[(finding the MVT point):]
Take $f(x) = x^2$ on $[1, 3]$. First check the hypotheses: $f$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $[1,3]$ and differentiable on $(1,3)$, so MVT applies.
:::

:::solution
Secant slope: $\dfrac{f(3) - f(1)}{3 - 1} = \dfrac{9 - 1}{2} = 4.$
Tangent slope: $f'(x) = 2x$. Set $2c = 4 \Rightarrow c = 2$.
Location check: $c = 2 \in (1, 3)$. ✓

So the tangent at $x = 2$ has slope $4$, exactly the secant slope from $(1,1)$ to $(3,9)$.

Sanity check: for $f(x) = x^2$ on $[a,b]$ the MVT point is always the midpoint $c = \frac{a+b}{2}$, since $\frac{b^2 - a^2}{b - a} = a + b = 2c$. Here $\frac{1+3}{2} = 2$, matching.
:::

## Rolle's Theorem (Special Case)

<!-- section: rolles-theorem -->

Rolle's theorem: if f(a)=f(b) and f continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), then there is c with f'(c)=0.

Proof: apply MVT; the right-hand side is zero, so f'(c)=0.

Rolle's is often proved first (using EVT to locate a max or min in the interior, then Fermat's stationary point theorem if the extremum is interior), then MVT is deduced from Rolle by considering an auxiliary function.

:::intuition
If a function starts and ends at the same height, then either it is flat somewhere (derivative zero) or it went up and came back down, in which case the tangent must be horizontal at the top or bottom.

This is the "there is a flat point" theorem.
:::

:::example[(Rolle directly: parallel tangent):]
Let $f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 3$ on $[1, 3]$. Then $f(1) = 1 - 4 + 3 = 0$ and $f(3) = 9 - 12 + 3 = 0$, so $f(1) = f(3)$; $f$ is a polynomial (continuous on $[1,3]$, differentiable on $(1,3)$). Rolle guarantees a $c \in (1,3)$ with $f'(c) = 0$. Solve: $f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow c = 2 \in (1,3)$. ✓ Indeed $x = 2$ is the vertex of the parabola, where the tangent is horizontal.
:::

:::example[(Rolle to bound the number of roots):]
To show $x^3 + 3x + 1 = 0$ has exactly one real root, let $f(x) = x^3 + 3x + 1$.
:::

:::solution
*At most one root (Rolle).* Suppose $f$ had two distinct roots $r_1 < r_2$. Since $f(r_1) = f(r_2) = 0$ and $f$ is differentiable, Rolle would give some $c \in (r_1, r_2)$ with $f'(c) = 0$. But $f'(x) = 3x^2 + 3 \ge 3 > 0$ for all $x$, so $f'$ is never zero — contradiction. Hence at most one root.
*At least one root (IVT).* $f(0) = 1 > 0$ and $f(-1) = (-1) + (-3) + 1 = -3 < 0$, so by the intermediate value theorem $f$ has a root in $(-1, 0)$.

Combining, there is exactly one real root. (This is the canonical way Rolle is used: a zero of $f'$ is forced between any two zeros of $f$.)
:::

## Consequences: Monotonicity, Inequalities, Taylor Remainder

<!-- section: consequences-monotonicity-taylor -->

- If f' > 0 on (a,b) then f is strictly increasing on [a,b] (apply MVT to any subinterval).
- If f'(x) ≥ g'(x) and f(a) ≥ g(a) then f(x) ≥ g(x) for x ≥ a (consider h = f−g).
- Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder: f(b) = f(a) + f'(a)(b−a) + (f''(c)/2)(b−a)^2 for some c, and higher orders similarly. The remainder term is controlled by bounds on the higher derivative.

:::intuition
Positive derivative means the function is always climbing, so overall it increases — like a hiker who is going uphill at every instant must end higher than they started.

The Taylor expansion with remainder is like a weather forecast with an error bar: the polynomial is the prediction, and the remainder term (built from the *next* derivative at some in-between point $c$) is a guaranteed bound on how wrong the prediction can be.
:::

:::example[(inequality via monotonicity):]
Prove $\sqrt{1 + x} \le 1 + \tfrac{x}{2}$ for $x \ge 0$. Let $f(x) = 1 + \tfrac{x}{2} - \sqrt{1 + x}$, defined and differentiable on $[0, \infty)$.
:::

:::solution
$f(0) = 1 + 0 - 1 = 0$.
$f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1 + x}}$. For $x \ge 0$ we have $\sqrt{1 + x} \ge 1$, so $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}} \le \tfrac12$, giving $f'(x) \ge 0$.
Since $f' \ge 0$, $f$ is non-decreasing, so $f(x) \ge f(0) = 0$ for $x \ge 0$, i.e. $\sqrt{1 + x} \le 1 + \tfrac{x}{2}$.

Sanity check at $x = 3$: $\sqrt{4} = 2 \le 1 + \tfrac32 = 2.5$. ✓
:::

:::example[(Taylor remainder bound):]
Bound the error of the linear approximation $\sqrt{1 + x} \approx 1 + \tfrac{x}{2}$ on $[0, 3]$ using the Lagrange remainder $R_1(x) = \tfrac{f''(c)}{2}x^2$. Here $f(x) = \sqrt{1+x}$, $f''(x) = -\tfrac14 (1+x)^{-3/2}$, so $|f''(c)| = \tfrac14 (1 + c)^{-3/2} \le \tfrac14$ (largest at $c = 0$). Thus for $x \in [0,3]$,
:::

:::solution
$$|R_1(x)| = \left|\frac{f''(c)}{2}\right| x^2 \le \frac{1/4}{2}\, x^2 = \frac{x^2}{8}.$$

At $x = 3$ this bound is $\tfrac{9}{8} = 1.125$; the actual error is $2.5 - 2 = 0.5$, comfortably within the bound. (The bound is loose because $|f''|$ shrinks quickly away from $0$; near $x = 0$ it is very sharp.)
:::

## Why Differentiability on the Open Interval Is Needed

<!-- section: necessity-differentiability -->

If we weaken the hypothesis to "continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable at *most* points of $(a,b)$," the MVT conclusion can fail. The point is that the guaranteed $c$ must be a point where $f$ actually is differentiable; if the derivative is allowed to be missing (or misbehave) on even a small set, no such $c$ need equal the average slope.

A dramatic example: a function can be continuous everywhere, differentiable almost everywhere, non-decreasing (indeed with total rise $> 0$), and yet have $f' = 0$ at almost every point where the derivative exists — the devil's staircase / Cantor function. Here the "average slope" over $[0,1]$ is positive, but the derivative is $0$ almost everywhere, so there is no interior $c$ with $f'(c)$ equal to that positive average. MVT in its classical form fails precisely because differentiability does not hold throughout $(a,b)$ in a usable way.

:::intuition
You can have a function that overall rises (like a staircase with flat parts and steep rises) but is "flat" almost everywhere; the rises are concentrated on a set of measure zero. Then the derivative (where it exists) is zero, yet net change is positive.

This shows why we need differentiability everywhere on (a,b) for the classical MVT.
:::

:::example
The Cantor (devil's staircase) function is constant on the intervals removed in the construction of the Cantor set, increases only on the Cantor set itself (measure zero), is continuous, and has derivative 0 almost everywhere, yet goes from 0 to 1. On [0,1] the average slope is 1, but there is no point where the derivative equals 1 (in fact derivative is 0 or does not exist).
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Stating MVT with differentiability only at endpoints or on a dense set**: The theorem requires differentiability throughout the open interval.
- **Confusing MVT with the mean value property for harmonic functions**: Different context (PDE).
- **Thinking that f' > 0 everywhere implies f is onto an interval**: It implies strictly increasing, hence one-to-one, but the image may still miss values if the domain is not connected or if f is not continuous up to the boundary.
- **Applying MVT to |x| on [−1,1]**: Not differentiable at 0, and indeed the secant slope is 0 but there is no c with derivative 0 in a way that matches? |x| derivative is ±1, secant from −1 to 1 is 0, and indeed no c where f'(c)=0.
- **Forgetting the conclusion is existence of some c, not for all c**: MVT does not say the derivative equals the average everywhere.

Taylor's theorem gives a polynomial approximation to a sufficiently smooth function together with an explicit remainder term that quantifies the error.

It is the theoretical basis for polynomial approximations, Taylor series, numerical methods (Newton, Euler), and asymptotic analysis.

## Statement (Lagrange Form of Remainder)

<!-- section: statement-lagrange -->

Let f be (n+1) times differentiable on an open interval containing a and x. Then there exists c strictly between a and x such that

f(x) = ∑_{k=0}^n [f^{(k)}(a)/k!] (x − a)^k  +  [f^{(n+1)}(c)/(n+1)!] (x − a)^{n+1} .

The sum is the Taylor polynomial of degree n centered at a; the last term is the Lagrange remainder.

:::intuition
The Taylor polynomial is the unique polynomial of degree ≤ n that matches f and its first n derivatives at a. Taylor's theorem tells you how far this polynomial deviates from f at a nearby point x, in terms of the (n+1)th derivative at some intermediate c.

For n=0 it reduces to the mean value theorem: f(x) = f(a) + f'(c)(x−a).
:::

:::example[(degree-2 Taylor with explicit remainder):]
Take $f(x) = e^x$, center $a = 0$, order $n = 2$. Since $f^{(k)}(x) = e^x$ for all $k$, we have $f^{(k)}(0) = 1$, so the Taylor polynomial is $P_2(x) = 1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}{2}$ and
:::

:::solution
$$e^x = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{e^c}{3!}\,x^3 = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{e^c}{6}\,x^3$$

for some $c$ strictly between $0$ and $x$. (Note $3! = 6$ in the denominator, matching $n + 1 = 3$.)

Now use this to approximate $e^{0.2}$ and bound the error. $P_2(0.2) = 1 + 0.2 + \tfrac{0.04}{2} = 1.22$. The remainder is $\tfrac{e^c}{6}(0.2)^3$ with $0 < c < 0.2$, so $e^c < e^{0.2} < e^{1} < 3$, giving

$$|R_2(0.2)| < \frac{3}{6}(0.008) = 0.004.$$

Sanity check: the true value is $e^{0.2} \approx 1.221403$, so the actual error is about $0.001403$, indeed less than the bound $0.004$. ✓
:::

## Other Forms of the Remainder (Integral, Cauchy, Peano)

<!-- section: other-remainder-forms -->

The Lagrange form is the most commonly stated, but there is also:

- Integral remainder: $R_n(x) = \frac{1}{n!} \int_a^x (x-t)^n f^{(n+1)}(t)\,dt$.
- Cauchy form, Peano form (little-o as $x \to a$: $R_n(x) = o\!\left((x-a)^n\right)$ when $f^{(n+1)}$ exists at $a$).

The integral form is often the most useful for estimates and for proving that the Taylor series converges to the function when the remainder →0.

:::intuition
The integral form comes from integrating the fundamental theorem of calculus repeatedly (or from integration by parts on the Lagrange form).

Peano form says that near a the error is smaller than any constant times (x−a)^n ; it is the minimal information needed for many local arguments.
:::

:::example[(deriving the integral remainder):]
Start from the fundamental theorem of calculus, $f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\,dt$, and integrate by parts. The clever choice is $u = f'(t)$, $dv = dt$ with antiderivative $v = -(x - t)$ (any constant works; $x$ is fixed here, so $\frac{d}{dt}[-(x-t)] = 1$). Then
:::

:::solution
$$\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt = \Big[-(x-t)f'(t)\Big]_a^x + \int_a^x (x - t)f''(t)\,dt = f'(a)(x - a) + \int_a^x (x-t)f''(t)\,dt.$$

So $f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \int_a^x (x-t) f''(t)\,dt$ — the degree-1 Taylor polynomial plus the $n=1$ integral remainder $\frac{1}{1!}\int_a^x (x-t)^1 f''(t)\,dt$. Repeating the integration by parts $n$ times peels off one Taylor term at each stage and leaves $R_n(x) = \frac{1}{n!}\int_a^x (x-t)^n f^{(n+1)}(t)\,dt$.
:::

:::example[(integral remainder gives Lagrange):]
Bounding the $n=1$ integral remainder recovers a Lagrange-type estimate. If $|f''| \le M$ on $[a,x]$ (assume $x > a$), then
:::

:::solution
$$|R_1(x)| = \left|\int_a^x (x-t)f''(t)\,dt\right| \le M \int_a^x (x - t)\,dt = M \cdot \frac{(x-a)^2}{2}.$$

For $f(x) = \sqrt{1+x}$ on $[0, 0.5]$ we have $|f''(t)| = \tfrac14(1+t)^{-3/2} \le \tfrac14$, so $|R_1(0.5)| \le \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac{0.25}{2} = 0.03125$. Check: $P_1(0.5) = 1 + \tfrac{0.5}{2} = 1.25$, true value $\sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.224745$, actual error $\approx 0.025255 < 0.03125$. ✓
:::

## Taylor Series and When It Represents the Function

<!-- section: taylor-series-representation -->

The Taylor series of f at a is the infinite sum ∑ [f^{(k)}(a)/k!] (x−a)^k from k=0 to ∞.

The series may or may not converge to f(x). Even if it converges, it may converge to the wrong function (the classic e^{-1/x^2} flat function whose Taylor series at 0 is 0 but the function is positive for x>0).

A sufficient condition for the series to equal $f$ is that the remainder $R_n(x) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ for each fixed $x$ in some interval.

:::intuition
The Taylor series is the "best" polynomial approximation of every degree, all rolled into one formal power series.

For many functions (exp, sin, cos, 1/(1−x) inside radius) the remainder does go to zero and the series equals the function. For others it does not.
:::

:::example[(why $e^x$ equals its Taylor series everywhere):]
For $f(x) = e^x$ centered at $a = 0$, the Lagrange remainder after $n$ terms is $R_n(x) = \dfrac{e^c}{(n+1)!}\,x^{n+1}$ for some $c$ between $0$ and $x$. Fix an arbitrary $x$; we show $R_n(x) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
:::

:::solution
Bound $e^c$: since $c$ lies between $0$ and $x$, $e^c \le e^{|x|}$, a constant independent of $n$.
So $|R_n(x)| \le e^{|x|} \cdot \dfrac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$.
The factor $\dfrac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$: for any fixed $x$, the ratio of consecutive terms is $\dfrac{|x|}{n+2} \to 0$, so eventually each term is less than half the previous one and the sequence collapses to $0$.

Hence $|R_n(x)| \to 0$ for every $x$, so the Taylor series $\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{x^k}{k!}$ converges to $e^x$ for all real $x$. (Contrast this with the flat function $e^{-1/x^2}$, whose remainder does *not* vanish, so its Taylor series at $0$ misses the function entirely.)
:::

## Applications: Approximations, Error Bounds, Asymptotics

<!-- section: applications-error-bounds -->

- Linear approximation (n=1): f(x) ≈ f(a) + f'(a)(x−a), error ≈ (f''(c)/2)(x−a)^2 .
- Quadratic approximation for better accuracy.
- Newton's method can be viewed as using the linear Taylor approximation to solve f(x)=0.
- Asymptotic expansions: even if the series diverges, the partial sums may still give useful approximations with controlled error (e.g. Stirling's series for n!).

:::intuition
For small changes, the tangent line (linear Taylor) gives a good first guess; the quadratic term corrects for curvature.

Error bounds from the remainder let engineers and scientists know how many terms are "safe" to keep for a desired precision.
:::

:::example[(approximation with a rigorous error bound):]
Approximate $\sin(0.1)$ by its degree-3 Taylor polynomial at $0$, $P_3(x) = x - \tfrac{x^3}{6}$:
:::

:::solution
$$P_3(0.1) = 0.1 - \frac{(0.1)^3}{6} = 0.1 - \frac{0.001}{6} \approx 0.1 - 0.00016667 = 0.09983333.$$

Error bound via Lagrange remainder: $R_3(x) = \frac{f^{(4)}(c)}{4!}x^4$ where $f = \sin$, so $f^{(4)} = \sin$ and $|f^{(4)}(c)| \le 1$. Thus $|R_3(0.1)| \le \frac{(0.1)^4}{24} = \frac{0.0001}{24} \approx 4.17 \times 10^{-6}$. (In fact the next nonzero term is $\frac{x^5}{120}$, giving the tighter estimate $\frac{(0.1)^5}{120} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-8}$.) The true value is $\sin(0.1) \approx 0.09983342$, so the actual error is about $8.3 \times 10^{-8}$ — well within the bound. ✓
:::

:::example[(Newton's method from linear Taylor):]
Solve $x^2 - 2 = 0$ (i.e. find $\sqrt 2$) by Newton's iteration $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ with $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, $f'(x) = 2x$. This simplifies to $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{x_n^2 - 2}{2x_n} = \dfrac{x_n}{2} + \dfrac{1}{x_n}$. Starting from $x_0 = 1$:
:::

:::solution
$x_1 = \tfrac12 + 1 = 1.5$
$x_2 = \tfrac{1.5}{2} + \tfrac{1}{1.5} = 0.75 + 0.6667 = 1.416\overline{6}$
$x_3 = \tfrac{1.4166667}{2} + \tfrac{1}{1.4166667} \approx 0.7083333 + 0.7058824 \approx 1.4142157$

Sanity check: $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.4142136$, so $x_3$ is already correct to $5$ decimal places — the quadratic convergence Newton inherits from discarding the $O((x - x_n)^2)$ Taylor remainder.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming the Taylor series always equals the function**: The flat function counter-example shows this is false even for C^∞ functions.
- **Forgetting that the c in the remainder depends on x and n**: You cannot treat c as fixed when taking limits.
- **Using Taylor with remainder when f is not sufficiently differentiable**: The hypotheses must hold on an interval containing a and x.
- **Confusing the center a with the expansion point x**: The polynomial is built from derivatives at the fixed center a; x is the evaluation point.
- **Thinking a divergent Taylor series is useless**: Asymptotic series can still be used for approximation when truncated optimally, even if they diverge as n→∞.

L'Hôpital's rule is a powerful tool for evaluating indeterminate forms 0/0 or ∞/∞ by replacing the limit of f/g with the limit of f'/g' (when the latter exists).

It is one of the most frequently used limit techniques in calculus and analysis, with careful hypotheses required for validity.

## The 0/0 Form (Basic Statement)

<!-- section: zero-over-zero -->

Let f and g be differentiable on (a,b) except possibly at c ∈ (a,b), with g'(x) ≠ 0. Suppose lim_{x→c} f(x) = 0 and lim_{x→c} g(x) = 0, and suppose lim_{x→c} f'(x)/g'(x) = L (finite or ±∞). Then lim_{x→c} f(x)/g(x) = L.

The rule also holds for one-sided limits and for limits as x→±∞ (with appropriate adjustments to the hypotheses).

:::intuition
When both numerator and denominator are approaching 0, their ratio may be anything; looking at the ratio of their slopes (derivatives) often resolves the "competition" between them.

Geometrically, near c both curves are close to 0; their tangents' slopes tell you the relative rates at which they approach 0.
:::

:::example[(basic 0/0):]
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$.
:::

:::solution
check the form: as $x \to 0$, $\sin x \to 0$ and $x \to 0$, so this is genuinely $0/0$ (L'Hôpital is licensed).
Step 2 — differentiate top and bottom *separately* (this is **not** the quotient rule): $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$, $\frac{d}{dx}x = 1$.
Step 3 — take the new limit: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1$, which exists, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$.

(Caveat: this is standard as an illustration, but it is mildly circular if used to *prove* $(\sin x)' = \cos x$, since that derivative already relies on this very limit.)
:::

:::example[(0/0 needing two applications):]
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2}$.
:::

:::solution
Form: $1 - \cos x \to 0$ and $x^2 \to 0$, so $0/0$.
First application: $\dfrac{\sin x}{2x}$. Still $0/0$ as $x \to 0$, so apply again.
Second application: $\dfrac{\cos x}{2} \to \dfrac{1}{2}$.

Hence the limit is $\tfrac12$. Sanity check with the Taylor series: $1 - \cos x = \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{24} + \cdots$, so $\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac12 - \frac{x^2}{24} + \cdots \to \frac12$. ✓
:::

## The ∞/∞ Form and Other Indeterminate Forms

<!-- section: infinity-over-infinity -->

If lim f = ±∞ and lim g = ±∞ (same or mixed signs), and if lim f'/g' exists, then lim f/g = lim f'/g'.

Other indeterminate forms (0·∞, ∞−∞, 0^0, 1^∞, ∞^0) are usually reduced to 0/0 or ∞/∞ by algebraic rewriting (taking logs for exponential forms, writing products as quotients, etc.).

:::intuition
When both go to infinity, the ratio may still settle; the derivatives often grow at rates whose ratio is easier to see.

For 0·∞ rewrite as 0 / (1/∞) which becomes 0/0 or similar.
:::

:::example[(∞/∞, repeated):]
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^2 + 3x}{e^x}$.
:::

:::solution
Form: numerator $\to \infty$, denominator $\to \infty$, so $\infty/\infty$.
First application: $\dfrac{2x + 3}{e^x}$. Still $\infty/\infty$.
Second application: $\dfrac{2}{e^x} \to 0$.

Hence the limit is $0$ — the exponential eventually dominates any polynomial. (Each differentiation lowers the polynomial's degree by one while the denominator stays $e^x$; after enough steps the top is constant and the bottom blows up.)
:::

:::example[(rewriting a $0 \cdot \infty$ form):]
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln x$. This is the indeterminate form $0 \cdot (-\infty)$; L'Hôpital needs a quotient, so rewrite the product as one:
:::

:::solution
$$x \ln x = \frac{\ln x}{1/x}, \quad \text{which is } \frac{-\infty}{+\infty} \text{ as } x \to 0^+.$$

Apply L'Hôpital: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac1x$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\,x^{-1} = -x^{-2}$, so the ratio becomes

$$\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \frac{1}{x} \cdot (-x^2) = -x \to 0.$$

Hence $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0^+} x \ln x = 0$. Sanity check at $x = 0.01$: $0.01 \cdot \ln(0.01) = 0.01 \cdot (-4.605) \approx -0.046$, small and shrinking toward $0$. ✓ (Note we rewrote as $\frac{\ln x}{1/x}$ rather than $\frac{x}{1/\ln x}$; the latter is valid too but leads to messier derivatives — choosing the convenient arrangement matters.)
:::

## Proof Ideas (Cauchy Mean Value Theorem)

<!-- section: proof-cauchy-mvt -->

The standard proof uses the Cauchy mean value theorem (generalized MVT): for f,g continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), there is c with [f(b)−f(a)] g'(c) = [g(b)−g(a)] f'(c).

To prove L'Hôpital, one considers an auxiliary interval from a point x near c to c itself (or a sequence of points), applies Cauchy MVT, and takes limits, using that f and g →0 (or ∞) to simplify the boundary terms.

:::intuition
Cauchy MVT is the "weighted" version of ordinary MVT that relates increments of two functions.

By applying it on [x,c] (or suitable) the f and g increments appear in the numerator and denominator exactly as in the original quotient, while the derivatives appear at the intermediate c; letting x→c forces the intermediate point to c as well and yields the result.
:::

:::example[(the L'Hôpital argument, in outline):]
For the $0/0$ case with finite $L$: suppose $f(c) = g(c) = 0$ (redefine at $c$ if needed, using the limits). Fix $x$ near $c$ and apply Cauchy MVT on the interval with endpoints $x$ and $c$:
:::

:::solution
$$\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{g(x) - g(c)} = \frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)} \quad \text{for some } \xi \text{ between } x \text{ and } c.$$

Since $f(c) = g(c) = 0$, the left side is exactly $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$. As $x \to c$, the intermediate point $\xi$ is squeezed to $c$ as well, so if $\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)} \to L$ then $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \to L$.
:::

:::example[(Cauchy MVT with numbers):]
Verify Cauchy MVT for $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x^3$ on $[1, 2]$. Both are polynomials, hence continuous on $[1,2]$ and differentiable on $(1,2)$, and $g'(x) = 3x^2 \neq 0$ on $(1,2)$. We seek $c$ with
:::

:::solution
$$\frac{f(2) - f(1)}{g(2) - g(1)} = \frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \frac{4 - 1}{8 - 1} = \frac{2c}{3c^2} = \frac{2}{3c}.$$

The left side is $\tfrac37$. Solving $\tfrac{2}{3c} = \tfrac37$ gives $3c \cdot 3 = 2 \cdot 7$, so $9c = 14$, $c = \tfrac{14}{9} \approx 1.556 \in (1, 2)$. ✓ This is the two-function analogue of the single tangent-parallel-to-secant statement.
:::

## Common Pitfalls and When the Rule Does Not Apply

<!-- section: pitfalls-when-not-apply -->

- The limit of f'/g' must exist (or be ±∞). If it does not, the rule says nothing; the original limit may still exist or not.
- g' must not be zero in the punctured neighborhood (otherwise the quotient f'/g' may not even be defined).
- Classic counter-example where lim f'/g' does not exist but lim f/g does: f(x) = x + sin x, g(x) = x, as x→∞. f'/g' = 1 + cos x oscillates, does not exist; but f/g → 1.
- Another: lim f/g may exist while lim f'/g' does not, or they may exist but be unequal if hypotheses violated.

:::intuition
L'Hôpital is a "if the derivatives' ratio settles, then the original does" implication, not an "if and only if".

You must check the hypotheses; blindly differentiating numerator and denominator can give wrong answers or inconclusive results.
:::

:::example[(L'Hôpital fails but the limit exists):]
Consider $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x + \sin x}{x}$, an $\infty/\infty$ form.
:::

:::solution
Direct route (works): $\dfrac{x + \sin x}{x} = 1 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$. Since $\left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right| \le \frac{1}{x} \to 0$, the squeeze theorem gives limit $1 + 0 = 1$.
L'Hôpital route (fails): the derivative ratio is $\dfrac{1 + \cos x}{1} = 1 + \cos x$, which oscillates between $0$ and $2$ and has **no** limit as $x \to \infty$.

The hypothesis "$\lim f'/g'$ exists" is violated, so L'Hôpital simply says nothing here — it does not tell you the original limit fails. The moral: L'Hôpital is a one-way implication, and a non-existent derivative ratio is *inconclusive*, not a proof that $\lim f/g$ diverges.
:::

:::example[(do not apply to a non-indeterminate form):]
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{x + 1}$ is **not** indeterminate: numerator $\to 1$, denominator $\to 1$, so the limit is simply $\tfrac11 = 1$ by direct substitution. Blindly "L'Hôpital-ing" would give $\dfrac{-\sin x}{1} \to 0$ — the wrong answer, because the rule's hypotheses ($0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$) were never met. Always confirm the form first.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying L'Hôpital when the limit of f'/g' fails to exist**: The theorem gives no information; the original limit may exist anyway (see counter-example).
- **Repeatedly differentiating without checking the form remains indeterminate**: After one application the new limit may be determinate; further applications are unnecessary or wrong.
- **Forgetting that 0^0, ∞^0 etc. require rewriting (usually via exp and ln)**: You cannot directly differentiate the base and exponent.
- **Using L'Hôpital for limits that are not indeterminate**: If lim f and lim g are both nonzero finite, the rule is not needed and may give a different (wrong) answer if misapplied.
- **Ignoring that g' ≠ 0 is required in a whole neighborhood**: A zero of g' at isolated points can be handled by continuity arguments, but the statement usually assumes g' ≠ 0 to keep the quotient defined.
