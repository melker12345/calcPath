# Riemann Integration

Riemann sums, the fundamental theorem of calculus, improper integrals, and Riemann–Stieltjes integration.

The Riemann integral is the rigorous definition of the area under a curve as the limit of Riemann sums (upper and lower sums over partitions) as the mesh of the partition tends to zero.

It is the integral used in elementary calculus; later the Lebesgue integral extends it to a much larger class of functions while preserving good limit theorems.

## Upper and Lower Sums; Partitions

<!-- section: upper-lower-sums -->

A partition $P$ of $[a,b]$ is a finite increasing sequence $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b$.

On each subinterval $[x_{i-1}, x_i]$ let $m_i = \inf f$ on that subinterval, $M_i = \sup f$.

The lower sum $L(f,P) = \sum m_i \,\Delta x_i$, upper sum $U(f,P) = \sum M_i \,\Delta x_i$.

$f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ if $\sup$ over all partitions of $L(f,P)$ equals $\inf$ of $U(f,P)$; that common value is the integral $\int_a^b f$.

**ELI5**

- For a given way of slicing the interval into thin strips, the lower sum is the total area of rectangles that fit under the graph (using the lowest height in each strip); the upper sum uses the tallest height in each strip.
- If as you make the slices thinner and thinner (mesh →0) the lower and upper totals get squeezed together to the same number, that number is the Riemann integral.

**Worked Example:**

Take $f(x) = x$ on $[0,1]$ with the uniform partition into $n$ equal parts, so $x_k = k/n$ and $\Delta x_i = 1/n$. Since $f$ is increasing, on $[x_{k-1}, x_k] = [(k-1)/n, k/n]$ the infimum is the left value $m_k = (k-1)/n$ and the supremum is the right value $M_k = k/n$.

Lower sum:
$$L(f,P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{k-1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k-1) = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot\frac{(n-1)n}{2} = \frac{n-1}{2n}.$$
Upper sum:
$$U(f,P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{k}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \frac{n+1}{2n}.$$

Their difference is $U - L = \dfrac{n+1}{2n} - \dfrac{n-1}{2n} = \dfrac{2}{2n} = \dfrac{1}{n} \to 0$, so the squeeze forces a common value. Both sums approach $\tfrac{1}{2}$ as $n \to \infty$, so $\int_0^1 x\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

Sanity check: this is exactly the area of the triangle with base $1$ and height $1$, namely $\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = \tfrac12$. $\checkmark$

## Criterion for Riemann Integrability (Riemann's Criterion)

<!-- section: riemann-criterion -->

A bounded function $f$ on $[a,b]$ is Riemann integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a partition $P$ such that $U(f,P) - L(f,P) < \varepsilon$. (Boundedness is required for the sup/inf on each subinterval to be finite.)

Equivalently, the upper integral $\inf_P U(f,P)$ and the lower integral $\sup_P L(f,P)$ agree.

Continuous functions on $[a,b]$ are Riemann integrable: since $[a,b]$ is compact, $f$ is uniformly continuous, so on fine enough partitions the oscillation $M_i - m_i$ on each subinterval is uniformly small, making $U - L$ small.

**ELI5**

- The difference $U - L$ measures the total "uncertainty" or "slack" in the area estimate coming from the variation of $f$ inside the slices — it is the combined area of the thin gap between the over-estimate and under-estimate rectangles.
- If you can make that slack arbitrarily small by choosing a fine enough partition, the area is pinned to a single number and is well-defined.

**Worked Example:**

Let $f$ jump once on $[0,1]$: $f(x) = 0$ for $x < \tfrac12$ and $f(x) = 1$ for $x \ge \tfrac12$. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and build a partition that isolates the jump inside one thin interval $[\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\delta}{2},\, \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\delta}{2}]$ of width $\delta$, with the two flat pieces $[0, \tfrac12 - \tfrac{\delta}{2}]$ and $[\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\delta}{2}, 1]$ each a single subinterval.

- On the two flat subintervals $f$ is constant, so $M_i - m_i = 0$ there; they contribute $0$ to $U - L$.
- On the jump subinterval, $m_i = 0$ and $M_i = 1$, so its contribution is $(M_i - m_i)\,\Delta x_i = 1\cdot\delta = \delta$.

Hence $U(f,P) - L(f,P) = \delta$. Choosing $\delta < \varepsilon$ (e.g. $\delta = \varepsilon/2$) gives $U - L < \varepsilon$, so Riemann's criterion is satisfied and $f$ is integrable.

Sanity check: the area is one rectangle of height $1$ over $[\tfrac12, 1]$, so $\int_0^1 f = \tfrac12$, and indeed $L \le \tfrac12 \le U$ with $U - L = \delta \to 0$. $\checkmark$

## Discontinuities and Integrability

<!-- section: discontinuities-integrability -->

A bounded function on $[a,b]$ with finitely many discontinuities is Riemann integrable: isolate each of the finitely many bad points inside its own small interval whose total length is $< \varepsilon/(2M)$, where $M = \sup|f|$; on those intervals the oscillation is at most $2M$, so their combined contribution to $U - L$ is $< \varepsilon$, and on the remaining compact pieces $f$ is continuous, hence has small oscillation on a fine enough sub-partition.

Thomae's function ($f(p/q) = 1/q$ for $p/q$ rational in lowest terms, $f = 0$ at irrationals) is Riemann integrable on $[0,1]$ with integral $0$, even though it is discontinuous at every rational (its discontinuities are infinite but not dense-with-oscillation the way Dirichlet's are).

The Dirichlet function ($1$ on rationals, $0$ on irrationals) is not Riemann integrable ($U = 1$, $L = 0$ for every partition). The contrast with Thomae is the key lesson: infinitely many discontinuities are fine as long as the *sizes* of the jumps can be squeezed down; it is dense discontinuity of a fixed size that kills integrability.

**ELI5**

- A few jumps or spikes of zero width do not affect the area; you can hide their uncertainty in tiny slices.
- If discontinuities are dense (rationals), the upper sums stay high (using the 1's) and lower sums stay low (using the 0's) no matter how fine the partition; the area is ambiguous.

**Worked Example (Dirichlet, not integrable):**

The characteristic function of the rationals in $[0,1]$ has, in every subinterval, $\inf = 0$ (there is always an irrational) and $\sup = 1$ (there is always a rational). So for any partition $M_i - m_i = 1$ on every piece, giving
$$U - L = \sum_i 1\cdot \Delta x_i = \sum_i \Delta x_i = b - a = 1,$$
which can never be made $< 1$. Riemann's criterion fails, so $f$ is not integrable.

**Worked Example (Thomae, integrable with integral $0$):**

For Thomae's function on $[0,1]$, fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and pick $N$ with $1/(N+1) < \varepsilon/2$.

- Step 1 (the tall values are few): the points where $f(x) \ge 1/N$ are exactly the rationals $p/q$ with denominator $q \le N$ in lowest terms. In $[0,1]$ there are only finitely many such points — call them $r_1,\dots,r_m$. Cover each $r_j$ by a tiny interval so their total length is $< \varepsilon/2$. Since $0 \le f \le 1$, these intervals contribute at most $1 \cdot (\varepsilon/2) = \varepsilon/2$ to $U - L$.
- Step 2 (everywhere else $f$ is small): on the rest of $[0,1]$ every point has $f(x) \le 1/(N+1) < \varepsilon/2$ (irrationals give $0$; the remaining rationals have $q > N$, i.e. $q \ge N+1$, so $f(x)=1/q \le 1/(N+1)$). Because the lower sum is always $0$ (each subinterval contains an irrational, so $m_i = 0$), these pieces contribute at most $(\varepsilon/2)\cdot(b-a) = \varepsilon/2$ to $U$, hence to $U - L$.

Adding the two parts, $U - L < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon$. So $f$ is integrable. Since $L(f,P) = 0$ for every $P$ and $U$ can be made arbitrarily small, the integral is $0$.

Sanity check: the value at each rational is a single point of zero width, so it is plausible that the area is $0$; the argument makes "the tall spikes are too sparse to accumulate area" precise. $\checkmark$

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking every bounded function is Riemann integrable**: The Dirichlet function is the standard counter-example.
- **Confusing Riemann with Lebesgue**: Lebesgue integrates the Dirichlet function (integral 0) because the rationals have measure zero.
- **Forgetting that the partition must be chosen after ε (the definition is existential on partitions)**: You do not have to use equal-width partitions or regular grids; arbitrary partitions are allowed and often necessary.
- **Believing that if f is integrable then f must be continuous a.e.**: True for Riemann (a theorem of Lebesgue), but the proof is nontrivial; there exist integrable functions discontinuous on a dense set (Thomae).
- **Mixing upper/lower sums with Riemann sums using tags**: The definition via upper/lower Darboux sums is equivalent to the limit of Riemann sums over tagged partitions with mesh→0.

The fundamental theorem of calculus (FTC) establishes that differentiation and (Riemann) integration are inverse operations, in two complementary parts.

It is the reason the indefinite integral (antiderivative) and definite integral are so closely linked, and why we can compute areas by finding antiderivatives.

## FTC Part 1: The Integral as a Function of the Upper Limit

<!-- section: ftc-part-one -->

Let $f$ be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$. Define $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ for $x \in [a,b]$.

Then $F$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ (in fact Lipschitz, since $|F(x) - F(y)| \le M|x-y|$ with $M = \sup|f|$). If in addition $f$ is continuous at $c \in [a,b]$, then $F$ is differentiable at $c$ and $F'(c) = f(c)$.

In other words, "the derivative of the integral is the integrand" — but only at points where $f$ is continuous.

**ELI5**

- $F(x)$ is the accumulated signed area from a fixed left endpoint $a$ up to the variable right endpoint $x$. As $x$ moves a tiny bit to the right, the added sliver of area is approximately $f(x)$ times the tiny width, so the rate of change of $F$ is $f(x)$.
- If $f$ has a jump, $F$ is still continuous (the accumulated area doesn't jump) but has a corner there (its slope jumps from the left-hand height to the right-hand height).

**Worked Example:**

Let $f(x) = x$ on $[0,1]$, which is continuous everywhere. Then
$$F(x) = \int_0^x t\,dt = \left[\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^x = \frac{x^2}{2}.$$
Differentiating, $F'(x) = x = f(x)$, matching FTC Part 1.

Sanity check via the definition: the difference quotient is
$$\frac{F(x+h) - F(x)}{h} = \frac{(x+h)^2/2 - x^2/2}{h} = \frac{xh + h^2/2}{h} = x + \frac{h}{2} \xrightarrow{h\to 0} x = f(x).\ \checkmark$$

## FTC Part 2: Evaluation of Definite Integrals via Antiderivatives

<!-- section: ftc-part-two -->

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ ($F' = f$ on $(a,b)$, $F$ continuous up to ends), then $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$.

This is the familiar "take antiderivative, plug in limits" rule used in calculus.

**ELI5**

- The net signed area under f from a to b equals the total change in any antiderivative from a to b.
- It turns the hard problem of summing infinitely many infinitesimal areas into the (often) easier problem of finding one antiderivative.

**Worked Example (easy):**

$$\int_0^1 x\,dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}.$$
The antiderivative $x^2/2$ changes by $\tfrac12$ over the interval, which is exactly the area. This agrees with the squeeze computation in the first section. $\checkmark$

**Worked Example (harder):**

Compute $\displaystyle\int_1^2 \frac{1}{x}\,dx$. Here $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous on $[1,2]$, and an antiderivative is $F(x) = \ln x$ (since $F'(x) = 1/x$). By Part 2,
$$\int_1^2 \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln 2 - \ln 1 = \ln 2 - 0 = \ln 2 \approx 0.693.$$
Sanity check: $1/x$ lies between $\tfrac12$ and $1$ on $[1,2]$, so the area is between $\tfrac12\cdot 1 = 0.5$ and $1\cdot 1 = 1$; indeed $0.5 < 0.693 < 1$. $\checkmark$

## Proof Ideas and the Role of Continuity

<!-- section: proof-ideas-continuity -->

Part 1 is proved by writing the difference quotient $\dfrac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h} = \dfrac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt$, then using that when $f$ is continuous at $x$ this integral average is close to $f(x)$ (by bounding $|f(t)-f(x)|$ small on a small interval around $x$).

Part 2 follows from Part 1: if $F' = f$ then the function $G(x) = \int_a^x f - F(x)$ has $G' = f - f = 0$, hence $G$ is constant, hence $G(b) = G(a)$, which rearranges to the desired equality.

Continuity of $f$ guarantees both the existence of the integral $\int_a^x f$ and that Part 1 gives $F' = f$, so that every antiderivative works in Part 2.

**ELI5**

- The average value of f over a tiny interval around x is forced to be close to f(x) by continuity; that average is exactly the difference quotient for the accumulated-area function.
- Once you know the derivative of the integral recovers f, any other function with the same derivative differs by a constant, so the net changes match.

**Worked Example:**

To prove Part 2: let $G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt - F(x)$. By Part 1, $G'(x) = f(x) - F'(x) = 0$ on $(a,b)$. Thus $G$ is constant on $[a,b]$ (by MVT or connectedness). Therefore $G(b) - G(a) = 0$, i.e. $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$.

## Generalizations and Limitations (Improper, Lebesgue)

<!-- section: generalizations-limitations -->

FTC Part 1 requires $f$ integrable (and continuity of $f$ at a point to get differentiability there); the strong form of Part 2 usually requires $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$. For improper integrals one takes limits of proper integrals.

In Lebesgue theory the FTC holds under weaker assumptions (absolute integrability, almost-everywhere differentiability of the indefinite integral, etc.), and the fundamental theorem of calculus for Lebesgue integrals recovers f almost everywhere.

**ELI5**

- If f has a bad discontinuity the indefinite integral may still be differentiable almost everywhere and recover f a.e. (Lebesgue).
- Improper integrals at endpoints or with singularities are handled by limits; the "FTC" then says the improper integral equals the limiting difference of the antiderivative (when the limit exists).

**Worked Example:**

The improper integral $\int_1^\infty \dfrac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ is handled by applying FTC Part 2 on $[1,b]$ and then taking a limit. An antiderivative of $1/x^2$ is $-1/x$, so
$$\int_1^b \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_1^b = -\frac{1}{b} - \left(-\frac{1}{1}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{b}.$$
Now let $b \to \infty$:
$$\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left(1 - \frac{1}{b}\right) = 1.$$
This equals "the antiderivative evaluated in the limit at $\infty$ minus at $1$", i.e. $0 - (-1) = 1$, valid because $-1/x$ has a finite limit as $x\to\infty$.

Sanity check: the region is unbounded to the right, but the tails shrink fast enough ($1/x^2$ decays faster than $1/x$) that the total area is finite. $\checkmark$

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying Part 2 when f is not continuous (only integrable)**: The antiderivative may fail to exist in the classical sense or the formula may need interpretation.
- **Forgetting the constant of integration when finding antiderivatives**: The evaluation F(b)−F(a) automatically cancels the constant.
- **Confusing the two parts**: Part 1 says differentiation undoes integration (under continuity); Part 2 says integration undoes differentiation (for any antiderivative).
- **Using FTC on functions that are not integrable**: e.g. 1/√x on (0,1] is improperly integrable but the proper integral on [ε,1] is fine; the theorem applies after taking limits.
- **Thinking every derivative is integrable**: There exist functions differentiable everywhere whose derivative is not Riemann (or even Lebesgue) integrable on [a,b]. The FTC still holds in a weaker sense for the Lebesgue integral when f' exists a.e. and is integrable.

An improper integral is a definite integral in which either the interval of integration is unbounded or the integrand has a discontinuity (singularity) inside the interval. It is defined as a limit of proper (ordinary Riemann) integrals.

Improper integrals appear throughout analysis, physics, and probability (e.g., the Gaussian integral, Laplace transforms, expected values of continuous random variables with unbounded support).

## Unbounded Intervals (Limits at Infinity)

<!-- section: unbounded-intervals -->

The integral $\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,dx$ is defined as $\lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$, provided the limit exists (finite).

Similarly for $\int_{-\infty}^b f$ and the two-sided $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f$, which is defined as $\int_{-\infty}^c f + \int_c^\infty f$ and requires *both* one-sided improper integrals to converge separately (the choice of split point $c$ does not matter). The Cauchy principal value may exist even when this ordinary two-sided improper integral does not.

**ELI5**

- You compute the integral out to a large but finite B, then let B run off to infinity and see whether the accumulated area settles to a definite finite number.
- If the tails keep adding a fixed positive amount no matter how far you go, the integral diverges to infinity.

**Worked Example (the $p$-test at infinity):**

Determine when $\int_1^\infty \dfrac{dx}{x^p}$ converges.

Case $p \ne 1$: an antiderivative is $\dfrac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}$, so
$$\int_1^b \frac{dx}{x^p} = \left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^b = \frac{b^{1-p} - 1}{1-p}.$$
As $b \to \infty$, the term $b^{1-p} \to 0$ exactly when the exponent $1 - p < 0$, i.e. $p > 1$; then the limit is $\dfrac{0 - 1}{1-p} = \dfrac{1}{p-1}$ (finite). If $p < 1$ then $b^{1-p} \to \infty$, so the integral diverges.

Case $p = 1$: $\int_1^b \dfrac{dx}{x} = \ln b \to \infty$, so it diverges.

Conclusion: $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$ converges if and only if $p > 1$, with value $\dfrac{1}{p-1}$.

Sanity check: at $p = 2$ this gives $\dfrac{1}{2-1} = 1$, matching the $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1$ computed earlier. $\checkmark$

## Integrable Singularities (Limits at Finite Points)

<!-- section: singularities-finite -->

If $f$ has a singularity (e.g. blows up) at an interior point $c \in (a,b)$, one defines the improper integral as $\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+} \int_a^{c-\varepsilon} f + \lim_{\eta\to 0^+}\int_{c+\eta}^b f$, requiring the two one-sided limits to exist *separately*. If the singularity is at an endpoint, only one such limit is taken.

Common singularities: $1/\sqrt{x}$ or $1/x^\alpha$ near $0$. The $p$-test at $0$ mirrors the one at $\infty$ but flips: $\int_0^1 x^{-\alpha}\,dx$ converges if and only if $\alpha < 1$ (mild enough blow-up).

**ELI5**

- The graph may shoot up to infinity or oscillate wildly at c, but if the "spike" is narrow enough and not too tall the area under it may still be finite.
- You cut out a small symmetric (or one-sided) neighborhood around the bad point, integrate on the good parts, then let the neighborhood shrink to zero and see if the missing piece's area contribution tends to a limit.

**Worked Example:**

Evaluate $\int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x}}$. The integrand blows up at the endpoint $0$, so this is improper there. Here $\alpha = \tfrac12 < 1$, so we expect convergence.

An antiderivative of $x^{-1/2}$ is $2\sqrt{x}$ (since $\frac{d}{dx}\,2x^{1/2} = x^{-1/2}$). Integrate from $\varepsilon$ to $1$ and let $\varepsilon \to 0^+$:
$$\int_\varepsilon^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}} = \left[2\sqrt{x}\right]_\varepsilon^1 = 2\sqrt{1} - 2\sqrt{\varepsilon} = 2 - 2\sqrt{\varepsilon} \xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0^+} 2.$$
Hence the improper integral converges to $2$.

Sanity check: even though the graph shoots to $+\infty$ at $0$, the spike is narrow enough ($\alpha < 1$) that the area is finite. Contrast $\int_0^1 dx/x$ ($\alpha = 1$), which gives $\ln 1 - \ln\varepsilon = -\ln\varepsilon \to \infty$: divergent. $\checkmark$

## Convergence Tests (Comparison, Limit Comparison, Absolute vs Conditional)

<!-- section: convergence-tests-improper -->

For positive integrands the usual comparison and limit comparison tests apply (compare with a known p-integral or exponential).

Absolute convergence: if $\int |f| < \infty$ then the improper integral of $f$ converges (and equals $\int f^+ - \int f^-$, the difference of the integrals of the positive and negative parts, both of which are finite since $f^\pm \le |f|$).

Conditional convergence for improper integrals is more subtle (oscillatory tails); Dirichlet test for integrals, etc.

**ELI5**

- If |f| has finite area, then the signed f cannot accumulate infinite positive or negative area; cancellations are controlled.
- For oscillating tails that decay slowly, absolute area may be infinite while signed cancellations still produce a finite net area (like alternating series).

**Worked Example (conditional convergence):**

Show $\int_1^\infty \dfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$ converges but $\int_1^\infty \left|\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\right|\,dx$ diverges.

Convergence of the signed integral (integration by parts). With $u = 1/x$, $dv = \sin x\,dx$, so $v = -\cos x$:
$$\int_1^b \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx = \left[-\frac{\cos x}{x}\right]_1^b - \int_1^b \frac{\cos x}{x^2}\,dx = \cos 1 - \frac{\cos b}{b} - \int_1^b \frac{\cos x}{x^2}\,dx.$$
As $b \to \infty$: the boundary term $\dfrac{\cos b}{b} \to 0$ (bounded numerator over $b\to\infty$), and the remaining integral converges absolutely because $\left|\dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}\right| \le \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1 < \infty$. So the limit exists and the integral converges.

Divergence of the absolute integral. On each interval $[k\pi, (k+1)\pi]$ we have $x \le (k+1)\pi$, so
$$\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} \frac{|\sin x|}{x}\,dx \ge \frac{1}{(k+1)\pi}\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} |\sin x|\,dx = \frac{1}{(k+1)\pi}\cdot 2 = \frac{2}{(k+1)\pi},$$
using $\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}|\sin x|\,dx = 2$ over one hump. Summing over $k$ gives a constant times the harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{k+1}$, which diverges. Hence $\int_1^\infty |\sin x / x|\,dx = \infty$.

Sanity check: this is the integral analogue of the alternating harmonic series ($\sum (-1)^n/n$ converges) versus the harmonic series ($\sum 1/n$ diverges) — cancellation saves the signed sum, absolute values do not converge. $\checkmark$

## Principal Value and Other Regularizations

<!-- section: principal-value -->

The Cauchy principal value for ∫_{-∞}^∞ f takes lim_{R→∞} ∫_{-R}^R f (symmetric limits).

For a singularity at $c$ inside a finite interval, $\operatorname{PV}\!\int_a^b = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\!\left(\int_a^{c-\varepsilon} + \int_{c+\varepsilon}^b\right)$.

The PV may exist when the ordinary improper integral does not (e.g. $\int_{-1}^1 dx/x$ has $\operatorname{PV} = 0$ but the ordinary improper integral diverges, because its two one-sided pieces are $-\infty$ and $+\infty$).

**ELI5**

- By approaching the bad point or infinity symmetrically from both sides, positive and negative infinities (or oscillations) may cancel in a principal-value sense even if one-sided they do not settle.
- PV is a useful regularization in physics and Fourier analysis, but it is not the same as the Lebesgue or ordinary improper integral.

**Worked Example:**

Compute $\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{-1}^1 \dfrac{dx}{x}$. The singularity is at the interior point $c = 0$, so the principal value uses one symmetric cutoff $\varepsilon$ on both sides:
$$\operatorname{PV}\!\int_{-1}^1 \frac{dx}{x} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^+}\left(\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{dx}{x} + \int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dx}{x}\right).$$
An antiderivative is $\ln|x|$. Evaluate each piece:
$$\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{dx}{x} = \big[\ln|x|\big]_{-1}^{-\varepsilon} = \ln\varepsilon - \ln 1 = \ln\varepsilon,$$
$$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dx}{x} = \big[\ln|x|\big]_{\varepsilon}^{1} = \ln 1 - \ln\varepsilon = -\ln\varepsilon.$$
Their sum is $\ln\varepsilon + (-\ln\varepsilon) = 0$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, so the limit is $\operatorname{PV} = 0$.

Contrast with the ordinary improper integral, which would require the two one-sided limits to exist separately: $\int_{\varepsilon}^1 dx/x = -\ln\varepsilon \to +\infty$ and $\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon} dx/x = \ln\varepsilon \to -\infty$. Neither converges, so the ordinary improper integral diverges even though the symmetric PV is $0$.

Sanity check: $1/x$ is odd, so the symmetric procedure cancels the two infinite halves against each other — that is exactly what the PV exploits, and why it can be finite when the plain improper integral is not. $\checkmark$

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming that if the antiderivative has a limit at infinity then the integral converges**: True only if the integrand is the derivative of that antiderivative and the fundamental theorem applies in the limit; counter-examples exist when f is not integrable but F has limits.
- **Confusing absolute and conditional convergence for improper integrals**: Absolute is safer for term-by-term operations; conditional requires more care (like alternating series).
- **Forgetting that existence of lim_{b→∞} F(b) − F(a) requires that F'(x) = f(x) and f integrable on every finite piece**: If f has non-integrable singularities the antiderivative may not be defined in the classical sense.
- **Thinking every improper integral that 'looks convergent' by comparison is Lebesgue integrable**: On the real line Riemann and Lebesgue improper integrals coincide for positive functions; differences appear for signed functions with conditional convergence.
- **Using the same variable for the limit and the dummy integration variable**: Always write $\lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ with distinct variables.

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral generalizes the ordinary Riemann integral by replacing the "dx" (length element) with dα where α is a monotone integrator function.

When α(x) = x one recovers the Riemann integral. When α is a step function the integral reduces to a finite or infinite sum. It provides a unified language for both integrals and sums and is the natural setting for integration by parts in Stieltjes form.

## Definition via Riemann–Stieltjes Sums

<!-- section: definition-stieltjes-sums -->

Given a partition $P$ of $[a,b]$ and tags $t_i$ in each subinterval, the Riemann–Stieltjes sum is $\sum f(t_i)\,(\alpha(x_i) - \alpha(x_{i-1}))$.

$f$ is Riemann–Stieltjes integrable with respect to $\alpha$ (written $\int f\,d\alpha$) if there is a number $I$ such that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a partition $P_\varepsilon$ so that for any refinement and any choice of tags the sum is within $\varepsilon$ of $I$.

A basic sufficient condition: if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $\alpha$ is monotone (or of bounded variation), then $\int_a^b f\,d\alpha$ exists. When $\alpha$ has a jump of size $s$ at an interior point $c$ and $f$ is continuous there, the integral picks up a contribution $f(c)\cdot s$ (like a delta / point-mass contribution).

**ELI5**

- Instead of adding f times width of slice, you add f times the change in the "weight function" α over the slice.
- If α is a staircase (jumps at certain points), the integral becomes a weighted sum of the f values at the jump locations, with weights the jump sizes.

**Worked Example:**

Let $\alpha(x) = 0$ for $x < 0$ and $\alpha(x) = 1$ for $x \ge 0$ (a Heaviside step with a single jump of size $1$ at $0$), and let $f$ be continuous. Compute $\int_{-1}^1 f\,d\alpha$.

Take any partition of $[-1,1]$ and any tags $t_i$. The increment $\alpha(x_i) - \alpha(x_{i-1})$ is $0$ on every subinterval *except* the one containing $0$, where it equals $1 - 0 = 1$. So the entire Riemann–Stieltjes sum collapses to a single term:
$$\sum_i f(t_i)\big(\alpha(x_i) - \alpha(x_{i-1})\big) = f(t^\ast)\cdot 1,$$
where $t^\ast$ is the tag in the subinterval straddling $0$. As the mesh $\to 0$ that subinterval shrinks to $\{0\}$, forcing $t^\ast \to 0$, and by continuity $f(t^\ast) \to f(0)$. Hence
$$\int_{-1}^1 f\,d\alpha = f(0).$$
This is the "integral against a Dirac delta" in elementary language: a jump of size $1$ in the integrator samples $f$ at the jump location.

Sanity check: if instead $f \equiv 5$ (constant), the sum is $5\cdot 1 = 5 = f(0)$, as expected. $\checkmark$

## Integration by Parts for Stieltjes Integrals

<!-- section: integration-by-parts-stieltjes -->

The formula $\int_a^b f\,d\alpha + \int_a^b \alpha\,df = f(b)\alpha(b) - f(a)\alpha(a)$ holds under suitable conditions: if $\int_a^b f\,d\alpha$ exists then $\int_a^b \alpha\,df$ also exists and the identity is true (a natural sufficient setting is $f$ and $\alpha$ having no common discontinuities). Note the symmetry — the roles of integrand and integrator swap.

This is the Stieltjes version of the familiar ∫ u dv = u v − ∫ v du .

It is extremely useful for converting between sums and integrals or for moving derivatives from one factor to another.

**ELI5**

- The product f α "telescopes" its changes; the total change f(b)α(b) − f(a)α(a) equals the sum of "f changes α" plus "α changes f".
- This is the discrete product rule summed up, or the continuous product rule integrated.

**Worked Example (counting via a step integrator):**

Take $\alpha(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ on $[0,n]$ (with $n$ a positive integer) and $f(x) = x$. The integrator $\lfloor x\rfloor$ has a jump of size $1$ at each integer $k = 1, 2, \dots, n$ inside $(0,n]$, and $f$ is continuous, so the Stieltjes integral is the sum of $f$ at the jumps weighted by the jump sizes:
$$\int_0^n x\,d\lfloor x\rfloor = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(k)\cdot 1 = \sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$

Now verify with integration by parts. Here $\int_a^b \alpha\,df = \int_0^n \lfloor x\rfloor\,dx$ (since $df = dx$). On $[k, k+1)$ we have $\lfloor x\rfloor = k$, so
$$\int_0^n \lfloor x\rfloor\,dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k\cdot 1 = \frac{(n-1)n}{2}.$$
The boundary term is $f(n)\alpha(n) - f(0)\alpha(0) = n\cdot n - 0\cdot 0 = n^2$. The Stieltjes integration-by-parts identity then predicts
$$\int_0^n x\,d\lfloor x\rfloor = \big[f(n)\alpha(n) - f(0)\alpha(0)\big] - \int_0^n \lfloor x\rfloor\,dx = n^2 - \frac{(n-1)n}{2} = \frac{2n^2 - n^2 + n}{2} = \frac{n^2 + n}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$

Sanity check: both routes give $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$, the familiar formula for $1 + 2 + \cdots + n$. This is summation by parts in disguise: replacing an integrator by a step function turns the Stieltjes formula into the discrete Abel summation identity. $\checkmark$

## Relation to Riemann and Lebesgue; When the Integral Exists

<!-- section: relation-riemann-lebesgue-existence -->

If α(x)=x the definition reduces exactly to the Riemann integral.

If $\alpha$ is absolutely continuous ($\alpha(x) = \int_a^x g(t)\,dt$ with $g$ integrable, so $\alpha' = g$ a.e.) then $\int_a^b f\,d\alpha = \int_a^b f(x)\,g(x)\,dx$ (an ordinary integral with weight $g$).

The integral $\int f\,d\alpha$ exists whenever $f$ and $\alpha$ have no common point of discontinuity (at each point, at least one of them is continuous).

**ELI5**

- Stieltjes with respect to x is ordinary area.
- Stieltjes with respect to a smooth increasing α is ordinary integral with weight α'.
- If f jumps exactly where α jumps the "product of jumps" creates an ambiguous contribution that prevents the integral from existing in the Riemann–Stieltjes sense.

**Worked Example (absolutely continuous integrator):**

Let $\alpha(x) = x^2$ on $[0,1]$, which is absolutely continuous with $\alpha'(x) = g(x) = 2x$. Take $f(x) = x$. Then
$$\int_0^1 x\,d(x^2) = \int_0^1 x\cdot 2x\,dx = \int_0^1 2x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{2x^3}{3}\right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3}.$$
Sanity check via integration by parts: the boundary term is $f(1)\alpha(1) - f(0)\alpha(0) = 1\cdot 1 - 0 = 1$, and $\int_0^1 \alpha\,df = \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13$, so $\int_0^1 x\,d(x^2) = 1 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$. $\checkmark$

**Worked Example (singular integrator, no density):**

Let $\alpha$ be the Cantor function: continuous, constant on each interval removed in building the Cantor set $C$, and increasing from $0$ to $1$ while all its increase happens on $C$ (which has measure zero). Because $\alpha' = 0$ almost everywhere, one cannot write $\int f\,d\alpha = \int f\,\alpha'\,dx = \int f\cdot 0\,dx = 0$ — that would be wrong. Instead $\int f\,d\alpha$ is a genuine "singular integral" that assigns all its weight to the measure-zero set $C$ and sees only the values of $f$ on $C$; it is not given by any ordinary density function. This is the cautionary case behind the common mistake "$d\alpha = \alpha'\,dx$".

## Applications: Probability (CDFs), Summation by Parts, Spectral Theory

<!-- section: applications-probability-sums -->

- In probability, if F is the cumulative distribution function of a random variable, then E[g(X)] = ∫ g dF (Stieltjes integral). This unifies discrete (sum p_k g(x_k)), continuous (∫ g f dx), and singular distributions.
- Summation by parts is the discrete Stieltjes integration by parts.
- In spectral theory and functional analysis the spectral theorem is often stated with Stieltjes integrals against projection-valued measures.

**ELI5**

- The expectation of a function of a random variable is "integrate g against the probability mass" — when the distribution has atoms (jumps in the CDF) you get ordinary sums; when it has a density you get the familiar integral.
- This language lets probabilists and analysts treat discrete and continuous cases with the same formulas.

**Worked Example:**

For a discrete random variable $X$ taking values $1, 2, 3$ with probabilities $\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16$, the CDF $F$ is a step function with jumps of those sizes at $1, 2, 3$. Taking $g(x) = x$, the Stieltjes integral against $F$ picks up $g$ at each jump weighted by the jump size:
$$E[X] = \int x\,dF(x) = 1\cdot\frac12 + 2\cdot\frac13 + 3\cdot\frac16.$$
Compute over a common denominator of $6$:
$$1\cdot\frac12 = \frac{3}{6},\qquad 2\cdot\frac13 = \frac{4}{6},\qquad 3\cdot\frac16 = \frac{3}{6},$$
$$E[X] = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{4}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = \frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}.$$

Sanity check: the probabilities sum to $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac{3+2+1}{6} = 1$ (a valid distribution), and the mean $\tfrac53 \approx 1.67$ lies between the smallest and largest values $1$ and $3$, as any expectation must. $\checkmark$

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming ∫ f dα always exists when f and α are both of bounded variation**: They must not share discontinuities.
- **Confusing dα with α' dx when α is not absolutely continuous**: The Cantor function has α'=0 almost everywhere yet increases; the Stieltjes integral is not the ordinary integral against 0.
- **Forgetting that the integrator α is usually taken monotone increasing**: The definition can be extended to α of bounded variation by splitting into positive and negative variations, but the basic theory is for monotone α.
- **Thinking the Riemann–Stieltjes integral is the same as the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral**: They coincide when the integral exists in the Riemann–Stieltjes sense, but Lebesgue–Stieltjes is more general (completes with respect to the measure induced by α).
- **Writing ∫ f(x) dα(x) with the same variable on both**: The notation is conventional but the dummy variable in the differential is not the same as a free variable.
