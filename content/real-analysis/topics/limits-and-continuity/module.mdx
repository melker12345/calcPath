# Limits & Continuity

ε-δ limits of functions, continuity, uniform continuity, and the intermediate and extreme value theorems.

The limit of f(x) as x approaches a is the value L that f(x) approaches when x gets close to a (but not necessarily equal to a). This idea is the heart of calculus: derivatives are limits of difference quotients, integrals are limits of Riemann sums, continuity is defined using limits.

The rigorous definition uses ε and δ in a way completely analogous to the sequence case, but now the "index" is a real variable approaching a.

## Intuitive Idea and One-Sided Limits

<!-- section: intuitive-one-sided -->

We write $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ when $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ ($x \neq a$).

Left-hand limit $\lim_{x \to a^-}$ uses only $x < a$; right-hand uses $x > a$. The two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.

Vertical asymptotes, removable discontinuities, and jump discontinuities are all described with limits.

**ELI5**

- Imagine walking toward a point a on the x-axis; look at the height of the graph. If the heights settle toward a single number L no matter how you approach (from left or right), that L is the limit.
- The function value exactly at a may be different or even undefined; the limit only cares about nearby points.

**Worked Example:**

Consider $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$, which is undefined at $x = 1$ (division by zero). Find $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$.

- Step 1 — factor the numerator: $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$, so $f(x) = \dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}$.
- Step 2 — cancel the common factor. The cancellation is legal precisely because the limit only sees $x \neq 1$, so $x - 1 \neq 0$: $f(x) = x + 1$ for all $x \neq 1$.
- Step 3 — take the limit of the simplified form: $\lim_{x \to 1}(x+1) = 2$.
- Sanity check: plug in nearby values, $f(0.99) = 1.99$ and $f(1.01) = 2.01$, both close to $2$. The limit is $2$ even though $f(1)$ does not exist — a removable discontinuity, since redefining $f(1) = 2$ would repair it.

Now a limit that does **not** exist. For $g(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{x}$ (defined for $x \neq 0$), the right-hand limit is $\lim_{x \to 0^+} g(x) = 1$ (since $|x|/x = 1$ for $x > 0$) but the left-hand limit is $\lim_{x \to 0^-} g(x) = -1$ (since $|x|/x = -1$ for $x < 0$). The one-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided $\lim_{x \to 0} g(x)$ does not exist — a jump discontinuity.

## The Precise ε-δ Definition

<!-- section: epsilon-delta-limit -->

$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means:

For every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that if 0 < |x − a| < δ then |f(x) − L| < ε.

The δ is allowed to depend on ε (and on a and f), but not on the particular x inside the punctured δ-neighborhood.

This is the direct translation of "no matter how small a tolerance ε around L you demand, I can find a small enough neighborhood around a (excluding a itself) inside which f stays within ε of L."

**ELI5**

- ε is your vertical tolerance on the y-values. δ is the horizontal tolerance you are willing to allow around a.
- You get to choose δ after seeing ε; smaller ε usually forces you to pick a smaller δ.

**Worked Example:**

Prove $\lim_{x \to 3} (2x + 1) = 7$ directly from the definition.

- Step 1 — scratch work (find δ). We want $|(2x+1) - 7| < \varepsilon$. Simplify the left side: $|(2x+1) - 7| = |2x - 6| = 2|x - 3|$. So we need $2|x-3| < \varepsilon$, i.e. $|x-3| < \varepsilon/2$. This tells us to pick $\delta = \varepsilon/2$.
- Step 2 — the actual proof. Let $\varepsilon > 0$ be given. Choose $\delta = \varepsilon/2 > 0$. Suppose $0 < |x - 3| < \delta$.
- Step 3 — verify the conclusion: $|(2x+1) - 7| = 2|x-3| < 2\delta = 2 \cdot \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check: with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ we get $\delta = 0.05$; then $|x-3| < 0.05$ forces $|(2x+1)-7| = 2|x-3| < 0.1$. ✓ Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, the definition is satisfied and the limit is $7$.

Note the two-stage structure: the scratch work in Step 1 is where you *discover* $\delta$ by working backwards, but the clean proof presents $\delta$ up front and derives the conclusion forwards.

## Infinite Limits, Limits at Infinity, and Limit Laws

<!-- section: infinite-limits-laws -->

We say $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = +\infty$ if for every $M > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x-a| < \delta$ implies $f(x) > M$.

Similar for −∞.

$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L$ means for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $x > N$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

Standard limit laws (sum, product, quotient, composition, squeeze) hold for function limits exactly as for sequences, provided the individual limits exist (with the usual caveats).

**ELI5**

- f(x) → +∞ as x→a means the graph shoots upward without bound as you approach a (vertical asymptote).
- f(x) → L as x→∞ means that far to the right the graph settles near height L (horizontal asymptote).

**Worked Example (infinite limit from the definition):**

Prove $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \dfrac{1}{x} = +\infty$.

- Step 1 — unpack the goal: given any threshold $M > 0$, we must force $\dfrac{1}{x} > M$ for $x$ close enough to $0$ on the right.
- Step 2 — solve for $x$: for $x > 0$, the inequality $\dfrac{1}{x} > M$ is equivalent to $x < \dfrac{1}{M}$.
- Step 3 — choose $\delta = \dfrac{1}{M} > 0$. Then $0 < x < \delta$ gives $x < \dfrac{1}{M}$, hence $\dfrac{1}{x} > M$.
- Sanity check: to beat $M = 1000$ we take $\delta = 0.001$; any $0 < x < 0.001$ gives $1/x > 1000$. ✓

**Worked Example (applying the limit laws at infinity):**

Compute $\lim_{x \to \infty} \dfrac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 5}$.

- Step 1 — divide numerator and denominator by the highest power $x^2$ (valid for $x \neq 0$): $\dfrac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 5} = \dfrac{3 + 2/x}{1 - 5/x^2}$.
- Step 2 — apply the standard facts $\lim_{x \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{x} = 0$ and $\lim_{x \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{x^2} = 0$: the numerator $\to 3 + 0 = 3$ and the denominator $\to 1 - 0 = 1$.
- Step 3 — the quotient law applies because the denominator's limit $1 \neq 0$: the limit is $\dfrac{3}{1} = 3$.
- Sanity check: at $x = 100$ the expression is $\dfrac{30200}{9995} \approx 3.02$, close to $3$. ✓ The quotient law's nonzero-denominator hypothesis is what makes this step legal.

## Common Mistakes

- **Requiring f(a) to equal the limit**: The definition explicitly excludes x=a; the limit can exist even when f is undefined or has a different value at a (removable discontinuity).
- **Reversing quantifiers in the definition**: 'There exists ε such that for all δ …' is wrong; it must be for all ε there exists δ.
- **Assuming one-sided limits automatically agree**: A jump discontinuity has different left and right limits.
- **Forgetting that limit laws require the component limits to exist**: You cannot conclude lim (f+g) exists just because you know something about lim f and lim g separately if one fails to exist.
- **Confusing 'limit is infinity' with 'the limit does not exist'**: In extended reals we write =+∞; in strict real analysis we say the limit does not exist (but diverges to +∞).

The ε-δ definition is the precise, quantifier-based formulation that replaced the intuitive but vague language of "approaches" and "arbitrarily close." It is the definition that makes all subsequent rigorous proofs in calculus and analysis possible.

Mastering the ε-δ definition is the single biggest conceptual hurdle in a first real analysis course; once it is internalized, most other definitions (continuity, uniform continuity, derivative, integral) become natural variations on the same theme.

## The Full Formal Statement

<!-- section: the-definition -->

Let f : D ⊆ ℝ → ℝ and let a be a limit point of D. We say

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

if and only if

∀ ε > 0 ∃ δ > 0 such that ∀ x ∈ D,   0 < |x − a| < δ   ⇒   |f(x) − L| < ε .

The phrase "a is a limit point of D" means every punctured neighborhood of a contains points of D (so there are points x ≠ a arbitrarily close to a at which f is defined).

**ELI5**

- "No matter which positive tolerance ε (vertical) I hand you, you must be able to hand me back a positive tolerance δ (horizontal) that forces the function values to stay inside the ε-band whenever x is inside the δ-band around a (but not at a itself)."
- The order of the quantifiers is crucial: ε is chosen first (adversarially), δ responds to it.

**Worked Example:**

Prove $\lim_{x \to 3} x^2 = 9$. Here $f(x) = x^2$, $a = 3$, $L = 9$. The difficulty over the linear case is that $|f(x) - L|$ contains a factor that still depends on $x$, so we need the "restrict δ first" trick.

- Step 1 — factor the target: $|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3|\,|x + 3|$. The factor $|x-3|$ is what we control; the factor $|x+3|$ is the nuisance that varies with $x$.
- Step 2 — tame the nuisance factor by pre-restricting $\delta \le 1$. If $|x - 3| < 1$ then $2 < x < 4$, so $5 < x + 3 < 7$, giving $|x + 3| < 7$. Hence, whenever $|x-3| < 1$, $|x^2 - 9| = |x-3|\,|x+3| < 7\,|x - 3|$.
- Step 3 — now force $7|x-3| < \varepsilon$ by also requiring $|x - 3| < \varepsilon/7$. To satisfy both restrictions at once, choose $\delta = \min\!\left(1, \dfrac{\varepsilon}{7}\right) > 0$.
- Step 4 — verify. Let $\varepsilon > 0$; with this $\delta$, suppose $0 < |x-3| < \delta$. Then $|x-3| < 1$ (so the bound $|x+3| < 7$ holds) and $|x-3| < \varepsilon/7$, hence $|x^2 - 9| < 7 \cdot \dfrac{\varepsilon}{7} = \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check: for $\varepsilon = 0.7$ we get $\delta = \min(1, 0.1) = 0.1$; then $|x-3| < 0.1$ means $2.9 < x < 3.1$, so $8.41 < x^2 < 9.61$, i.e. $|x^2 - 9| < 0.61 < 0.7$. ✓

## Proving Limits from the Definition (Typical Strategies)

<!-- section: proofs-using-def -->

Common techniques:
- Factor or bound |f(x) − L| by a constant times |x − a| (linear case, polynomials).
- Restrict δ to a convenient value (e.g. ≤1) to control other factors that depend on x.
- Use the triangle inequality or reverse triangle inequality.
- For rational functions, bound the denominator away from zero in a small neighborhood.

The δ you produce is allowed to be any positive number that works; there is no "best" or "largest" δ required.

**ELI5**

- You are allowed to make the neighborhood smaller than you think you need in order to simplify the algebra (the "min(1, …)" trick).
- Once you have an expression |f(x)−L| ≤ K |x−a|, you simply solve for |x−a| < ε/K.

**Worked Example:**

Prove $\lim_{x \to 0} x \sin(1/x) = 0$ (the classic "oscillating but squeezed" limit; note $\sin(1/x)$ itself has no limit at $0$, but the $x$ factor damps it).

- Step 1 — bound the bad factor. For every $x \neq 0$, $|\sin(1/x)| \le 1$ (sine is always between $-1$ and $1$, no matter how wildly its argument oscillates).
- Step 2 — bound the whole expression: $|x \sin(1/x) - 0| = |x|\,|\sin(1/x)| \le |x| \cdot 1 = |x|$.
- Step 3 — choose $\delta = \varepsilon$. Let $\varepsilon > 0$; if $0 < |x| < \delta = \varepsilon$ then $|x \sin(1/x) - 0| \le |x| < \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check: at $x = 0.01$, $\sin(1/x) = \sin(100)$ lies in $[-1,1]$, so $|x\sin(1/x)| \le 0.01$ regardless of the oscillation. ✓ This is the squeeze theorem in disguise ($-|x| \le x\sin(1/x) \le |x|$ with both bounds $\to 0$), but here we proved it directly from the definition.

## Limit Laws and Their ε-δ Proofs

<!-- section: limit-laws-epsdelta -->

To prove the sum rule from the definition:

Assume lim f = L, lim g = M.

Given ε>0, obtain δ1 for f with ε/2, δ2 for g with ε/2.

Let δ = min(δ1,δ2).

Then 0<|x−a|<δ implies both |f−L|<ε/2 and |g−M|<ε/2, hence |(f+g)−(L+M)| < ε.

Similar (but more bookkeeping) proofs exist for product, quotient, and composition.

**ELI5**

- You split your total error budget ε into pieces (ε/2 + ε/2) and ask each function to stay within its piece.
- The min of the two δ's makes both promises hold simultaneously.

**Worked Example:**

Prove the product rule $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)g(x) = LM$, assuming $\lim_{x \to a} f = L$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g = M$ both exist.

- Step 1 — the algebraic trick. Add and subtract $f(x)M$ to split the difference into two controllable pieces: $fg - LM = f(g - M) + M(f - L)$. By the triangle inequality, $|fg - LM| \le |f|\,|g - M| + |M|\,|f - L|$.
- Step 2 — bound the stray factor $|f|$. Because $f \to L$, apply the definition with tolerance $1$: there is $\delta_0 > 0$ so that $0 < |x-a| < \delta_0 \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| < 1$, hence $|f(x)| \le |L| + 1$ (reverse triangle inequality). This turns the first term into $|f|\,|g-M| \le (|L|+1)\,|g - M|$.
- Step 3 — allocate the error budget $\varepsilon$ across the two terms. We aim to make each term $< \varepsilon/2$.
  - For the first term: since $g \to M$, pick $\delta_1$ so that $0 < |x-a| < \delta_1 \Rightarrow |g - M| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2(|L| + 1)}$; then $(|L|+1)|g-M| < \varepsilon/2$.
  - For the second term: since $f \to L$, pick $\delta_2$ so that $0 < |x-a| < \delta_2 \Rightarrow |f - L| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2(|M| + 1)}$; then $|M|\,|f-L| \le (|M|+1)|f-L| < \varepsilon/2$. (Using $|M|+1$ rather than $|M|$ safely covers the case $M = 0$.)
- Step 4 — combine. Let $\delta = \min(\delta_0, \delta_1, \delta_2) > 0$. For $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ all three bounds hold simultaneously, so $|fg - LM| \le (|L|+1)|g-M| + |M|\,|f-L| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2} = \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check on the split: the two allowances $\varepsilon/2$ sum to exactly $\varepsilon$, and taking the minimum of the three δ's is what makes every promise hold at the same time. ✓

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing 'let ε = …' before 'given ε>0'**: The proof must begin with an arbitrary ε handed to you; you may not choose ε.
- **Forgetting the punctured neighborhood**: The implication is only required when 0 < |x−a|, so the value f(a) is irrelevant.
- **Choosing δ that depends on x**: δ must be a fixed positive number once ε is fixed; it cannot vary with the x you later pick.
- **Assuming a largest possible δ exists and must be found**: Any positive δ that works is acceptable; often a very small one is easiest to write down.
- **Neglecting to handle the case when the expression for δ would be negative or zero**: Always take min( some convenient number, ε/K ) with K>0.

A function $f$ is continuous at a point $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. In other words, small changes in the input produce small changes in the output, and the function value at the point itself matches the limiting value.

Continuity is the most basic regularity condition in analysis; it is preserved by sums, products, quotients (denom ≠0), and compositions.

## The ε-δ Definition of Continuity at a Point

<!-- section: epsilon-delta-continuity -->

f is continuous at a (where a is in the domain) if:

For every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that if |x − a| < δ (now including x = a) then |f(x) − f(a)| < ε.

Compare with the limit definition: the only difference is that we no longer exclude x = a, and we require that the limit equals the actual function value.

**ELI5**

- At a continuous point, when you zoom in on the graph around (a, f(a)) it looks like a single unbroken curve without jumps or holes; the height at the exact point matches the heights nearby.
- The δ-ε promise now includes the center point itself, which is automatically satisfied when x=a (both sides zero).

**Worked Example:**

Prove $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous at an arbitrary point $a$ (i.e. $\lim_{x \to a} x^2 = a^2 = f(a)$).

- Step 1 — factor the target: $|x^2 - a^2| = |x - a|\,|x + a|$.
- Step 2 — control the nuisance factor $|x+a|$ by restricting $\delta \le 1$. If $|x - a| < 1$ then by the triangle inequality $|x + a| = |(x - a) + 2a| \le |x - a| + 2|a| < 1 + 2|a|$.
- Step 3 — choose $\delta = \min\!\left(1, \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2|a| + 1}\right) > 0$. Then $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ gives $|x^2 - a^2| = |x-a|\,|x+a| < \dfrac{\varepsilon}{2|a|+1} \cdot (2|a| + 1) = \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check at $a = 2$, $\varepsilon = 0.5$: $\delta = \min(1, 0.5/5) = 0.1$, so $|x-2| < 0.1$ means $1.9 < x < 2.1$, giving $3.61 < x^2 < 4.41$, i.e. $|x^2 - 4| < 0.41 < 0.5$. ✓ Notice the $\delta$ shrinks as $|a|$ grows — a foreshadowing of why $x^2$ is *not* uniformly continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

## Continuity on a Set and Global Properties

<!-- section: continuity-on-sets -->

f is continuous on a set S if it is continuous at every point of S.

A function continuous on a closed bounded interval [a,b] is bounded, attains its max and min (extreme value theorem), and has the intermediate value property.

Uniform continuity on S is a stronger uniform version of the δ-ε (δ independent of the point a ∈ S); see next lesson.

**ELI5**

- On a closed interval a continuous function cannot "run away to infinity" or have jumps; it must reach actual highest and lowest points.
- The graph can be drawn without lifting the pencil on a connected domain.

**Worked Example:**

Show the rational function $f(x) = \dfrac{x + 1}{x - 2}$ is continuous on its natural domain $\{x : x \neq 2\}$, and pin down where it fails.

- Step 1 — identify the building blocks. The numerator $x + 1$ and denominator $x - 2$ are polynomials, each continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$ (built from the identity map $x \mapsto x$, which is continuous with $\delta = \varepsilon$, and constants, via sums and products).
- Step 2 — apply the quotient rule for continuity. A quotient of continuous functions is continuous at every point where the denominator is nonzero. Here $x - 2 \neq 0 \iff x \neq 2$.
- Step 3 — conclude: $f$ is continuous at every $a \neq 2$. At $a = 2$ the function is undefined; moreover $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist (the numerator tends to $3 \neq 0$ while the denominator tends to $0$), so this is a non-removable, infinite discontinuity — a vertical asymptote.
- Sanity check at $a = 3$: $f(3) = \dfrac{4}{1} = 4$, and for $x$ near $3$, e.g. $f(3.1) = \dfrac{4.1}{1.1} \approx 3.73$, the values stay near $4$. ✓

More generally, every polynomial is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, and every rational function is continuous exactly on its natural domain (all points where the denominator is nonzero).

## Sequential Characterization and Discontinuities

<!-- section: sequential-discontinuities -->

$f$ is continuous at $a$ iff for every sequence $x_n \to a$ (with $x_n$ in domain) we have $f(x_n) \to f(a)$.

Discontinuities are classified as removable (limit exists but ≠ f(a) or undefined), jump (left and right limits exist but differ), or essential (oscillatory or infinite).

**ELI5**

- Sequential continuity: if you approach $a$ along any path of points, the function values must approach $f(a)$. If some sneaky sequence approaching $a$ makes $f(x_n)$ misbehave, then $f$ is discontinuous at $a$.
- Thomae's function is continuous at irrationals and discontinuous at rationals — a subtle example.

**Worked Example:**

Define $f(x) = 0$ for $x$ irrational, $f(p/q) = 1/q$ in lowest terms. At an irrational $a$, any sequence $x_n \to a$ must eventually have denominators $\to \infty$ (otherwise it would be eventually rational with bounded denom, hence isolated), so $f(x_n) \to 0 = f(a)$. At a rational the constant sequence $a, a, a, \ldots$ gives $f(x_n) = $ positive number not $\to 0$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking continuity at a point implies continuity in a neighborhood**: A function can be continuous only at a single point (or on ℚ).
- **Forgetting that the δ now includes the point a**: But because both sides become zero it does not change the algebra much.
- **Confusing uniform continuity with ordinary continuity**: On unbounded sets or sets with holes the two differ (e.g. f(x)=x² on ℝ is continuous but not uniformly continuous).
- **Assuming every discontinuity is a jump**: Essential discontinuities (sin(1/x) at 0) are worse.
- **Believing the sequential characterization only works for metric spaces**: It characterizes continuity in metric spaces; in general topological spaces one needs nets or filters.

A function f is uniformly continuous on a set S if for every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 (depending only on ε and f and S, not on any particular point) such that for all x, y ∈ S with |x − y| < δ we have |f(x) − f(y)| < ε.

The key word is "uniform": the same δ works everywhere in S simultaneously.

On compact sets (closed bounded intervals in ℝ) ordinary continuity implies uniform continuity (Heine-Cantor theorem). On unbounded domains this can fail.

## Definition and Comparison with Pointwise Continuity

<!-- section: definition-comparison -->

Pointwise continuity at each a ∈ S: for every a and every ε there is a δ_a (that may shrink as a moves or as ε shrinks).

Uniform continuity: one δ(ε) works for the whole set at once — for any pair of points in S that are < δ apart, their images are < ε apart.

If f is uniformly continuous on S then it is (pointwise) continuous on S, but the converse requires extra assumptions on S or on f.

**ELI5**

- Uniform continuity means your "zoom level" or tolerance δ is the same no matter where you are on the domain; you do not have to zoom in more carefully near some dangerous points.
- On a wiggly function on the whole real line you may need finer and finer control as |x| gets large.

**Worked Example:**

f(x) = x is uniformly continuous on ℝ: |f(x)−f(y)| = |x−y|, so δ = ε works everywhere.

f(x) = x² is continuous on ℝ but not uniformly: |x² − y²| = |x−y| |x+y|; when |x| and |y| are large, even a tiny |x−y| can make the product large. For ε=1 no single δ works for all pairs (take x large, y = x + δ/2).

## Heine-Cantor Theorem: Continuous on Compact ⇒ Uniformly Continuous

<!-- section: heine-cantor -->

Theorem: If f is continuous on a compact set K (in ℝ, a closed bounded interval), then f is uniformly continuous on K.

Proof sketch (one common version): suppose not. Then there is $\varepsilon > 0$ such that for every $n$ there are points $x_n, y_n$ in $K$ with $|x_n - y_n| < 1/n$ but $|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \geq \varepsilon$. By Bolzano-Weierstrass extract convergent subsequences; both subsequences converge to the same limit point $c \in K$ (because $|x_n - y_n| \to 0$). By continuity $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c)$ and $f(y_{n_k}) \to f(c)$, contradicting the separation by $\varepsilon$.

**ELI5**

- On a closed bounded interval the function cannot "get steeper and steeper forever" because it has to attain its max slope somewhere or the steepness would be controlled by compactness.
- The proof uses that any sequence of pairs that get closer must have a limit pair where continuity forces the images to get close.

**Worked Example:**

Show that $f(x) = x^2$, which fails to be uniformly continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, *is* uniformly continuous once we restrict it to the compact interval $[0, 3]$ — first by Heine-Cantor, then by an explicit δ.

- Step 1 — Heine-Cantor route. $[0,3]$ is closed and bounded, hence compact, and $x^2$ is continuous there. Therefore $x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,3]$. Done, with no computation.
- Step 2 — explicit δ (to see *why* compactness helps). For $x, y \in [0,3]$, $|x^2 - y^2| = |x - y|\,|x + y| \le |x - y| \cdot (3 + 3) = 6\,|x - y|$, because both $x, y \le 3$ bounds $|x+y| \le 6$. This bound holds *uniformly* — it does not depend on where in $[0,3]$ the points sit.
- Step 3 — given $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $\delta = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{6}$ (independent of $x, y$). Then $|x - y| < \delta$ implies $|x^2 - y^2| \le 6|x-y| < 6 \cdot \dfrac{\varepsilon}{6} = \varepsilon$.
- Sanity check: on the whole line the factor $|x+y|$ was unbounded, which is exactly what broke uniform continuity; capping the domain at $3$ caps that factor at $6$, so a single δ works everywhere. ✓ This is Heine-Cantor made concrete — compactness is what bounds the "steepness".

## Criterion via Cauchy Sequences; Extensions

<!-- section: cauchy-criterion-extension -->

A function f : S → ℝ (S metric) is uniformly continuous iff it maps Cauchy sequences in S to Cauchy sequences in ℝ.

This criterion is useful for showing that a uniformly continuous f on a dense subset (e.g. ℚ) extends uniquely to a continuous function on the closure (e.g. ℝ).

**ELI5**

- Uniform continuity preserves "terms getting close to each other"; ordinary continuity only preserves "terms approaching a fixed point."
- This is exactly why the completion process works nicely for uniformly continuous maps.

**Worked Example (the criterion detecting a failure):**

Use the Cauchy-preservation criterion to *disprove* uniform continuity of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ on $(0, 1]$.

- Step 1 — produce a Cauchy sequence in the domain. Let $x_n = \dfrac{1}{n}$. This is Cauchy in $(0,1]$ (it converges to $0$ in $\mathbb{R}$, and convergent sequences are Cauchy), even though its limit $0$ lies outside the domain.
- Step 2 — apply $f$: $f(x_n) = \dfrac{1}{1/n} = n$. The sequence $1, 2, 3, \dots$ is unbounded, hence not Cauchy.
- Step 3 — $f$ maps a Cauchy sequence to a non-Cauchy one, so by the criterion $f$ is **not** uniformly continuous on $(0,1]$. (Consistent with the fact that $(0,1]$ is not compact — it is missing the point $0$.)

**Worked Example (the criterion enabling an extension):**

Show $f(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]$ extends uniquely to a continuous function on $[0,1]$.

- Step 1 — verify uniform continuity on the dense set. For rational $x, y \in [0,1]$, $|x^2 - y^2| = |x-y|\,|x+y| \le 2|x - y|$, so $\delta = \varepsilon/2$ works uniformly.
- Step 2 — extend by continuity. For any real $c \in [0,1]$, take rationals $q_n \to c$; then $(q_n)$ is Cauchy, so $(f(q_n)) = (q_n^2)$ is Cauchy (uniform continuity), hence converges in the complete space $\mathbb{R}$. Define $\bar f(c) = \lim_n q_n^2$.
- Step 3 — well-defined and unique: any two rational sequences approaching $c$ interleave into one sequence approaching $c$, whose images converge, forcing the two limits to agree; and any continuous extension must take this value, so it is unique. The result is the familiar $\bar f(x) = x^2$ on $[0,1]$.
- Sanity check: $q_n = $ decimal truncations of $\sqrt{1/2}$ give $q_n^2 \to 1/2 = \bar f(\sqrt{1/2})$. ✓

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming continuity on ℝ implies uniform continuity**: x² and x sin x are counter-examples.
- **Forgetting that the δ must be independent of location**: Many students produce a δ that secretly depends on the point a when trying to prove uniform continuity.
- **Thinking uniform continuity implies bounded derivative**: A function can be uniformly continuous with unbounded derivative (e.g. √x on [0,1]).
- **Confusing with Lipschitz continuity**: Lipschitz ( |f(x)−f(y)| ≤ K |x−y| ) implies uniform continuity with δ = ε/K, but the converse is false (√x is uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz at 0).
- **Neglecting the domain**: A function may be uniformly continuous on [0,1] but its natural extension to ℝ may not be.

The intermediate value theorem (IVT) says that a continuous function on an interval takes on every value between f(a) and f(b). It is the reason we know that continuous curves cannot "jump over" horizontal lines without crossing them.

IVT is the theoretical foundation for the bisection method for root finding and for the existence of solutions to many equations.

## Statement and Proof Idea

<!-- section: statement-proof -->

Let f be continuous on the closed interval [a,b]. Let k be any number strictly between f(a) and f(b). Then there exists c ∈ (a,b) such that f(c) = k.

Without loss of generality assume $f(a) < k < f(b)$ (otherwise replace $f$ by $-f$). Let $S = \{x \in [a,b] : f(x) \le k\}$. $S$ is nonempty (it contains $a$, since $f(a) < k$) and bounded above (by $b$), so $c = \sup S$ exists by the least-upper-bound property of $\mathbb{R}$.

The finish shows $f(c) = k$ by ruling out both other cases using continuity: if $f(c) < k$, continuity keeps $f < k$ on a small interval to the right of $c$, giving points of $S$ larger than $c$ and contradicting that $c$ is an upper bound; if $f(c) > k$, continuity keeps $f > k$ on a small interval to the left of $c$, so those points are not in $S$ and a smaller upper bound than $c$ exists, contradicting that $c$ is the *least* upper bound. Hence $f(c) = k$, and since $f(a) \neq k \neq f(b)$ we have $c \in (a,b)$.

**ELI5**

- Imagine the graph of a continuous curve from (a, f(a)) to (b, f(b)). If you draw any horizontal line y = k between the starting and ending heights, the curve must cross that line at least once; it cannot teleport from below k to above k without passing through height k.
- The proof uses the "last point where the function is still ≤ k"; continuity forces it to be exactly k.

**Worked Example:**

Use IVT to prove that a positive real square root of $2$ exists.

- Step 1 — pick a continuous function and interval. Let $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, continuous on $[1, 2]$ (it is a polynomial).
- Step 2 — check the endpoint values straddle the target $k = 0$: $f(1) = 1 - 2 = -1 < 0$ and $f(2) = 4 - 2 = 2 > 0$. So $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$.
- Step 3 — apply IVT with $k = 0$: there exists $c \in (1, 2)$ with $f(c) = 0$, i.e. $c^2 = 2$. This $c$ is a positive square root of $2$.
- Sanity check: $1.4^2 = 1.96 < 2$ and $1.5^2 = 2.25 > 2$, so the root sits in $(1.4, 1.5)$, consistent with $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$. ✓ Note IVT gives *existence* only; it does not hand you the decimal expansion.

## Consequences and Applications

<!-- section: consequences-applications -->

- Every odd-degree polynomial with real coefficients has at least one real root (because it goes to +∞ in one direction and −∞ in the other, so crosses zero).
- The bisection method: repeatedly halve an interval where the function changes sign; the nested intervals converge to a root (when the function is continuous).
- Fixed-point theorems in one dimension: if f : [a,b] → [a,b] is continuous then f has a fixed point (apply IVT to g(x) = f(x) − x).

**ELI5**

- A polynomial like x^5 − x + 1 must cross the x-axis because as x → +∞ it is positive and as x → −∞ it is negative.
- Bisection is a practical algorithm that only needs sign changes and continuity; it is guaranteed to work.

**Worked Example:**

Locate a root of $f(x) = x^3 + x - 1$ by bisection.

- Step 1 — bracket a sign change. $f(0) = 0 + 0 - 1 = -1 < 0$ and $f(1) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 > 0$. Since $f$ is continuous and changes sign, IVT guarantees a root in $(0, 1)$.
- Step 2 — first bisection at the midpoint $0.5$: $f(0.5) = 0.125 + 0.5 - 1 = -0.375 < 0$. The sign change is now between $0.5$ and $1$, so the root lies in $(0.5, 1)$.
- Step 3 — second bisection at $0.75$: $f(0.75) = 0.421875 + 0.75 - 1 = 0.171875 > 0$. The root now lies in $(0.5, 0.75)$.
- Step 4 — third bisection at $0.625$: $f(0.625) \approx 0.244 + 0.625 - 1 = -0.131 < 0$. The root lies in $(0.625, 0.75)$.
- Sanity check: each step halves the interval that must contain the root, so the length after $n$ steps is $2^{-n}$; the nested intervals shrink to the unique real root $\approx 0.6823$. ✓ Bisection needs only sign changes and continuity — no derivative.

## Why Continuity Is Essential (Counter-examples)

<!-- section: necessity-continuity -->

If continuity is dropped, IVT can fail dramatically. Example: f(x) = 0 for x < 0, f(x) = 1 for x ≥ 0 on [−1,1]. f(−1)=0, f(1)=1, but no c with f(c) = 1/2.

Another classic is the Dirichlet function ($0$ on rationals, $1$ on irrationals): on $[0,1]$ its values include both $0$ and $1$, yet its image is only the two-point set $\{0, 1\}$ — it takes no value strictly between. It is discontinuous at every point, which is exactly why it can skip intermediate values.

**ELI5**

- A jump discontinuity lets the graph leap from below k to above k without ever being exactly k.
- A function that is wildly discontinuous can miss values even if it starts below and ends above.

**Worked Example:**

Show a discontinuous function can start below a value $k$ and end above it yet never equal $k$. Define on $[-1, 1]$:
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} -1, & -1 \le x < 0, \\ 1, & 0 \le x \le 1. \end{cases}$$

- Step 1 — endpoint values straddle $k = 0$: $f(-1) = -1 < 0 < 1 = f(1)$.
- Step 2 — examine the image. For every $x$, $f(x)$ is either $-1$ or $1$; the image is the two-point set $\{-1, 1\}$.
- Step 3 — conclude IVT fails: there is **no** $c$ with $f(c) = 0$ (indeed no $c$ with $f(c)$ equal to anything strictly between $-1$ and $1$). The single hypothesis that breaks is continuity — $f$ has a jump at $0$, where the left limit $-1$ and right limit $1$ disagree.
- Sanity check: pick any $c$; if $c < 0$ then $f(c) = -1 \neq 0$, and if $c \ge 0$ then $f(c) = 1 \neq 0$. ✓ No value of $c$ hits $0$.

## Common Mistakes

- **Stating IVT without the continuity hypothesis**: The theorem is false without continuity (step functions).
- **Thinking the c is unique**: There may be many crossings (oscillating functions).
- **Applying IVT on open intervals or to discontinuous functions**: The closed interval and continuity are both essential for the standard statement.
- **Confusing IVT with the mean value theorem**: IVT is about values of the function; MVT is about values of the derivative.
- **Believing IVT gives a constructive method**: The pure existence proof via sup does not tell you how to compute c; bisection does, but slowly.

The extreme value theorem (EVT) asserts that a continuous real-valued function on a closed bounded interval attains both a maximum and a minimum value on that interval.

Together with the intermediate value theorem, EVT is one of the two pillars that make continuous functions on compact intervals behave like "nice" functions from calculus.

## Statement

<!-- section: statement -->

If f is continuous on the closed bounded interval [a,b], then there exist points c, d ∈ [a,b] such that f(c) ≤ f(x) ≤ f(d) for all x ∈ [a,b].

In other words, the image f([a,b]) is a closed bounded interval [m, M] where m = min f, M = max f.

**ELI5**

- On a closed interval a continuous function cannot "escape to infinity" at the ends or have jumps; it must actually reach a highest point and a lowest point somewhere on the interval (possibly at the endpoints).
- Think of the graph as a continuous curve from x=a to x=b; it must have a highest and lowest y-value attained.

**Worked Example:**

Find and confirm the extrema of $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-1, 1]$, whose existence EVT guarantees since $f$ is continuous and $[-1,1]$ is closed and bounded.

- Step 1 — minimum. $x^2 \ge 0$ for all $x$, with equality at $x = 0 \in [-1,1]$. So the minimum value is $0$, attained at $x = 0$ (an interior point).
- Step 2 — maximum. On $[-1,1]$ we have $|x| \le 1$, so $x^2 \le 1$, with equality at $x = \pm 1$. So the maximum value is $1$, attained at both endpoints $x = -1$ and $x = 1$.
- Sanity check: the image is $f([-1,1]) = [0, 1]$, a closed bounded interval $[m, M] = [0, 1]$ that contains both its min and max — exactly what EVT predicts. ✓ Note the max here lands on the endpoints, not at an interior critical point.

## Proof Using Completeness

<!-- section: proof-completeness -->

One proof: the image $f([a,b])$ is bounded (otherwise by IVT or directly one constructs a sequence $x_n$ where $|f(x_n)| > n$, which by Bolzano-Weierstrass has a convergent subsequence; continuity would imply $f$ bounded near the limit point, contradiction).

Let $M = \sup f([a,b])$ (exists by LUB). There is a sequence $y_n$ in the image with $y_n \to M$. Write $y_n = f(x_n)$. The $x_n$ have a convergent subsequence $x_{n_k} \to c \in [a,b]$ by compactness. By continuity $f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c)$, hence $f(c) = M$, so the sup is attained.

The argument for the inf is identical (or apply to −f).

**ELI5**

- Boundedness comes from compactness preventing sequences from running away while f grows.
- The sup of the values is approached by some sequence of values $f(x_n)$; the $x_n$ must cluster somewhere $c$; continuity carries the values $f(x_n)$ to $f(c)$, forcing $f(c)$ to be the sup.

**Worked Example:**

To see why closed is necessary: f(x) = 1/x on (0,1] is continuous but has no maximum (approaches +∞ as x→0+) and no minimum on the open end (inf=0 not attained).

## Why Both Hypotheses Are Necessary

<!-- section: necessity-hypotheses -->

EVT needs **both** continuity **and** a compact (closed and bounded) domain. Drop either one and attainment can fail.

- Continuity is needed. Define $f$ on the compact interval $[0,1]$ by $f(x) = x$ for $0 \le x < 1$ and $f(1) = 0$. This is bounded with $\sup_{[0,1]} f = 1$, but the supremum is never attained: $f(x) = x < 1$ for $x < 1$, and $f(1) = 0$. The domain is compact, so the only broken hypothesis is continuity (there is a jump at $1$).
- Closedness is needed. On the half-open interval $(0,1]$, the continuous function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ is unbounded above ($1/x \to +\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$), so it has no maximum. It does have a maximum-free "escape" precisely because the endpoint $0$ is missing.
- Boundedness is needed. On the unbounded interval $[0, \infty)$, the continuous function $f(x) = x$ has no maximum, since it grows without bound.

**ELI5**

- A jump lets the function miss values or fail to reach a supposed max in the middle.
- On an open or unbounded interval the function can "run away" toward the missing end or to infinity without ever reaching a max or min.

**Worked Example:**

Show $f(x) = \tan x$ on the open interval $\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ has neither a maximum nor a minimum, and identify which EVT hypothesis is missing.

- Step 1 — continuity holds: $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ is continuous wherever $\cos x \neq 0$, and $\cos x \neq 0$ throughout the open interval.
- Step 2 — the domain is bounded but **not closed** (the endpoints $\pm\pi/2$ are excluded). So the compactness hypothesis fails.
- Step 3 — attainment fails: as $x \to (\pi/2)^-$, $\cos x \to 0^+$ and $\sin x \to 1$, so $\tan x \to +\infty$ (no maximum); symmetrically $\tan x \to -\infty$ as $x \to (-\pi/2)^+$ (no minimum).
- Sanity check: $\tan(1.5) \approx 14.1$ while $\tan(1.57) \approx 1256$, confirming the values run off to $+\infty$ near the open endpoint. ✓ Closing the interval is impossible here because $\tan$ cannot even be continuously defined at $\pm\pi/2$.

## Relation to Compactness

<!-- section: compactness-relation -->

In general metric spaces (or topological spaces) the extreme value theorem generalizes to: a continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains its max and min.

In ℝ^n this is equivalent (Heine-Borel) to closed and bounded.

This is one of the reasons compact sets are so important in analysis.

**ELI5**

- Compact = "small and complete" in a precise sense: every sequence has a convergent subsequence (Bolzano-Weierstrass) and the set contains its limit points.
- Continuous images of compact sets are compact; for subsets of ℝ compact = closed bounded interval, whose only compact subsets are themselves closed bounded.

**Worked Example:**

Apply the compact-set form of EVT to the height function on the unit circle $S^1 = \{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$.

- Step 1 — the domain is compact. $S^1$ is closed (it is the level set $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ of a continuous function) and bounded (it sits inside the disk of radius $1$); by Heine-Borel, closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^2$ means compact.
- Step 2 — the function is continuous. The height $h(x, y) = y$ is a coordinate projection, which is continuous.
- Step 3 — apply EVT: a continuous real-valued function on a compact set attains its max and min. So $h$ attains a maximum and minimum on $S^1$.
- Sanity check: parametrize $S^1$ by $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$; then $h = \sin\theta$ attains its maximum $1$ at the top point $(0, 1)$ and its minimum $-1$ at the bottom $(0, -1)$. ✓ These attained values match $\max = 1$, $\min = -1$ as promised.

## Common Mistakes

- **Stating EVT on open intervals**: Counter-examples are easy (x on (0,1) has no max).
- **Forgetting that the max/min may be attained at endpoints**: Students sometimes look only for critical points inside (a,b).
- **Confusing attainment of extrema with existence of critical points**: EVT guarantees attainment; Fermat's theorem (if interior extremum and differentiable then f'=0) gives a way to locate them, but requires differentiability.
- **Thinking a bounded continuous function on ℝ must attain its max/min**: It need not, because ℝ is not compact. The function $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{1 + |x|}$ is continuous and bounded ($|f(x)| < 1$ for all $x$) with $\sup f = 1$ and $\inf f = -1$, yet it never reaches $\pm 1$ (those are only approached as $x \to \pm\infty$).
- **Applying EVT to discontinuous functions on compact sets**: The image need not be closed or bounded in the way expected (though boundedness may still hold or not).
