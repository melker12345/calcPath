# Ordinals & Cardinals

Counting does two different jobs, and the natural numbers do both so quietly that the difference is easy to miss. When you say a room holds seven chairs you are reporting a *size*. When you say a runner finished seventh you are reporting a *position in an order*. For finite collections the two notions march in step: a set of size seven can be arranged in an order, and however you arrange it the last element is the seventh. Cantor's discovery, and the subject of this chapter, is that beyond the finite the two notions come apart completely, and each generalises into its own infinite number system.

The order-side generalisation gives the **ordinals**. An ordinal is a canonical well-ordered set, a ruler against which any other well-ordering can be laid. The ordinals begin $0, 1, 2, \dots$, then keep going: $\omega$, the order type of all the naturals laid end to end, then $\omega + 1$, $\omega + 2$, and eventually $\omega \cdot 2$, $\omega^2$, $\omega^\omega$, and further than any notation you care to invent. The arithmetic of ordinals is genuinely strange — addition is associative but not commutative, and $1 + \omega = \omega$ while $\omega + 1 \neq \omega$ — and the strangeness is not a defect. It is the honest record of what happens when you append one item to the front of an infinite queue rather than to the back.

The size-side generalisation gives the **cardinals**. Two sets have the same cardinal exactly when some bijection matches them element for element, and this single criterion, applied without flinching to infinite sets, forces conclusions that took mathematics a generation to digest: there are exactly as many rationals as naturals, strictly more reals than naturals, and no largest cardinal at all. Cardinal arithmetic then collapses almost to triviality — $\kappa + \lambda = \kappa \cdot \lambda = \max(\kappa, \lambda)$ for infinite cardinals — with exactly one exception, exponentiation, and the whole difficulty of modern set theory is concentrated in that exception. The continuum hypothesis, which asks whether $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$, is the first and most famous question about it, and the answer is not that we do not know: the answer is that the axioms do not decide.

The chapter closes with the proof technique the ordinals exist to support. Transfinite induction and transfinite recursion extend ordinary induction past $\omega$, and they do it by adding a genuinely new case — the **limit step**, where there is no previous stage to inherit from and the construction must assemble itself from everything below. That step is what makes transfinite induction more than bookkeeping, and it is where every failed attempt at a transfinite argument fails.

Throughout, $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ includes zero, **ZF** is Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, **AC** is the axiom of choice, and **ZFC** is ZF with AC. Where a result needs choice, it is said so in the statement rather than smuggled in.

## Ordinals as transitive sets

<!-- section: definition-ordinals -->

The naive description of an ordinal is "an order type of a well-ordered set". That is the right idea and the wrong definition, because an order type so described is a proper class of isomorphic orders, not a set. Von Neumann's fix is to pick one representative from each class by a rule so rigid that the representative is forced: an ordinal *is* the set of all smaller ordinals. Zero is the empty set, one is $\{0\}$, two is $\{0, 1\}$, and $n$ is the set of its $n$ predecessors. The definition below is the self-contained version of that idea, with no circularity.

:::definition[Transitive set]{#transitive-set}
A set $A$ is **transitive** if every element of $A$ is also a subset of $A$; equivalently, if $x \in y$ and $y \in A$ together imply $x \in A$.
:::

:::definition[Von Neumann ordinal]{#ordinal}
An **ordinal** is a transitive set $\alpha$ that is strictly well-ordered by the membership relation $\in$. That is, $\in$ restricted to $\alpha$ is irreflexive, transitive as a relation, satisfies trichotomy on $\alpha$, and every nonempty subset of $\alpha$ has an $\in$-least element.

We write $\alpha < \beta$ as a synonym for $\alpha \in \beta$, and $\alpha \le \beta$ for $\alpha \in \beta$ or $\alpha = \beta$. Lower-case Greek letters $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \lambda$ always denote ordinals.
:::

:::notation
"Transitive" is being used in two senses one line apart and they are not the same. A transitive *set* satisfies $x \in y \in A \Rightarrow x \in A$. A transitive *relation* satisfies $x R y R z \Rightarrow x R z$. An ordinal is required to be both: a transitive set, and one on which $\in$ happens to be a transitive relation. The coincidence of names is unfortunate and universal.
:::

:::intuition
Read $\alpha$ as "the set of stages already completed". The ordinal $5 = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ literally *is* its own list of predecessors, so "how far along are you?" and "what have you passed?" have the same answer. Transitivity is the statement that the list has no holes: if stage $2$ is on the list and stage $1$ came before stage $2$, then stage $1$ is on the list too. Under this convention $\alpha < \beta$, $\alpha \in \beta$ and $\alpha \subsetneq \beta$ all say the same thing, which is why ordinal proofs feel so economical.
:::

Under the axiom of foundation every set is automatically $\in$-irreflexive and every nonempty class has an $\in$-minimal element, so in ZF the definition can be shortened to "a transitive set totally ordered by $\in$". We keep the long form because it makes the arguments below self-contained: nothing in this section uses foundation.

:::theorem[Elements of ordinals are ordinals]{#elements-are-ordinals}
If $\alpha$ is an ordinal and $\beta \in \alpha$, then $\beta$ is an ordinal, and $\beta = \{\gamma \in \alpha : \gamma \in \beta\}$ is exactly the set of elements of $\alpha$ below $\beta$.
:::

:::proof
Since $\alpha$ is transitive and $\beta \in \alpha$, we have $\beta \subseteq \alpha$. So $\in$ restricted to $\beta$ inherits irreflexivity, transitivity as a relation, trichotomy and the least-element property from $\in$ restricted to $\alpha$; a nonempty subset of $\beta$ is a nonempty subset of $\alpha$ and therefore has an $\in$-least element. Hence $\beta$ is well-ordered by $\in$.

It remains to show $\beta$ is a transitive set. Let $x \in y \in \beta$. Since $\beta \subseteq \alpha$ we get $y \in \alpha$, and since $\alpha$ is transitive, $y \subseteq \alpha$, so $x \in \alpha$. Now $x$, $y$ and $\beta$ all lie in $\alpha$, where $\in$ is a transitive relation, and $x \in y \in \beta$ gives $x \in \beta$. Thus every element of $\beta$ is a subset of $\beta$, and $\beta$ is an ordinal.

Finally, $\beta \subseteq \alpha$ means every element of $\beta$ is an element of $\alpha$ lying below $\beta$, and conversely an element of $\alpha$ lying below $\beta$ is by definition a member of $\beta$. So $\beta$ is precisely the initial segment of $\alpha$ determined by $\beta$.
:::

That last sentence is the sentence to remember: **an ordinal is its own set of predecessors, and each of its elements is the initial segment it cuts out.** Every structural fact below is squeezed out of it.

:::proposition[Ordinals are closed downward under subsets]{#ordinal-subset}
If $\alpha$ is an ordinal and $\beta \subseteq \alpha$ is a transitive subset with $\beta \neq \alpha$, then $\beta \in \alpha$.
:::

:::proof
Since $\beta \subsetneq \alpha$, the set $\alpha \setminus \beta$ is nonempty, so it has an $\in$-least element $\gamma$. We claim $\beta = \gamma$.

If $x \in \gamma$ then $x \in \alpha$ by transitivity of $\alpha$, and $x \notin \alpha \setminus \beta$ by minimality of $\gamma$, so $x \in \beta$. Hence $\gamma \subseteq \beta$.

Conversely let $x \in \beta$. Then $x, \gamma \in \alpha$, so trichotomy gives $x \in \gamma$, $x = \gamma$, or $\gamma \in x$. The second is impossible since $\gamma \notin \beta$. If $\gamma \in x$ then, $\beta$ being transitive and $x \in \beta$, we would get $\gamma \in \beta$, again impossible. So $x \in \gamma$, giving $\beta \subseteq \gamma$.

Therefore $\beta = \gamma \in \alpha$.
:::

:::example[Checking the definition on a small set]
Verify directly that $3 = \{0, 1, 2\} = \{\varnothing, \{\varnothing\}, \{\varnothing, \{\varnothing\}\}\}$ is an ordinal, and explain why $\{0, 2\}$ is not.
:::

:::solution
1. Transitivity of $3$: its elements are $0$, $1 = \{0\}$ and $2 = \{0,1\}$, and each of these is a subset of $\{0,1,2\}$ — the first vacuously, the second because $0 \in 3$, the third because $0, 1 \in 3$.
2. Well-ordering by $\in$: the membership relations among the three elements are exactly $0 \in 1$, $0 \in 2$, $1 \in 2$, which is the strict linear order $0 < 1 < 2$. Every nonempty subset of a three-element linear order has a least element, so $\in$ well-orders $3$.
3. The set $\{0, 2\}$ fails transitivity: $1 \in 2 \in \{0,2\}$ but $1 \notin \{0,2\}$. It has a hole, so it is not an initial segment and not an ordinal — even though $\in$ does linearly order its two elements.
:::

:::pitfall
Being well-ordered by $\in$ is not enough on its own, and being transitive is not enough on its own. $\{0,2\}$ is well-ordered by $\in$ but not transitive. The set $\{\varnothing, \{\{\varnothing\}\}\}$ is not transitive either. Both halves of the definition do work.
:::

## Order types, comparability and Burali-Forti

<!-- section: order-types -->

The point of ordinals is to measure well-orderings, so we need the language of order-isomorphism and then two facts: that ordinals are themselves well-ordered by $<$, and that every well-ordering is matched by exactly one ordinal. The section ends with the result that stopped set theory from being naive — that the ordinals are too numerous to form a set at all.

:::definition[Well-order and order-isomorphism]{#well-order}
A **well-order** on a set $X$ is a strict linear order $<$ on $X$ such that every nonempty subset of $X$ has a least element. An **order-isomorphism** between well-ordered sets $(X, <_X)$ and $(Y, <_Y)$ is a bijection $f : X \to Y$ with $a <_X b \iff f(a) <_Y f(b)$. We write $X \cong Y$ when one exists and call the common **order type** what they share.

For $a \in X$, the **initial segment** $X_{<a} = \{x \in X : x <_X a\}$ is the set of elements strictly below $a$.
:::

:::lemma[Rigidity of well-orders]{#rigidity}
Let $(X, <)$ be a well-ordered set and $f : X \to X$ an order-preserving injection. Then $f(a) \ge a$ for every $a \in X$. Consequently no well-ordered set is order-isomorphic to a proper initial segment of itself, and any two order-isomorphic well-orders are isomorphic by exactly one isomorphism.
:::

:::proof
Suppose the set $S = \{a \in X : f(a) < a\}$ is nonempty and let $a$ be its least element. Then $f(a) < a$, and applying the order-preserving $f$ to this inequality gives $f(f(a)) < f(a)$. So $f(a) \in S$ and $f(a) < a$, contradicting the minimality of $a$. Hence $S = \varnothing$ and $f(a) \ge a$ always.

If $g : X \to X_{<b}$ were an isomorphism onto a proper initial segment, then viewing $g$ as an order-preserving injection $X \to X$ gives $g(b) \ge b$, whereas $g(b) \in X_{<b}$ forces $g(b) < b$ — a contradiction.

For uniqueness, if $f, g : X \to Y$ are both isomorphisms then $g^{-1} \circ f : X \to X$ is an order-preserving bijection, so by the first part both it and its inverse move nothing downward, forcing $g^{-1}(f(a)) = a$ for all $a$, i.e. $f = g$.
:::

The uniqueness clause is worth pausing on, because it is what makes "the order type" a well-defined object rather than a choice. A well-ordered set has no symmetries at all: the only order-automorphism is the identity. Contrast a group or a vector space, where automorphisms are abundant.

:::theorem[Ordinals are linearly ordered by membership]{#ordinal-trichotomy}
For any ordinals $\alpha$ and $\beta$, exactly one of $\alpha \in \beta$, $\alpha = \beta$, $\beta \in \alpha$ holds. Moreover $\alpha \le \beta$ if and only if $\alpha \subseteq \beta$.
:::

:::proof
First, $\gamma = \alpha \cap \beta$ is a transitive set: if $x \in y \in \gamma$ then $y$ lies in both $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and transitivity of each gives $x \in \alpha$ and $x \in \beta$. Also $\gamma \subseteq \alpha$, so $\in$ well-orders $\gamma$; hence $\gamma$ is an ordinal.

By *Ordinals are closed downward under subsets*, either $\gamma = \alpha$ or $\gamma \in \alpha$, and likewise either $\gamma = \beta$ or $\gamma \in \beta$. If $\gamma \in \alpha$ and $\gamma \in \beta$ both held, then $\gamma \in \alpha \cap \beta = \gamma$, contradicting irreflexivity of $\in$ on the ordinal $\gamma \cup \{\gamma\}$'s members — concretely, $\gamma \in \gamma$ is impossible because $\in$ is irreflexive on any ordinal containing $\gamma$. So at least one of $\gamma = \alpha$, $\gamma = \beta$ holds.

If $\gamma = \alpha = \beta$ we get $\alpha = \beta$. If $\gamma = \alpha \neq \beta$ then $\gamma \in \beta$, i.e. $\alpha \in \beta$. Symmetrically if $\gamma = \beta \neq \alpha$ then $\beta \in \alpha$. That is trichotomy; the three cases exclude one another because $\alpha \in \beta \in \alpha$ would give $\alpha \in \alpha$ by transitivity, again impossible.

For the last claim: if $\alpha \in \beta$ then $\alpha \subseteq \beta$ by transitivity of $\beta$, and $\alpha = \beta$ trivially gives $\alpha \subseteq \beta$. Conversely if $\alpha \subseteq \beta$ and $\alpha \neq \beta$, then *Ordinals are closed downward under subsets* gives $\alpha \in \beta$.
:::

:::theorem[Every set of ordinals is well-ordered]{#ordinals-well-ordered}
Let $A$ be a nonempty set of ordinals. Then $A$ has a $<$-least element, namely $\bigcap A$. Consequently $<$ well-orders any set of ordinals, and $\sup A = \bigcup A$ is an ordinal, the least ordinal $\ge$ every element of $A$.
:::

:::proof
Pick any $\alpha \in A$. If $\alpha$ is already least we are done. Otherwise $\alpha \cap A$ is a nonempty subset of the ordinal $\alpha$, so it has an $\in$-least element $\beta$. For any $\delta \in A$, trichotomy gives $\delta \in \beta$, $\delta = \beta$ or $\beta \in \delta$; the first would put $\delta \in \beta \in \alpha$, hence $\delta \in \alpha \cap A$ below $\beta$, contradicting minimality. So $\beta \le \delta$ for all $\delta \in A$, and $\beta$ is least. Linearity comes from *Ordinals are linearly ordered by membership*, so $<$ is a well-order on $A$.

For the supremum, put $\sigma = \bigcup A$. It is transitive: $x \in y \in \sigma$ means $y \in \alpha$ for some $\alpha \in A$, and transitivity of $\alpha$ gives $x \in \alpha \subseteq \sigma$. Its elements are ordinals by *Elements of ordinals are ordinals*, and a set of ordinals is linearly ordered and well-ordered by the previous paragraph, so $\sigma$ is an ordinal. Each $\alpha \in A$ satisfies $\alpha \subseteq \sigma$, so $\alpha \le \sigma$; and if $\tau \ge \alpha$ for all $\alpha \in A$ then $\alpha \subseteq \tau$ for all $\alpha$, so $\sigma \subseteq \tau$ and $\sigma \le \tau$.
:::

We can now state the result that killed the naive theory. Cantor knew it by 1897; Burali-Forti published it that year, and it is the earliest of the set-theoretic paradoxes.

:::theorem[Burali-Forti paradox]{#burali-forti}
There is no set of all ordinals. The ordinals form a proper class $\mathrm{Ord}$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose toward a contradiction that $\Omega = \{\alpha : \alpha \text{ is an ordinal}\}$ is a set.

$\Omega$ is transitive: if $\beta \in \alpha \in \Omega$ then $\beta$ is an ordinal by *Elements of ordinals are ordinals*, so $\beta \in \Omega$.

$\Omega$ is well-ordered by $\in$: it is linearly ordered by *Ordinals are linearly ordered by membership*, and every nonempty subset has a least element by *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered*.

So $\Omega$ meets both clauses of the definition of an ordinal, and therefore $\Omega$ is itself an ordinal. But every ordinal is a member of $\Omega$, so $\Omega \in \Omega$ — and $\in$ is irreflexive on the ordinal $\Omega$, so $\Omega \notin \Omega$. This is a flat contradiction, and the only assumption made was that $\Omega$ is a set.
:::

:::remark
The paradox is not a defect in the ordinals; it is information about the universe of sets. The same shape of argument shows there is no set of all sets and no largest cardinal. Modern set theory absorbs it by distinguishing *sets* from *classes*: $\mathrm{Ord}$ is a definable collection which is not a set, and axiom schemes like separation and replacement are exactly the tools that stop such collections from being formed. Read positively, Burali-Forti says the ordinals never run out — for any *set* $A$ of ordinals, $\sup A + 1$ is an ordinal outside $A$.
:::

:::theorem[Representation of well-orders]{#representation}
Every well-ordered set $(X, <)$ is order-isomorphic to a unique ordinal, called its **order type** and written $\mathrm{ot}(X)$.
:::

:::proof
*Uniqueness* is immediate: if $\alpha \cong X \cong \beta$ then $\alpha \cong \beta$, and if $\alpha \neq \beta$ then by trichotomy one is an element, hence a proper initial segment, of the other, which *Rigidity of well-orders* forbids.

*Existence* (this half uses the replacement scheme, and nothing else beyond ZF). Call $a \in X$ *good* if the initial segment $X_{<a}$ is isomorphic to some ordinal; by uniqueness that ordinal is unique, call it $F(a)$. We show every $a$ is good by induction along the well-order: suppose every $b < a$ is good. Then $F$ is defined on $X_{<a}$, and by replacement $\{F(b) : b < a\}$ is a *set* of ordinals; call it $S$. Each $F(b)$ is an initial segment of each $F(b')$ for $b < b'$, so $S$ is transitive and hence an ordinal $\alpha$, and $b \mapsto F(b)$ is an order-isomorphism $X_{<a} \to \alpha$. So $a$ is good with $F(a) = \alpha$. By transfinite induction on the well-order $X$ — which is legitimate here because it is ordinary induction along a well-order, proved in *Transfinite induction on a well-ordered set* below and independent of this theorem — every $a \in X$ is good.

Now apply replacement once more to the whole of $X$: $\{F(a) : a \in X\}$ is a set of ordinals, transitive by the same argument, hence an ordinal $\alpha$, and $F : X \to \alpha$ is an order-isomorphism.
:::

:::note
The existence half is where replacement earns its keep. Zermelo's original axioms, without replacement, cannot prove that $\omega + \omega$ exists as a set, let alone that every well-order has an ordinal type. Fraenkel and Skolem added replacement in the early 1920s precisely to make constructions of this length legitimate.
:::

:::example[Order types of small explicit orders]
Give the order type of (a) the set $\{1 - 1/n : n \ge 1\}$ under the usual order on $\mathbb{R}$, (b) that same set together with the point $1$, and (c) $\{-1/n : n \ge 1\} \cup \{n : n \ge 1\}$.
:::

:::solution
1. In (a) the points $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac23, \tfrac34, \dots$ increase and are indexed by $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ in order, so the map $n \mapsto 1 - 1/n$ is an order-isomorphism from $\mathbb{N}$, and the order type is $\omega$.
2. In (b) we have the same $\omega$-sequence with one extra point sitting above all of them. That is $\omega$ followed by one more step, so the order type is $\omega + 1$.
3. In (c) the first block $-1, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac13, \dots$ has type $\omega$, every element of it is below every element of the second block $1, 2, 3, \dots$, which also has type $\omega$. Two copies of $\omega$ end to end give order type $\omega + \omega = \omega \cdot 2$.
:::

## Successors, limits, and the first infinite ordinal

<!-- section: successor-limit -->

Every ordinal is reached in exactly one of three ways: it is the starting point, it is one step past something, or it is the accumulation of everything below it with no single step immediately before. That trichotomy organises every transfinite construction in mathematics, because a recursion has to be told what to do in each of the three cases.

:::definition[Successor ordinal]{#successor}
For an ordinal $\alpha$, the **successor** is $\alpha + 1 = \alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$, also written $S(\alpha)$. An ordinal is a **successor ordinal** if it equals $\beta + 1$ for some ordinal $\beta$.
:::

:::proposition[The successor is the next ordinal]{#successor-least}
For every ordinal $\alpha$, the set $\alpha + 1$ is an ordinal, $\alpha < \alpha + 1$, and there is no ordinal $\gamma$ with $\alpha < \gamma < \alpha + 1$.
:::

:::proof
Transitivity: an element of $\alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$ is either an element of $\alpha$, hence a subset of $\alpha \subseteq \alpha+1$, or is $\alpha$ itself, which is a subset of $\alpha + 1$. Well-ordering: $\alpha + 1$ is a set of ordinals, so *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered* applies. Thus $\alpha + 1$ is an ordinal, and $\alpha \in \alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$ gives $\alpha < \alpha + 1$.

If $\alpha < \gamma < \alpha + 1$ then $\gamma \in \alpha \cup \{\alpha\}$, so $\gamma \in \alpha$ or $\gamma = \alpha$; either way $\gamma \le \alpha$, contradicting $\alpha < \gamma$.
:::

:::definition[Limit ordinal]{#limit-ordinal}
An ordinal $\lambda$ is a **limit ordinal** if $\lambda \neq 0$ and $\lambda$ is not a successor. Equivalently, $\lambda \neq 0$ and $\lambda = \sup\{\beta : \beta < \lambda\} = \bigcup \lambda$.
:::

:::theorem[Classification of ordinals]{#classification}
Every ordinal $\alpha$ is exactly one of: $0$; a successor $\beta + 1$; or a limit. Moreover $\alpha$ is a successor if and only if $\bigcup \alpha \neq \alpha$, and in that case $\alpha = (\bigcup \alpha) + 1$.
:::

:::proof
The three cases are mutually exclusive by definition, and exhaustive because "limit" is defined as the leftover case. For the criterion, $\bigcup \alpha = \sup\{\beta : \beta < \alpha\}$ by *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered*.

If $\alpha = \beta + 1$ then $\beta < \alpha$ and every $\gamma < \alpha$ satisfies $\gamma \le \beta$, so the supremum is $\beta \neq \alpha$, and $(\bigcup\alpha) + 1 = \beta + 1 = \alpha$.

Conversely suppose $\sigma = \bigcup\alpha \neq \alpha$. Every $\gamma < \alpha$ has $\gamma \le \sigma$, and $\sigma \subseteq \alpha$ gives $\sigma \le \alpha$; with $\sigma \neq \alpha$ this forces $\sigma < \alpha$, so $\sigma$ is the largest element of $\alpha$ and $\alpha = \sigma \cup \{\sigma\} = \sigma + 1$ is a successor. (If $\alpha = 0$ then $\bigcup \alpha = \varnothing = \alpha$, so the criterion correctly excludes $0$.)
:::

:::definition[The ordinal omega]{#omega}
$\omega$ is the least ordinal that is neither $0$ nor a successor: the set of all finite ordinals, $\omega = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\} = \mathbb{N}$. Its existence is the axiom of infinity.
:::

:::proposition[Omega is the least limit ordinal]{#omega-limit}
$\omega$ is a limit ordinal, and every ordinal $\alpha < \omega$ is either $0$ or a successor.
:::

:::proof
Every element of $\omega$ is a finite ordinal $n$, and $n + 1$ is again finite, so $\omega$ has no largest element and $\bigcup \omega = \omega$; with $\omega \neq 0$, *Classification of ordinals* makes $\omega$ a limit. An ordinal $n < \omega$ is a finite ordinal, and each nonzero finite ordinal $n$ contains a largest element $n - 1$, so $n = (n-1) + 1$ is a successor.
:::

:::intuition
The difference between successor and limit is the difference between "and then one more" and "after all of those". You reach $\omega$ not by taking one more step from something, but by standing past the entire infinite sequence $0, 1, 2, \dots$ at once. Nothing is immediately before $\omega$: for any $n < \omega$ there is $n + 1$ still below $\omega$. That gap is why a recursion needs a separate limit clause — at $\omega$ there is no "previous value" to feed in.
:::

:::example[Successor or limit?]
Classify each of $17$, $\omega$, $\omega + 5$, $\omega \cdot 2$, $\omega^2$, and $\omega^\omega$.
:::

:::solution
1. $17 = 16 + 1$ is a successor; so is every nonzero finite ordinal.
2. $\omega$ is a limit, by *Omega is the least limit ordinal*.
3. $\omega + 5 = (\omega + 4) + 1$ is a successor: it sits five steps past $\omega$, and the immediately preceding ordinal $\omega + 4$ exists.
4. $\omega \cdot 2 = \sup\{\omega + n : n < \omega\}$ is a limit — it has no immediate predecessor, since for any $\omega + n$ below it, $\omega + n + 1$ is still below.
5. $\omega^2 = \sup\{\omega \cdot n : n < \omega\}$ is a limit, and indeed a limit of limits.
6. $\omega^\omega = \sup\{\omega^n : n < \omega\}$ is a limit, and a limit of limits of limits and so on.
:::

:::pitfall
"Limit ordinal" is not the same as "infinite ordinal". Every limit ordinal is infinite, but $\omega + 1$, $\omega + 2$, $\omega \cdot 2 + 7$ are all infinite successors. Infinitude is about size; being a limit is about how the ordinal is reached.
:::

## Ordinal arithmetic

<!-- section: examples-ordinals -->

Ordinal arithmetic is defined by gluing order types together, and the definitions look entirely familiar until you compute with them. Addition is concatenation, multiplication is replacement of each point of one order by a whole copy of another — and because concatenation cares which block comes first, neither operation commutes.

:::definition[Ordinal addition and multiplication]{#ordinal-arith}
Let $\alpha, \beta$ be ordinals.

$\alpha + \beta$ is the order type of the disjoint union $\alpha \sqcup \beta$ ordered so that $\alpha$'s copy comes first, entirely below $\beta$'s copy, with each copy keeping its own order.

$\alpha \cdot \beta$ is the order type of the cartesian product $\beta \times \alpha$ ordered lexicographically — equivalently, $\beta$ copies of $\alpha$ laid end to end.

Equivalently, by recursion: $\alpha + 0 = \alpha$, $\alpha + (\beta+1) = (\alpha + \beta) + 1$, and $\alpha + \lambda = \sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$ for limit $\lambda$; and $\alpha \cdot 0 = 0$, $\alpha \cdot (\beta+1) = \alpha\cdot\beta + \alpha$, $\alpha \cdot \lambda = \sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha \cdot \beta)$ for limit $\lambda$.
:::

:::note
Read "$\alpha \cdot \beta$" as "$\beta$ copies of $\alpha$". The convention is universal in set theory and is the opposite of the schoolroom reading; it is what makes the recursive clause $\alpha \cdot (\beta + 1) = \alpha\cdot\beta + \alpha$ come out right.
:::

:::theorem[Associativity]{#ordinal-associativity}
For all ordinals, $(\alpha + \beta) + \gamma = \alpha + (\beta + \gamma)$ and $(\alpha \cdot \beta)\cdot\gamma = \alpha\cdot(\beta\cdot\gamma)$. Ordinal addition and multiplication are also left-distributive: $\alpha\cdot(\beta+\gamma) = \alpha\cdot\beta + \alpha\cdot\gamma$.
:::

:::proof
Use the order-type definitions, where all three claims become statements about rearranging brackets in a concatenation.

For addition: both $(\alpha+\beta)+\gamma$ and $\alpha+(\beta+\gamma)$ are the order type of the disjoint union $\alpha \sqcup \beta \sqcup \gamma$ with the three blocks in that order and each block internally ordered as itself. The identity map between the two bracketings is a bijection, and it preserves order because in both readings a point of an earlier block precedes a point of a later block and points within a block compare as they did originally. By *Representation of well-orders* the two order types are the same ordinal.

For multiplication: $(\alpha\cdot\beta)\cdot\gamma$ is $\gamma$ copies of ($\beta$ copies of $\alpha$), and $\alpha\cdot(\beta\cdot\gamma)$ is ($\beta\cdot\gamma$ = $\gamma$ copies of $\beta$) copies of $\alpha$. Both are the lexicographic order on $\gamma \times \beta \times \alpha$, the leftmost coordinate most significant; associativity of the cartesian product gives the order-isomorphism.

For distributivity: $\alpha\cdot(\beta+\gamma)$ is $\beta+\gamma$ copies of $\alpha$, i.e. $\beta$ copies followed by $\gamma$ copies, which is exactly $\alpha\cdot\beta$ followed by $\alpha\cdot\gamma$.
:::

:::theorem[Neither operation is commutative]{#ordinal-noncommutative}
Ordinal addition and multiplication are not commutative. Specifically

$$1 + \omega = \omega \neq \omega + 1, \qquad 2 \cdot \omega = \omega \neq \omega \cdot 2.$$

Right distributivity also fails: $(1+1)\cdot\omega = \omega$ while $1\cdot\omega + 1\cdot\omega = \omega\cdot 2$.
:::

:::proof
**$1 + \omega = \omega$.** The order type $1 + \omega$ is one point followed by a copy of $\mathbb{N}$: $\ast, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Map $\ast \mapsto 0$ and $n \mapsto n+1$. This is a bijection onto $\omega$ and it clearly preserves order, so the order type is $\omega$. Structurally: relabelling a sequence that has a first element and no last element as $0, 1, 2, \dots$ always yields $\omega$.

**$\omega + 1 \neq \omega$.** The order $\omega + 1$ is $0, 1, 2, \dots$ followed by one extra point $\ast$ above all of them. It has a *greatest element*, and $\omega$ does not; having a greatest element is preserved by order-isomorphism, so the two types differ. By trichotomy and $\omega \in \omega+1$ we get $\omega < \omega + 1$.

**$2\cdot\omega = \omega$.** This is $\omega$ copies of the two-point order: $(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1),(2,0),(2,1),\dots$ — an increasing sequence with a first element, no last element, and every element having only finitely many predecessors. Mapping the $k$-th term to $k$ is an order-isomorphism onto $\omega$, so $2\cdot\omega = \omega$.

**$\omega\cdot 2 \neq \omega$.** This is two copies of $\omega$ end to end: $0,1,2,\dots$ then $0',1',2',\dots$. The element $0'$ has infinitely many predecessors, whereas in $\omega$ every element has finitely many. "Number of predecessors" is an order-isomorphism invariant, so the types differ, and $\omega\cdot 2 = \omega + \omega > \omega$.

**Right distributivity.** $(1+1)\cdot\omega = 2\cdot\omega = \omega$ by the third computation, while $1\cdot\omega + 1\cdot\omega = \omega + \omega = \omega\cdot 2 > \omega$.
:::

:::example[Computing with ordinal sums and products]
Simplify $(\omega + 3) + \omega$, $\;3 + \omega + 3$, $\;(\omega\cdot 2)\cdot 3$, and $\;\omega\cdot(2\cdot 3)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $(\omega+3)+\omega = \omega + (3 + \omega)$ by associativity, and $3 + \omega = \omega$ by the same absorption argument as $1+\omega=\omega$ — a finite head in front of an $\omega$-sequence relabels away. So the answer is $\omega + \omega = \omega\cdot 2$.
2. $3 + \omega + 3 = (3+\omega) + 3 = \omega + 3$. The leading $3$ vanishes; the trailing $3$ does not.
3. $(\omega\cdot2)\cdot3 = \omega\cdot(2\cdot3) = \omega\cdot 6$ by associativity. Concretely: three copies of (two copies of $\omega$) is six copies of $\omega$.
4. $\omega\cdot(2\cdot3) = \omega\cdot 6$ as well — associativity is genuine, and it is only *commutativity* that fails.
:::

:::example[Left cancellation holds, right cancellation does not]
Show that $\alpha + \beta = \alpha + \gamma$ implies $\beta = \gamma$, but find $\beta \neq \gamma$ with $\beta + \alpha = \gamma + \alpha$.
:::

:::solution
1. Left cancellation: addition on the right is strictly increasing, $\beta < \gamma \Rightarrow \alpha+\beta < \alpha+\gamma$, because the copy of $\beta$ is a proper initial segment of the copy of $\gamma$ sitting above the same copy of $\alpha$. A strictly increasing function is injective, so $\alpha+\beta = \alpha+\gamma$ forces $\beta=\gamma$.
2. Right cancellation fails: take $\beta = 1$, $\gamma = 2$, $\alpha = \omega$. Then $1 + \omega = \omega = 2 + \omega$ but $1 \neq 2$. The asymmetry is exactly the absorption of a finite head.
:::

:::theorem[Cantor normal form]{#cantor-normal-form}
Every ordinal $\alpha > 0$ can be written uniquely as

$$\alpha = \omega^{\beta_1}c_1 + \omega^{\beta_2}c_2 + \cdots + \omega^{\beta_k}c_k$$

with $k \ge 1$, ordinals $\beta_1 > \beta_2 > \cdots > \beta_k \ge 0$, and finite $c_i$ with $1 \le c_i < \omega$.
:::

:::proof
*Existence*, by induction on $\alpha$ using *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered*. Let $\beta_1$ be the largest ordinal with $\omega^{\beta_1} \le \alpha$; it exists because $\omega^\gamma > \alpha$ for $\gamma$ large (e.g. $\gamma = \alpha+1$) and the set of $\gamma$ with $\omega^\gamma \le \alpha$ is a set of ordinals bounded above, so it has a maximum. Let $c_1$ be the largest finite $c$ with $\omega^{\beta_1}c \le \alpha$; it is finite, for otherwise $\omega^{\beta_1}\cdot\omega = \omega^{\beta_1+1} \le \alpha$, contradicting maximality of $\beta_1$. By subtraction on the left there is a unique $\alpha'$ with $\alpha = \omega^{\beta_1}c_1 + \alpha'$, and $\alpha' < \omega^{\beta_1}$ by maximality of $c_1$; in particular $\alpha' < \alpha$, so the induction hypothesis expands $\alpha'$, and all its exponents are $< \beta_1$.

*Uniqueness.* If two such expressions were equal, compare leading terms. The value of $\omega^{\beta_1}c_1 + \alpha'$ with $\alpha' < \omega^{\beta_1}$ lies in the interval $[\omega^{\beta_1}c_1, \omega^{\beta_1}(c_1+1))$, and these intervals for distinct pairs $(\beta_1, c_1)$ are disjoint. So the leading exponent and coefficient are determined by $\alpha$, and then $\alpha'$ is determined by left cancellation; induction finishes the argument.
:::

:::example[Reading off a Cantor normal form]
Write $\omega^2\cdot 3 + \omega\cdot 5 + 7$ in Cantor normal form and state its leading exponent, the number of terms, and the sum of its coefficients.
:::

:::solution
1. It is already in normal form: exponents $2 > 1 > 0$ strictly decrease and all coefficients $3, 5, 7$ are finite and nonzero.
2. The leading exponent is $\beta_1 = 2$; the number of terms is $k = 3$; the coefficients sum to $3 + 5 + 7 = 15$.
3. As a sanity check, note the term $7$ is $\omega^0 \cdot 7$, so the constant term is a legitimate term of the form and is written last.
:::

:::pitfall
When adding two Cantor normal forms, terms of the left summand whose exponent is *smaller* than the leading exponent of the right summand are annihilated: $(\omega^2 + \omega + 5) + \omega^3 = \omega^3$, because $\omega^2 + \omega^3 = \omega^3$ by absorption. Ordinal addition is not "collect like terms".
:::

## Cardinality: counting by bijection

<!-- section: definition-cardinals -->

Cantor's move was to take the one thing counting really means — pairing off — and apply it without restriction. Two sets have the same size if their elements can be matched one to one. No appeal to a number is made; the comparison is direct.

:::definition[Equinumerosity and cardinal comparison]{#equinumerous}
Sets $A$ and $B$ are **equinumerous**, written $|A| = |B|$ or $A \approx B$, if there is a bijection $A \to B$. We write $|A| \le |B|$ if there is an injection $A \to B$, and $|A| < |B|$ if $|A| \le |B|$ but not $|A| = |B|$.
:::

:::proposition[Equinumerosity behaves like equality]{#approx-equivalence}
For all sets, $A \approx A$; if $A \approx B$ then $B \approx A$; and if $A \approx B$ and $B \approx C$ then $A \approx C$. Likewise $\le$ is reflexive and transitive.
:::

:::proof
The identity map witnesses $A \approx A$. If $f : A \to B$ is a bijection then $f^{-1} : B \to A$ is a bijection. If $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to C$ are bijections then $g \circ f$ is a bijection, being a composite of injections and of surjections. The same three constructions, read for injections only, give reflexivity and transitivity of $\le$.
:::

Antisymmetry — that $|A| \le |B|$ and $|B| \le |A|$ force $|A| = |B|$ — is not formal at all. Two injections give no bijection in any obvious way, and the theorem that they do is the backbone of the whole comparison theory.

:::theorem[Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein]{#csb}
If there are injections $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to A$, then there is a bijection $A \to B$. Equivalently, $|A| \le |B|$ and $|B| \le |A|$ imply $|A| = |B|$. No form of the axiom of choice is used.
:::

:::proof
Think of $A$ and $B$ as disjoint and of $f$, $g$ as arrows. Every element has at most one arrow into it, so following arrows *backwards* from any point gives a chain that is uniquely determined: from $a \in A$ go back to the unique $b$ with $g(b) = a$ if one exists, then to the unique $a'$ with $f(a') = b$ if one exists, and so on. Each chain either runs backward forever, or terminates at a point of $A$ not in the image of $g$, or terminates at a point of $B$ not in the image of $f$.

Partition $A$ accordingly: let $A_A$ be the elements whose backward chain stops in $A$, $A_B$ those whose chain stops in $B$, and $A_\infty$ those whose chain is infinite. Partition $B$ into $B_A, B_B, B_\infty$ the same way. Since $f$ and $g$ shift a chain by one step without changing where it ultimately stops, $f$ restricts to a bijection $A_A \to B_A$ and to a bijection $A_\infty \to B_\infty$, while $g$ restricts to a bijection $B_B \to A_B$.

Define $h : A \to B$ by $h(a) = f(a)$ for $a \in A_A \cup A_\infty$ and $h(a) = g^{-1}(a)$ for $a \in A_B$. The three pieces are bijections onto the three disjoint pieces $B_A$, $B_\infty$, $B_B$ whose union is $B$, so $h$ is a bijection.

No choices were made: each backward step is *unique* when it exists, because $f$ and $g$ are injective, so the chain of any element is defined outright rather than selected.
:::

:::definition[Cardinal number]{#cardinal}
A **cardinal** is an ordinal $\kappa$ that is not equinumerous with any smaller ordinal — an **initial ordinal**. For a well-orderable set $A$, $|A|$ denotes the least ordinal equinumerous with $A$, which is then a cardinal.
:::

:::note
Under AC every set can be well-ordered, so every set has a cardinal in this sense and cardinals are exactly the initial ordinals. Without AC one falls back on Scott's trick — take the set of all sets of least rank equinumerous with $A$ — which still assigns a set-sized invariant to every $A$. Nothing in this chapter depends on which route is taken.
:::

:::example[Same cardinal, different order type]
Exhibit two well-orderings of $\mathbb{N}$ with different order types, and explain why this cannot happen for a finite set.
:::

:::solution
1. The usual order $0 < 1 < 2 < \cdots$ has type $\omega$.
2. Reorder by putting $0$ last: $1 < 2 < 3 < \cdots < 0$. This is still a well-order on the same set, and it has a greatest element, so its type is $\omega + 1$.
3. Hence $|\omega| = |\omega + 1| = \aleph_0$ while $\mathrm{ot} = \omega$ and $\omega+1$ differ. The ordinal $\omega+1$ is therefore not a cardinal.
4. For a finite set this is impossible: any two well-orderings of an $n$-element set are isomorphic, because the isomorphism can be built by repeatedly matching least elements, and the process runs out simultaneously on both sides. Order and size only come apart in the infinite.
:::

## Finite, countable, and the first surprises

<!-- section: finite-vs-infinite -->

:::definition[Finite, countable, uncountable]{#countable}
A set is **finite** if it is equinumerous with some $n < \omega$, and **infinite** otherwise. It is **countably infinite** if it is equinumerous with $\omega$, **countable** if finite or countably infinite, and **uncountable** otherwise. We write $\aleph_0 = |\omega|$.
:::

:::theorem[Pigeonhole principle]{#pigeonhole}
For $m < n < \omega$ there is no injection $n \to m$. Consequently no finite set is equinumerous with a proper subset of itself, and the $n$ in the definition of finite is unique.
:::

:::proof
Induct on $m$. For $m = 0$: any $n > 0$ is nonempty and there is no function at all into $\varnothing$. Suppose the claim holds for $m$ and let $f : n \to m+1$ be injective with $n > m+1$. If $m$ is not in the range of $f$, then $f$ maps $n$ injectively into $m$, and $n > m+1 > m$ contradicts the hypothesis. Otherwise let $k = f^{-1}(m)$ and swap values so that the top element $n-1$ maps to $m$: define $f'$ equal to $f$ except $f'(n-1) = m$ and $f'(k) = f(n-1)$. Then $f'$ is injective and restricts to an injection $n - 1 \to m$ with $n - 1 > m$, contradicting the hypothesis again.
:::

:::theorem[The integers and rationals are countable]{#n-z-q}
$|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{Z}| = |\mathbb{Q}| = \aleph_0$.
:::

:::proof
**$|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{Z}|$.** Define $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z}$ by $f(n) = n/2$ for even $n$ and $f(n) = -(n+1)/2$ for odd $n$. This lists $\mathbb{Z}$ as $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, \dots$. It is injective since evens go to non-negatives and odds to negatives, and within each parity the formula is injective; it is surjective since $k \ge 0$ is hit by $2k$ and $k < 0$ by $-2k - 1$.

**$|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}|$.** The Cantor pairing function $\pi(m,n) = \tfrac{(m+n)(m+n+1)}{2} + n$ enumerates $\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}$ by sweeping the anti-diagonals $m+n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ in turn, each diagonal being finite; it is a bijection because the diagonals partition the plane and $\tfrac{d(d+1)}{2}$ is exactly the number of points on earlier diagonals.

**$|\mathbb{N}| = |\mathbb{Q}|$.** Each rational has a unique representation $p/q$ in lowest terms with $q \ge 1$, giving an injection $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N} \approx \mathbb{N}$. In the other direction $n \mapsto n$ injects $\mathbb{N}$ into $\mathbb{Q}$. By *Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein*, $|\mathbb{Q}| = \aleph_0$. Note that this conclusion is not defeated by $\mathbb{Q}$ being dense: density is an order property, and cardinality does not see order.
:::

:::theorem[A countable union of countable sets is countable]{#countable-union}
If $\{A_n : n < \omega\}$ is a family of countable sets then $\bigcup_{n<\omega} A_n$ is countable. **This uses the axiom of countable choice.**
:::

:::proof
Each $A_n$ is countable, so the set $E_n$ of surjections $\omega \to A_n$ is nonempty (for $A_n = \varnothing$ discard the index). Countable choice supplies a single function $n \mapsto e_n$ with $e_n \in E_n$ — and this is the step that cannot be avoided: the enumerations exist individually, but nothing in ZF picks one for every $n$ at once.

Given the $e_n$, define $g : \omega\times\omega \to \bigcup_n A_n$ by $g(n,k) = e_n(k)$. Every element of the union lies in some $A_n$ and so is $e_n(k)$ for some $k$; hence $g$ is surjective. Composing with a bijection $\omega \to \omega\times\omega$ from *The integers and rationals are countable* gives a surjection $\omega \to \bigcup_n A_n$, and a surjection from $\omega$ onto a nonempty set $S$ yields an injection $S \to \omega$ by sending $s$ to the least preimage — a definable choice, needing no further axiom. So the union is countable.
:::

:::remark
It is consistent with ZF alone that the real numbers are a countable union of countable sets. That sounds absurd until you notice what fails: the sets are each countable, but no simultaneous system of enumerations exists, so the diagonal argument has nothing to diagonalise against. Countable choice is exactly the missing ingredient, and this is the cleanest illustration of why "each of them can be listed" is weaker than "they can all be listed at once".
:::

:::example[Building a bijection between $\mathbb{N}$ and a union]
Show directly that the set of all finite strings over the alphabet $\{a,b\}$ is countable, without using choice.
:::

:::solution
1. Let $A_n$ be the set of strings of length exactly $n$. Then $|A_n| = 2^n$, so each $A_n$ is finite, and the whole set is $\bigcup_{n<\omega} A_n$.
2. No choice is needed here because each $A_n$ carries a *canonical* enumeration: list its $2^n$ strings in alphabetical order. The choice function is definable, so we do not have to assume one.
3. Concatenating those lists enumerates every string: $\varepsilon$; $a, b$; $aa, ab, ba, bb$; and so on. Each string appears exactly once, so the set is countably infinite.
:::

:::pitfall
"Infinite" and "uncountable" are not synonyms, and neither is "dense" a size condition. $\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite, dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and still has exactly as many elements as $\mathbb{N}$.
:::

## Cantor's theorem and the uncountable

<!-- section: alephs-and-continuum -->

Everything so far has been a levelling: the integers, the rationals, the algebraic numbers all collapse to $\aleph_0$. The natural guess at that point is that all infinite sets are the same size. Cantor's theorem destroys the guess, and does so for every set at once.

:::theorem[Cantor's theorem]{#cantor}
For every set $A$, $|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|$. In particular there is no largest cardinal.
:::

:::proof
The map $a \mapsto \{a\}$ is an injection $A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$, so $|A| \le |\mathcal{P}(A)|$.

Suppose some $f : A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$ were surjective, and set

$$D = \{a \in A : a \notin f(a)\},$$

which is a subset of $A$ and hence, by surjectivity, equals $f(d)$ for some $d \in A$. Ask whether $d \in D$. If $d \in D$ then by the definition of $D$ we have $d \notin f(d) = D$. If $d \notin D = f(d)$ then $d$ satisfies the defining condition, so $d \in D$. Both cases are contradictory, so no surjection $A \to \mathcal{P}(A)$ exists; in particular no bijection does, and $|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|$.
:::

:::intuition
The diagonal argument in one sentence: list the subsets alongside the elements, read down the diagonal asking "is element $a$ in the set indexed by $a$?", and flip every answer. The resulting subset differs from the $a$-th listed subset at the $a$-th place, for every $a$, so it was never on the list. The same trick shows no program enumerates all programs' behaviour, which is Turing's halting argument with the same skeleton.
:::

:::corollary[The continuum is uncountable]{#continuum-uncountable}
$|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| = 2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$, and $|\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0}$. Hence $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable and the irrational numbers are uncountable.
:::

:::proof
Subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ correspond bijectively to their indicator functions $\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}$, so $|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| = 2^{\aleph_0}$, and *Cantor's theorem* with $A = \mathbb{N}$ gives $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$.

For $|\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0}$: binary expansions give an injection $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}} \to \mathbb{R}$ via $s \mapsto \sum_n 2 s(n) 3^{-n-1}$, whose image lies in the Cantor set and which is injective because ternary digits $0$ and $2$ never produce a carry collision. Conversely each real is determined by the set of rationals below it, giving an injection $\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Q}) \approx \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})$. *Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein* closes the sandwich.

If the irrationals were countable then $\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q}\cup(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})$ would be a union of two countable sets, hence countable by *A countable union of countable sets is countable* — and that instance needs no choice, since both enumerations are given. So the irrationals are uncountable.
:::

:::example[The algebraic numbers are countable, so transcendentals exist]
Show that the set of algebraic real numbers is countable, and deduce that transcendental numbers exist.
:::

:::solution
1. A polynomial with integer coefficients of degree $\le n$ is determined by its $n+1$ coefficients, so the set of such polynomials injects into $\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}$, which is countable.
2. The set of all integer polynomials is therefore a countable union over $n$ of countable sets, and each has a canonical enumeration, so no choice is needed: it is countable.
3. Each nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ real roots, so the algebraic reals are a countable union of finite sets and are countable.
4. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable by *The continuum is uncountable*, the complement — the transcendental reals — is nonempty, and in fact uncountable. Cantor obtained the existence of transcendentals this way in 1874 without exhibiting a single one.
:::

## The arithmetic of cardinals

<!-- section: cardinal-arithmetic -->

Cardinal operations are defined on sets and then read off as sizes. The definitions mirror the ordinal ones in name only: cardinal addition is disjoint union with *no* regard for order, which is precisely why it commutes.

:::definition[Cardinal sum, product and power]{#cardinal-ops}
For cardinals $\kappa = |A|$ and $\lambda = |B|$ with $A \cap B = \varnothing$:

$$\kappa + \lambda = |A \cup B|, \qquad \kappa\cdot\lambda = |A\times B|, \qquad \kappa^{\lambda} = |A^{B}|,$$

where $A^B$ is the set of all functions $B \to A$. These do not depend on the representatives chosen.
:::

:::pitfall
The symbols $+$, $\cdot$ and exponentiation now mean two different things. As *ordinals*, $\omega + 1 > \omega$ and $2^{\omega} = \sup_n 2^n = \omega$. As *cardinals*, $\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0$ and $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$. Same notation, different operations; context, and whether the letters are $\alpha,\beta$ or $\kappa,\lambda$, is what tells them apart.
:::

:::proposition[Commutative, associative, distributive]{#cardinal-laws}
For all cardinals, $\kappa+\lambda = \lambda+\kappa$, $\kappa\lambda = \lambda\kappa$, both are associative, $\kappa(\lambda+\mu) = \kappa\lambda + \kappa\mu$, and the exponent laws $\kappa^{\lambda+\mu} = \kappa^\lambda\kappa^\mu$, $(\kappa^\lambda)^\mu = \kappa^{\lambda\mu}$, $(\kappa\lambda)^\mu = \kappa^\mu\lambda^\mu$ hold.
:::

:::proof
Each is a canonical bijection. Commutativity of $+$ is $A\cup B = B\cup A$; of $\cdot$, the swap $(a,b)\mapsto(b,a)$. Associativity of $\cdot$ is the regrouping $(a,b,c)$. Distributivity is $A\times(B\sqcup C) = (A\times B)\sqcup(A\times C)$. For $\kappa^{\lambda+\mu}$, a function on $B \sqcup C$ is exactly a pair consisting of its restrictions to $B$ and to $C$. For $(\kappa^\lambda)^\mu$, currying identifies a function $C \to A^B$ with a function $B\times C \to A$. For $(\kappa\lambda)^\mu$, a function into a product is a pair of functions into the factors. None of these bijections makes a choice.
:::

:::theorem[Absorption for infinite cardinals]{#absorption}
For infinite cardinals $\kappa, \lambda$ with $\lambda \neq 0$,

$$\kappa + \lambda = \kappa\cdot\lambda = \max(\kappa,\lambda).$$

**This uses the axiom of choice** (via the fact, equivalent to AC, that every infinite cardinal $\kappa$ satisfies $\kappa\cdot\kappa = \kappa$).
:::

:::proof
The engine is $\kappa\cdot\kappa = \kappa$ for every infinite cardinal $\kappa$, proved by transfinite induction on $\kappa$ using the Gödel pairing: well-order $\kappa\times\kappa$ by comparing first $\max(\alpha,\beta)$, then $\alpha$, then $\beta$. Every proper initial segment of that order sits inside $\gamma\times\gamma$ for some $\gamma<\kappa$, which by induction has size $|\gamma| < \kappa$; so the order type of $\kappa\times\kappa$ is at most $\kappa$, giving $\kappa\cdot\kappa \le \kappa$, and $\ge$ is clear. (AC enters in guaranteeing that every infinite set *is* such a $\kappa$, i.e. is well-orderable.)

Now let $\mu = \max(\kappa,\lambda)$, so $\mu \le \kappa+\lambda \le \mu + \mu = 2\cdot\mu \le \mu\cdot\mu = \mu$, and *Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein* gives $\kappa+\lambda = \mu$. Similarly $\mu \le \kappa\cdot\lambda \le \mu\cdot\mu = \mu$, so $\kappa\cdot\lambda = \mu$.
:::

:::theorem[Exponentiation is the hard operation]{#exponentiation-hard}
Cardinal exponentiation is not determined by $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ in the way $+$ and $\cdot$ are. Already $2^{\aleph_0}$ is not decided by ZFC: it is consistent that $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$, and consistent that $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_{17}$, or $\aleph_{\omega+1}$, or many other values. The only ZFC constraints on $2^{\aleph_0}$ are that it exceeds $\aleph_0$ and that its cofinality is uncountable.
:::

:::proof
The positive constraints are provable here. $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$ is *Cantor's theorem*. That $\operatorname{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) > \aleph_0$ is König's theorem: if $2^{\aleph_0}$ were the supremum of countably many smaller cardinals $\mu_n$, then $2^{\aleph_0} = (2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} \le \prod_n \mu_n' $ for suitable $\mu_n' < 2^{\aleph_0}$, and König's inequality $\sum_n \mu_n < \prod_n \nu_n$ whenever $\mu_n<\nu_n$ yields a contradiction. So $2^{\aleph_0} \neq \aleph_\omega$, whose cofinality is $\omega$.

The negative half — that no further value is excluded — is Cohen's forcing theorem, discussed in *History and independence* below, and is not proved here. Easton's theorem sharpens it: for regular cardinals, the function $\kappa\mapsto 2^\kappa$ may be prescribed almost arbitrarily subject only to monotonicity and the cofinality restriction.
:::

:::example[Cardinal computations that all collapse]
Compute $\aleph_0 + \aleph_0$, $\;\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0$, $\;\aleph_0 + 2^{\aleph_0}$, $\;(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}$, and $\;\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\aleph_0+\aleph_0 = \max(\aleph_0,\aleph_0) = \aleph_0$ by *Absorption for infinite cardinals*; concretely, evens and odds partition $\mathbb{N}$.
2. $\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0 = \aleph_0$, the Cantor pairing bijection again.
3. $\aleph_0 + 2^{\aleph_0} = \max(\aleph_0, 2^{\aleph_0}) = 2^{\aleph_0}$.
4. $(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0\cdot\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$ by the exponent law and step 2. So the set of real sequences has the same size as $\mathbb{R}$.
5. $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0} \le (2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$ and $\ge 2^{\aleph_0}$, so it equals $2^{\aleph_0}$. Sums and products collapse; exponentials jump exactly once and then stabilise at this level.
:::

## The aleph hierarchy

<!-- section: definition-alephs -->

The alephs are the cardinals indexed by ordinals: a transfinite list of infinite sizes with nothing between consecutive entries. Building it requires knowing that a next cardinal always exists, and that is a theorem, not an assumption.

:::lemma[Hartogs' theorem]{#hartogs}
For every set $A$ there is a least ordinal $\mathcal{H}(A)$ that does not inject into $A$. It is a cardinal, and it is obtained without the axiom of choice.
:::

:::proof
Consider the collection $W$ of all pairs $(S, <)$ where $S \subseteq A$ and $<$ well-orders $S$. Each such pair is an element of $\mathcal{P}(A)\times\mathcal{P}(A\times A)$, so $W$ is a set by separation. By *Representation of well-orders* and replacement, $H = \{\mathrm{ot}(S,<) : (S,<)\in W\}$ is a set of ordinals.

An ordinal $\alpha$ injects into $A$ if and only if $\alpha \in H$: an injection transports the well-order of $\alpha$ onto a subset of $A$, and conversely. $H$ is transitive, since a smaller ordinal injects whenever a larger one does, so $H$ is itself an ordinal, and it is the least ordinal not in $H$ — that is, the least ordinal not injecting into $A$. It is a cardinal because any smaller ordinal injects into $A$ while $H$ does not, so $H$ is not equinumerous with a smaller ordinal. No choice was used: every step was separation, replacement or power set.
:::

:::definition[The aleph function]{#aleph-function}
Define by transfinite recursion

$$\aleph_0 = |\omega|, \qquad \aleph_{\alpha+1} = \mathcal{H}(\aleph_\alpha) = \aleph_\alpha^{+}, \qquad \aleph_\lambda = \sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\aleph_\alpha \ \text{ for limit } \lambda.$$

Here $\kappa^{+}$ denotes the least cardinal greater than $\kappa$. The ordinal underlying $\aleph_\alpha$ is also written $\omega_\alpha$.
:::

:::theorem[The alephs exhaust the infinite cardinals]{#alephs-exhaust}
The function $\alpha\mapsto\aleph_\alpha$ is a strictly increasing enumeration of all infinite cardinals that are well-orderable. **Under AC**, every infinite cardinal is $\aleph_\alpha$ for exactly one ordinal $\alpha$.
:::

:::proof
Strict increase: $\aleph_{\alpha+1} > \aleph_\alpha$ by *Hartogs' theorem*, and at limits the supremum of a strictly increasing family of cardinals is a cardinal exceeding each of them — it is a cardinal because an initial ordinal is not equinumerous with anything smaller, and a bijection with some $\gamma < \sup$ would already be a bijection at some stage below.

Exhaustiveness: let $\kappa$ be an infinite well-ordered cardinal. The class $\{\alpha : \aleph_\alpha \le \kappa\}$ is nonempty ($\alpha=0$) and is bounded, since $\aleph_\alpha \ge \alpha$ for every $\alpha$ by induction, so $\aleph_{\kappa+1} > \kappa$. Take $\beta$ the largest index with $\aleph_\beta\le\kappa$. If $\aleph_\beta < \kappa$ then $\aleph_{\beta+1} = \aleph_\beta^{+}\le\kappa$, contradicting maximality; so $\kappa = \aleph_\beta$. Uniqueness follows from strict increase. Under AC every set is well-orderable, so every infinite cardinal is covered.
:::

:::note
Without AC the two statements come apart badly: the alephs still form a well-ordered chain, but there can be infinite cardinals incomparable with all of them. $|\mathbb{R}|$ is then not necessarily an aleph, and "how big is the continuum" stops even being a question about the aleph scale.
:::

## Successor and limit cardinals

<!-- section: successor-and-limit -->

The successor/limit split for ordinals induces one for cardinals, but the interesting invariant is finer: how *fast* a cardinal can be approached from below.

:::definition[Successor cardinal, limit cardinal, cofinality]{#cofinality}
A cardinal is a **successor cardinal** if it is $\kappa^{+}$ for some $\kappa$, and a **limit cardinal** otherwise. The **cofinality** $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)$ of a limit ordinal $\lambda$ is the least order type of a subset of $\lambda$ that is unbounded in $\lambda$. A cardinal is **regular** if $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa) = \kappa$ and **singular** otherwise.
:::

:::theorem[Successor cardinals are regular]{#successor-regular}
**Assuming AC**, $\kappa^{+}$ is regular for every infinite cardinal $\kappa$. In particular $\aleph_1, \aleph_2, \dots$ are regular, while $\aleph_\omega$ is singular with $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega) = \omega$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $A \subseteq \kappa^{+}$ is unbounded with $|A| \le \kappa$. Each $\alpha \in A$ has $|\alpha|\le\kappa$, so $\kappa^{+} = \bigcup_{\alpha\in A}\alpha$ has cardinality at most $\kappa\cdot\kappa = \kappa$ by *Absorption for infinite cardinals* — a union of at most $\kappa$ sets each of size at most $\kappa$. That contradicts $|\kappa^{+}| = \kappa^{+} > \kappa$. So every unbounded subset has size $\kappa^{+}$ and $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa^{+}) = \kappa^{+}$.

For $\aleph_\omega$: the set $\{\aleph_n : n<\omega\}$ is unbounded in $\aleph_\omega$ by definition of the limit clause and has order type $\omega$, so $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega)=\omega < \aleph_\omega$ and $\aleph_\omega$ is singular.
:::

:::intuition
Cofinality measures the shortest ladder to the top. $\aleph_1$ is regular: you cannot reach it along a countable ladder, because a countable union of countable sets is countable. $\aleph_\omega$ is singular: the ladder $\aleph_0,\aleph_1,\aleph_2,\dots$ has only $\omega$ rungs and gets there. That difference is what König's theorem exploits to rule out $2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_\omega$.
:::

:::example[Cofinalities of small ordinals]
Find $\operatorname{cf}(\omega)$, $\operatorname{cf}(\omega\cdot 2)$, $\operatorname{cf}(\omega^2)$, $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_1)$ and $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_{\omega_1})$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\operatorname{cf}(\omega)=\omega$: the least unbounded subset is an infinite increasing sequence, of type $\omega$.
2. $\operatorname{cf}(\omega\cdot2)=\omega$: the second block $\omega, \omega+1, \omega+2, \dots$ is unbounded and of type $\omega$; no finite set is unbounded in a limit.
3. $\operatorname{cf}(\omega^2)=\omega$: the sequence $\omega\cdot n$ for $n<\omega$ is unbounded, of type $\omega$.
4. $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_1)=\aleph_1$ by *Successor cardinals are regular*.
5. $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_{\omega_1}) = \operatorname{cf}(\omega_1) = \aleph_1$: the cofinality of $\aleph_\lambda$ at a limit $\lambda$ equals the cofinality of $\lambda$, since the ladder of indices transfers.
:::

## Arithmetic on the aleph scale

<!-- section: cardinal-arithmetic-aleph -->

:::corollary[Aleph sums and products]{#aleph-arith}
For all ordinals $\alpha,\beta$,

$$\aleph_\alpha + \aleph_\beta = \aleph_\alpha\cdot\aleph_\beta = \aleph_{\max(\alpha,\beta)},$$

and $\aleph_\alpha + n = \aleph_\alpha$, $\aleph_\alpha\cdot n = \aleph_\alpha$ for every finite $n \ge 1$. **AC is assumed.**
:::

:::proof
Apply *Absorption for infinite cardinals* to $\kappa = \aleph_\alpha$ and $\lambda = \aleph_\beta$, noting that $\max(\aleph_\alpha,\aleph_\beta) = \aleph_{\max(\alpha,\beta)}$ because $\alpha\mapsto\aleph_\alpha$ is strictly increasing. For a finite $n\ge1$ we have $\aleph_\alpha \le \aleph_\alpha + n \le \aleph_\alpha+\aleph_\alpha = \aleph_\alpha$, and likewise for the product.
:::

:::proposition[Exponentials on the aleph scale]{#aleph-exp}
For infinite cardinals, $\kappa^\lambda \le 2^{\kappa\cdot\lambda} = 2^{\max(\kappa,\lambda)}$, with equality when $\kappa \le 2^\lambda$. In particular $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$, and $\kappa^{\lambda} = 2^{\lambda}$ whenever $2 \le \kappa \le 2^{\lambda}$.
:::

:::proof
A function $\lambda\to\kappa$ is determined by its graph, a subset of $\lambda\times\kappa$, so $\kappa^\lambda \le 2^{|\lambda\times\kappa|} = 2^{\max(\kappa,\lambda)}$ using *Absorption for infinite cardinals*.

If $2\le\kappa\le 2^\lambda$ then monotonicity gives $2^\lambda \le \kappa^\lambda \le (2^\lambda)^\lambda = 2^{\lambda\cdot\lambda} = 2^\lambda$, so $\kappa^\lambda = 2^\lambda$. Taking $\lambda=\aleph_0$ and $\kappa = \aleph_0$ or $\aleph_1$ — both at most $2^{\aleph_0}$, the latter because $\aleph_1$ is the least uncountable cardinal and $2^{\aleph_0}$ is uncountable — gives the stated values.
:::

:::example[Sizes of familiar function spaces]
Compute the cardinality of: the set of all functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$; the set of all *continuous* functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$; and the set of all countable subsets of $\mathbb{R}$.
:::

:::solution
1. All functions: $|\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}| = (2^{\aleph_0})^{2^{\aleph_0}} = 2^{\aleph_0\cdot 2^{\aleph_0}} = 2^{2^{\aleph_0}}$, strictly larger than $|\mathbb{R}|$ by *Cantor's theorem*.
2. Continuous functions: a continuous function is determined by its restriction to $\mathbb{Q}$, giving an injection into $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Q}}$, of size $(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$. Constants give the reverse injection, so there are exactly $2^{\aleph_0}$ continuous functions. Continuity cuts the count down by a whole exponential.
3. Countable subsets: each is the image of a function $\omega\to\mathbb{R}$, so there are at most $(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0}=2^{\aleph_0}$ of them, and the singletons give at least that many. The answer is $2^{\aleph_0}$.
:::

## Beth numbers and where the continuum sits

<!-- section: examples-alephs -->

The alephs count *sizes in order*; the beths count *iterated power sets*. Whether the two scales agree is exactly the continuum question.

:::definition[Beth numbers]{#beth}
Define $\beth_0 = \aleph_0$, $\beth_{\alpha+1} = 2^{\beth_\alpha}$, and $\beth_\lambda = \sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\beth_\alpha$ at limits. Thus $\beth_1 = 2^{\aleph_0} = |\mathbb{R}|$ and $\beth_2 = 2^{2^{\aleph_0}} = |\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}|$.
:::

:::proposition[Beths dominate alephs]{#beth-dominates}
$\aleph_\alpha \le \beth_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$, and $\aleph_\alpha = \beth_\alpha$ for all $\alpha$ if and only if the generalised continuum hypothesis holds.
:::

:::proof
Induct on $\alpha$. At $0$ the two agree. At a successor, $\aleph_{\alpha+1} = \aleph_\alpha^{+} \le 2^{\aleph_\alpha} \le 2^{\beth_\alpha} = \beth_{\alpha+1}$, the first inequality because $2^{\aleph_\alpha} > \aleph_\alpha$ by *Cantor's theorem* and $\aleph_\alpha^{+}$ is the least cardinal above $\aleph_\alpha$. At a limit, the supremum of a pointwise-dominated family is dominated.

The inequality $\aleph_{\alpha}^{+} \le 2^{\aleph_\alpha}$ is an equality for all $\alpha$ precisely when GCH holds, and an easy induction shows the two hierarchies agree at every index exactly in that case.
:::

:::example[Placing familiar sets on the beth scale]
Give the cardinality, as a beth number, of $\mathbb{Q}$, of $\mathbb{R}$, of the set of continuous functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, and of $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$.
:::

:::solution
1. $|\mathbb{Q}| = \aleph_0 = \beth_0$ by *The integers and rationals are countable*.
2. $|\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0} = \beth_1$ by *The continuum is uncountable*.
3. The continuous functions number $2^{\aleph_0} = \beth_1$, computed in *Sizes of familiar function spaces* — the same rung as $\mathbb{R}$ itself.

## The continuum hypothesis, stated precisely

<!-- section: statement-ch -->

:::definition[Continuum hypothesis]{#ch}
The **continuum hypothesis** (CH) is the statement

$$2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1,$$

equivalently: every infinite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is either countable or equinumerous with $\mathbb{R}$; equivalently, there is no cardinal $\kappa$ with $\aleph_0 < \kappa < 2^{\aleph_0}$.

The **generalised continuum hypothesis** (GCH) is $2^{\aleph_\alpha} = \aleph_{\alpha+1}$ for every ordinal $\alpha$ — equivalently, $\aleph_\alpha = \beth_\alpha$ throughout.
:::

Three readings of the same sentence are worth keeping separate. As a statement about *sets of reals*, CH says no set of reals has an intermediate size. As a statement about *cardinal arithmetic*, it evaluates one exponential. As a statement about *two hierarchies*, it says the aleph ladder and the beth ladder agree at the first rung. The equivalences are immediate: $\aleph_1$ is by definition the least cardinal above $\aleph_0$, so "$2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$" and "nothing strictly between" say the same thing once we know, from AC, that $2^{\aleph_0}$ is an aleph at all.

:::pitfall
CH is *not* the claim that $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable — that is Cantor's theorem, and it is proved. CH is the much sharper claim about exactly *how far* above $\aleph_0$ the continuum sits. Nor is CH the claim that $\aleph_1$ exists; $\aleph_1$ exists by *Hartogs' theorem* regardless.
:::

:::example[Which of these are decided by ZFC?]
For each statement, say whether ZFC proves it, refutes it, or leaves it undecided: (a) $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$; (b) $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$; (c) $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_\omega$; (d) $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$.
:::

:::solution
1. (a) Proved — *Cantor's theorem*.
2. (b) Undecided: this is CH itself, consistent with ZFC by Gödel and independent by Cohen.
3. (c) Refuted: $\operatorname{cf}(\aleph_\omega) = \omega$, while König's theorem forces $\operatorname{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) > \omega$, as recorded in *Exponentiation is the hard operation*.
4. (d) Undecided: Cohen's method produces models of ZFC in which $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$, and Gödel's model refutes it, so neither direction is provable.
:::

## Gödel, Cohen, and what independence means

<!-- section: history-and-independence -->

Cantor posed the problem in 1878 and worked at it for the rest of his life; Hilbert put it first on his 1900 list of problems. The resolution came in two halves, thirty years apart, and the resolution is that there is no resolution of the kind Hilbert expected.

**Gödel, 1938–1940: CH cannot be refuted.** Gödel constructed the *constructible universe* $L$, built by transfinite recursion: $L_0 = \varnothing$, $L_{\alpha+1}$ is the set of subsets of $L_\alpha$ definable with parameters over $L_\alpha$, and $L_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha$. $L$ is an inner model of ZF in which AC and even GCH hold, because a definable subset of $L_\alpha$ appears at a stage indexed not much later than $\alpha$, which pins the power set down to the next cardinal. Hence if ZF is consistent, so is ZFC + GCH; CH cannot be disproved.

**Cohen, 1963: CH cannot be proved.** Cohen invented *forcing*, a method for adjoining new sets to a model in a controlled way. Starting from a model $M$ of ZFC, one chooses a partially ordered set of finite approximations — for CH's failure, finite partial functions from $\aleph_2\times\omega$ to $\{0,1\}$ — and adjoins a generic filter $G$ meeting every dense set of $M$. The extension $M[G]$ again satisfies ZFC, but now contains $\aleph_2$ distinct new reals, and a combinatorial condition on the poset (the countable chain condition) guarantees that the cardinals of $M$ are not collapsed, so $\aleph_2$ still means $\aleph_2$. In $M[G]$, $2^{\aleph_0}\ge\aleph_2$ and CH fails. Cohen received the Fields Medal in 1966, the only one ever awarded for work in logic.

:::remark
**Independent is not the same as unknown.** An unknown statement is one we have not yet settled — the Riemann hypothesis is unknown. An independent statement is one that provably cannot be settled from the given axioms: there are models of ZFC where CH holds and models where it fails, and both are models of exactly the same axioms. Asking for a proof of CH from ZFC is like asking to prove, from the group axioms alone, that a group is abelian. Some groups are and some are not; the axioms do not mention it.

Two caveats. First, independence is relative to an axiom system — CH is decided outright in ZFC + $V=L$ (where it is true). Second, independence is not a statement that CH has no truth value; that is a philosophical position, not a theorem, and it is not universally held.
:::

## What CH buys and what its negation buys

<!-- section: consequences -->

Because CH is independent, both it and its negation are available as extra axioms, and each has a characteristic effect on the rest of mathematics.

Assuming CH, one gets a strong transfinite induction principle of length $\omega_1$ over the reals: any well-ordering of $\mathbb{R}$ in type $\omega_1$ has all proper initial segments countable, which is exactly the hypothesis needed for a long list of constructions. Consequences include a *Sierpiński set* (uncountable, meeting every measure-zero set in a countable set), a *Luzin set* (uncountable, meeting every meagre set countably), a non-measurable set of a particularly explicit kind, and — via Sierpiński's theorem — the decomposition of the plane into two pieces, one meeting every vertical line countably and the other meeting every horizontal line countably. In functional analysis, CH implies that every algebra homomorphism from $C(X)$ into a Banach algebra is continuous fails — Kaplansky's problem has a discontinuous solution under CH, and none under Martin's Axiom plus $\neg$CH. In model theory, CH implies all countably saturated models of the same complete theory of size $2^{\aleph_0}$ are isomorphic, and ultrapowers of $\mathbb{R}$ modulo nonprincipal ultrafilters on $\omega$ are all isomorphic.

Assuming $\neg$CH, in the strong form given by **Martin's Axiom**, the sets of size less than $2^{\aleph_0}$ behave like countable sets: every such set of reals has measure zero and is meagre, every such union of measure-zero sets has measure zero, and the Suslin hypothesis holds. The structure below the continuum becomes homogeneous rather than stratified, which is why MA + $\neg$CH is a popular working hypothesis in set-theoretic topology.

:::example[A construction that CH makes possible]
Sketch why CH gives a set $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ that is uncountable but meets every measure-zero set in a countable set.
:::

:::solution
1. Under CH there are exactly $\aleph_1$ Borel measure-zero sets, so enumerate them as $\{N_\alpha : \alpha<\omega_1\}$ — this step is where CH is used, since without it there could be $\aleph_2$ of them and the recursion would not fit.
2. Recurse along $\omega_1$: having chosen $\{x_\beta : \beta<\alpha\}$, pick $x_\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{\beta\le\alpha}N_\beta$, possible because a countable union of measure-zero sets has measure zero and so cannot be all of $\mathbb{R}$.
3. Put $S = \{x_\alpha : \alpha<\omega_1\}$. It is uncountable. Given a measure-zero set $N \subseteq N_\gamma$, every $x_\alpha$ with $\alpha\ge\gamma$ avoids $N_\gamma$, so $S\cap N \subseteq \{x_\alpha : \alpha<\gamma\}$ is countable.
4. The limit step is doing real work: at each stage the set already built is countable, which is exactly what CH guarantees for every proper initial segment of $\omega_1$.
:::

## Modern perspectives

<!-- section: modern-views -->

The independence result did not end the subject; it changed the question from "is CH true?" to "which extensions of ZFC should we adopt, and on what grounds?"

**The $V = L$ position.** Adopting constructibility settles CH, GCH and a great deal more. Almost nobody adopts it, because $L$ is provably too thin to contain large cardinals above a measurable, and the large cardinal hierarchy has turned out to be the organising spine of the subject.

**Large cardinals do not settle CH.** Measurable, supercompact and huge cardinals decide an enormous range of questions — projective determinacy, the regularity properties of definable sets of reals — but Levy and Solovay showed that small forcings preserve large cardinals, so any large cardinal axiom compatible with ZFC is compatible with both CH and its negation. The continuum's exact value is insensitive to the height of the universe.

**Forcing axioms.** Martin's Maximum and the Proper Forcing Axiom are strong generalisations of Martin's Axiom, and remarkably they *do* decide the continuum: both imply $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$. Woodin's $\Omega$-logic programme argued for the same value from a different direction, though he has since explored the opposite programme, an "ultimate $L$" in which a CH-deciding canonical inner model accommodates all large cardinals.

**The multiverse view.** Hamkins argues that the independence phenomenon is telling us there is no single universe of sets to be right about: CH is settled in each model and simply has different truth values across a well-understood multiverse of them, much as the parallel postulate is settled differently in different geometries.

:::note
Whatever one's philosophy, the working mathematics is unaffected in one important respect: everyday analysis, algebra and topology are carried out in ZFC and do not depend on CH. Where CH does surface — in questions about arbitrary sets of reals, Banach algebra homomorphisms, or the structure of $\beta\mathbb{N}$ — the honest statement of a result names the hypothesis it used.
:::

## From ordinary induction to well-ordering

<!-- section: ordinary-induction -->

Everything in the last half of the chapter rests on a single structural fact, and it is worth isolating it before generalising: induction works on $\mathbb{N}$ *because* $\mathbb{N}$ is well-ordered.

:::theorem[Induction and well-ordering are equivalent on the naturals]{#induction-equivalence}
For $S\subseteq\mathbb{N}$, the following two principles are equivalent:

1. (Induction) If $0\in S$ and $n\in S \Rightarrow n+1\in S$ for all $n$, then $S=\mathbb{N}$.
2. (Well-ordering) Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{N}$ has a least element.
:::

:::proof
**(2) $\Rightarrow$ (1).** Assume $S$ satisfies the induction hypotheses but $S \neq \mathbb{N}$. Then $\mathbb{N}\setminus S$ is nonempty, so it has a least element $m$. Since $0\in S$, $m \neq 0$, so $m = k+1$ for some $k$, and $k < m$ forces $k\in S$; but then $k+1 = m \in S$, a contradiction.

**(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2).** Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ be nonempty with no least element, and let $S = \{n : \text{no } k \le n \text{ lies in } A\}$. Then $0\in S$, since $0\in A$ would make $0$ least. If $n\in S$ then no $k\le n$ is in $A$; if $n+1$ were in $A$ it would be least in $A$, so $n+1\in S$. By induction $S = \mathbb{N}$, whence $A=\varnothing$ — contradiction.
:::

The proof of (2) $\Rightarrow$ (1) used only one property of $\mathbb{N}$: that a nonempty set of naturals has a least element. It never used the successor structure, and in particular it never used that every nonzero element has an immediate predecessor. That is the hinge. Drop the predecessor assumption and the same argument still runs — on any well-ordered set at all.

## Transfinite induction and recursion

<!-- section: transfinite-version -->

:::theorem[Transfinite induction on a well-ordered set]{#transfinite-induction}
Let $(X,<)$ be well-ordered and $S\subseteq X$. Suppose for every $a\in X$:

$$X_{<a}\subseteq S \implies a\in S.$$

Then $S=X$. In particular, for a class $C$ of ordinals: if every ordinal all of whose predecessors lie in $C$ is itself in $C$, then $C$ contains every ordinal.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $S\neq X$. Then $X\setminus S$ is a nonempty subset of a well-ordered set, so it has a least element $a$. Minimality means every $b < a$ lies in $S$, i.e. $X_{<a}\subseteq S$. The hypothesis then gives $a\in S$, contradicting $a\in X\setminus S$. Hence $X\setminus S=\varnothing$.

For the class version applied to all ordinals, note that if some ordinal failed to be in $C$, say $\alpha\notin C$, then $(\alpha+1)\setminus C$ is a nonempty *set* of ordinals and *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered* supplies a least element; the same argument applies. This detour through a set is needed because $\mathrm{Ord}$ is not a set, by the *Burali-Forti paradox*.
:::

:::theorem[Transfinite recursion]{#transfinite-recursion}
Let $G$ be a class function assigning a set to every function whose domain is an ordinal. Then there is a unique class function $F$ on the ordinals with

$$F(\alpha) = G\big(F\restriction\alpha\big) \quad \text{for every ordinal } \alpha.$$
:::

:::proof
Call $f$ an *$\alpha$-approximation* if $f$ is a function with domain $\alpha$ and $f(\beta) = G(f\restriction\beta)$ for all $\beta<\alpha$.

*Uniqueness of approximations.* If $f$ and $g$ are $\alpha$- and $\beta$-approximations with $\alpha\le\beta$, they agree on $\alpha$: otherwise the least $\gamma$ where they differ satisfies $f\restriction\gamma = g\restriction\gamma$, so $f(\gamma)=G(f\restriction\gamma)=G(g\restriction\gamma)=g(\gamma)$, a contradiction.

*Existence.* By transfinite induction on $\alpha$, an $\alpha$-approximation exists for every $\alpha$. For $\alpha = 0$ take the empty function. If $f$ is an $\alpha$-approximation, then $f\cup\{(\alpha, G(f))\}$ is an $(\alpha+1)$-approximation. If $\alpha$ is a limit and approximations exist below, replacement collects them into a set, and by uniqueness their union is a single function, which is an $\alpha$-approximation.

Now define $F(\alpha) = f(\alpha)$ where $f$ is any $(\alpha+1)$-approximation; uniqueness makes this well-defined and the recursion equation holds by construction. If $F'$ also satisfied it, the least $\alpha$ with $F(\alpha)\neq F'(\alpha)$ gives the same contradiction as above.
:::

:::note
Induction *proves* statements about all ordinals; recursion *defines* objects for all ordinals. They are separate theorems and the second is the harder one, because a definition has to produce a set at each stage and the stages form a proper class. Every use of the phrase "define by transfinite recursion" in this chapter — the aleph function, the beths, Gödel's $L$, ordinal arithmetic — is an appeal to this theorem.
:::

## Why the limit step is the whole story

<!-- section: limit-steps -->

Ordinary induction has one inductive case. Transfinite induction, once you use it to *define* something, has two, and the second has no analogue below $\omega$.

Written in the three-case form, a transfinite recursion specifies:

- **Base.** What $F(0)$ is.
- **Successor step.** How to get $F(\alpha+1)$ from $F(\alpha)$ — the familiar case, where there is a single previous value to work with.
- **Limit step.** How to get $F(\lambda)$ from the whole family $\{F(\alpha):\alpha<\lambda\}$ — where there is *no* previous value, only an infinite collection of earlier ones.

The limit step almost always takes a union or a supremum, because those are the operations that turn a family into a single object without picking a last member: $\aleph_\lambda = \sup_{\alpha<\lambda}\aleph_\alpha$, $L_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha$, $\alpha+\lambda = \sup_{\beta<\lambda}(\alpha+\beta)$.

:::pitfall
The classic failed transfinite argument is: "each step preserves property $P$, so $P$ holds at every stage." It does not follow. Each finite stage of the recursion $A_n = \{0,1,\dots,n\}$ is finite, and $A_\omega = \bigcup_n A_n = \omega$ is not. *Preservation under successors does not imply preservation at limits*, and any property not closed under unions of increasing chains will break exactly at $\lambda=\omega$. Whenever a transfinite proof feels too easy, check what it claims at limits.
:::

:::example[A property that survives successors but dies at $\omega$]
Define $A_0=\varnothing$ and $A_{\alpha+1} = A_\alpha\cup\{\alpha\}$, with unions at limits. Which of "finite", "countable", "well-ordered by $\in$" survive to $A_{\omega_1}$?
:::

:::solution
1. Finiteness dies at $\omega$: every $A_n$ is finite with $n$ elements, but $A_\omega = \omega$ is infinite. The successor step adds one element, which preserves finiteness; the union of infinitely many finite sets need not be finite.
2. Countability survives to $A_\omega$ and every $A_\alpha$ with $\alpha<\omega_1$, but dies at $\omega_1$: $A_{\omega_1}=\omega_1$ is uncountable. It survives as far as it does only because a countable union of countable sets is countable — the limit step is exactly where the theorem is needed.
3. Being well-ordered by $\in$ survives everywhere, because $A_\alpha=\alpha$ is always an ordinal and a union of ordinals is an ordinal by *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered*. This is a property genuinely closed under increasing unions, and that is why the recursion is sound.
:::

## Applications of transfinite methods

<!-- section: applications -->

:::example[The cumulative hierarchy]
Define the von Neumann hierarchy $V_\alpha$ by transfinite recursion and state what it says about the universe of sets.
:::

:::solution
1. Base: $V_0=\varnothing$. Successor: $V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal{P}(V_\alpha)$. Limit: $V_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha$.
2. The axiom of foundation is equivalent, over the rest of ZF, to the statement that every set lies in some $V_\alpha$; the least such $\alpha$ is the set's **rank**.
3. So the universe is stratified in ordinal-many layers, each obtained from the previous by taking all subsets, and the ordinals index the layers. The finite levels are small — $|V_n|$ is a tower of exponentials — while $|V_{\omega+n}| = \beth_n$.
:::

:::example[Cantor–Bendixson: a transfinite derivative]
Explain how transfinite recursion proves that every closed subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is the union of a perfect set and a countable set.
:::

:::solution
1. For closed $C$ let $C' $ be its set of limit points. Recurse: $C^{(0)}=C$, $C^{(\alpha+1)}=(C^{(\alpha)})'$, and $C^{(\lambda)}=\bigcap_{\alpha<\lambda}C^{(\alpha)}$ at limits.
2. The sequence is decreasing, and at each successor stage the points removed are isolated in $C^{(\alpha)}$, hence each carries a rational interval meeting $C^{(\alpha)}$ only in it; so only countably many points are discarded per stage.
3. The sequence must stabilise at some countable ordinal: strictly decreasing would give uncountably many distinct stages, each discarding a nonempty set with its own rational witness, and there are only $\aleph_0$ rationals. The stabilising stage $\alpha_0 < \omega_1$ is the **Cantor–Bendixson rank**.
4. At stabilisation $C^{(\alpha_0)}$ equals its own derivative, i.e. is perfect, and the discarded remainder is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable. So closed sets satisfy CH outright, with no extra axiom.
:::

:::example[Goodstein sequences and $\varepsilon_0$]
Why does every Goodstein sequence reach $0$, and why is ordinal notation the natural tool?
:::

:::solution
1. Write $n$ in hereditary base $b$, bump the base to $b+1$, subtract $1$, and repeat. The integer values explode — starting from $4$ the sequence climbs past $3\cdot 2^{402653211}$ — so no naive bound works.
2. Replace every base by $\omega$ to obtain an ordinal in Cantor normal form below $\varepsilon_0$. Bumping the base does not change this ordinal, since the base has been replaced anyway, and subtracting $1$ strictly decreases it.
3. So the associated ordinals form a strictly decreasing sequence of ordinals. By *Every set of ordinals is well-ordered* there is no infinite strictly decreasing sequence, so the process terminates, which means the integer sequence reaches $0$.
4. Kirby and Paris showed this statement is not provable in Peano arithmetic: the argument genuinely needs induction up to $\varepsilon_0$, which is exactly the ordinal strength PA lacks.
:::

:::summary
The chapter's spine, in order of dependence. **Von Neumann ordinals** are transitive sets well-ordered by $\in$; their elements are ordinals, they are linearly ordered by $\in$, and every set of them has a least element — so they are well-ordered but, by **Burali-Forti**, too many to be a set. Every well-order is uniquely an ordinal (**Representation of well-orders**, via replacement). Ordinal arithmetic is concatenation: associative, distributive on the left, and non-commutative, with $1+\omega=\omega<\omega+1$ and $2\cdot\omega=\omega<\omega\cdot2$. On the size side, bijections define cardinality, **Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein** makes $\le$ antisymmetric, $|\mathbb{N}|=|\mathbb{Z}|=|\mathbb{Q}|=\aleph_0$, and **Cantor's theorem** gives $2^{\aleph_0}>\aleph_0$ with no largest cardinal. **Hartogs** builds the aleph ladder without choice; with choice, **absorption** makes $+$ and $\cdot$ trivial and leaves exponentiation as the only hard operation. **CH** asks whether $2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_1$: Gödel's $L$ (1940) shows it is consistent, Cohen's forcing (1963) shows its negation is too, so it is independent. Finally **transfinite induction** and **transfinite recursion** follow from well-ordering, and their limit clause — take a union or a supremum — is the case with no finite analogue and the case every flawed transfinite argument gets wrong.
:::

## Common Mistakes

<!-- section: common-mistakes -->

1. **Confusing ordinal and cardinal arithmetic.** $\omega+1 > \omega$ as ordinals, while $\aleph_0 + 1 = \aleph_0$ as cardinals. The notation is shared; the operations are not. Watch whether the letters are $\alpha,\beta$ or $\kappa,\lambda$.

2. **Assuming ordinal addition commutes.** $1+\omega=\omega$ but $\omega+1>\omega$. Adding on the left is absorbed; adding on the right is not. The same asymmetry makes right cancellation fail.

3. **Assuming ordinal multiplication commutes.** $2\cdot\omega=\omega$ but $\omega\cdot2=\omega+\omega>\omega$. Remember the convention: $\alpha\cdot\beta$ is $\beta$ copies of $\alpha$.

4. **Thinking non-commutativity means non-associativity.** Both operations *are* associative, and multiplication distributes over addition on the left. Only commutativity and right distributivity fail.

5. **Calling every infinite ordinal a limit.** $\omega+1$, $\omega\cdot2+3$ and $\omega^\omega+5$ are infinite successors. Limit means "no immediate predecessor", not "infinite".

6. **Treating $\mathrm{Ord}$ as a set.** It is a proper class; assuming otherwise gives the Burali-Forti contradiction directly. The same applies to the class of all cardinals and the class of all sets.

7. **Believing every ordinal is a cardinal.** $\omega+1$, $\omega\cdot2$ and $\omega^\omega$ all have cardinality $\aleph_0$ and so are not initial ordinals. Cardinals are exactly the ordinals not equinumerous with anything smaller.

8. **Concluding two sets differ in size because one is a proper subset.** $\mathbb{N}\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}$ and $|\mathbb{N}|=|\mathbb{Z}|$. Being equinumerous with a proper subset is the *definition* of infinite, not a paradox.

9. **Assuming density implies uncountability.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ and countable. Density is an order property; cardinality does not see order.

10. **Using "a countable union of countable sets is countable" without flagging choice.** The theorem needs countable choice; the enumerations exist individually, but selecting one for each index at once does not follow from ZF.

11. **Writing $|\mathbb{R}|=\aleph_1$ as if it were a theorem.** $|\mathbb{R}|=2^{\aleph_0}=\beth_1$ is a theorem; that it equals $\aleph_1$ is CH, and CH is independent.

12. **Saying CH is unknown.** It is independent: ZFC proves neither it nor its negation, and this is itself a theorem. Nothing further will be discovered that settles it within ZFC.

13. **Expecting large cardinals to settle CH.** By Levy–Solovay, small forcings preserve large cardinals, so no large cardinal axiom decides the continuum.

14. **Omitting the limit step in a transfinite argument.** Proving the successor step proves nothing at $\omega$. Finiteness is preserved by every successor step of $A_{\alpha+1}=A_\alpha\cup\{\alpha\}$ and fails at the first limit.

15. **Confusing transfinite induction with transfinite recursion.** Induction proves statements; recursion defines functions and needs replacement to collect the approximations. Citing the wrong one hides where the real work is.
