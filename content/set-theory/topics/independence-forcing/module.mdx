# Independence & Forcing

Every other chapter of mathematics answers questions. This one explains why some questions have no answer — not "no answer yet", but provably no answer from the axioms we have agreed to use.

The question that forced the issue is Cantor's. He proved that $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable, so $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$, and then asked whether there is a set of reals whose size falls strictly between $\aleph_0$ and $2^{\aleph_0}$. His guess — that there is not — is the **continuum hypothesis**, and it stood first on Hilbert's 1900 list. It took sixty-three years and two completely different inventions to settle, and the settlement was not "yes" or "no". Gödel showed in 1938 that CH cannot be refuted from the axioms of ZFC, by exhibiting a sparse inner universe $L$ in which it holds. Cohen showed in 1963 that CH cannot be proved from them either, by inventing **forcing**, a method for enlarging a model of set theory in a controlled way until CH fails. Between them, CH is *independent*.

Two techniques run through everything here, and they are exact mirror images. An **inner model** is built by throwing sets away: keep only the sets some construction can name, and you obtain a thinner universe which still satisfies every axiom, but in which the continuum has had no room to grow. A **forcing extension** is built by adding sets: choose a partially ordered set of finite approximations, adjoin an idealised object that no approximation alone describes, and check that the axioms survive. Thinning gives consistency of CH; fattening gives consistency of its negation. Neither can decide CH, and that is the theorem.

A warning about the shape of the arguments. Nothing in this chapter proves CH, or $\neg$CH, or the consistency of ZFC. Everything is **relative**: statements of the form "if ZFC is consistent, then so is ZFC + CH". Gödel's second incompleteness theorem forbids anything stronger, and one section below derives exactly why. Keeping the conditional visible is not pedantry — it is the difference between a correct theorem and a claim that is provably false.

Throughout, ZFC is Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with choice, ZF is the same without choice, $\mathrm{Ord}$ is the class of ordinals, $V_\alpha$ is the cumulative hierarchy, and $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ abbreviates the arithmetical sentence asserting that the theory $T$ proves no contradiction. Lower-case $p, q, r$ always denote conditions in a forcing poset; $M$ always denotes a ground model; $G$ always denotes a generic filter.

## Independence: what the word means

<!-- section: what-is-independence -->

Independence is a statement about a *theory*, not about a mathematical object. It says that a particular list of axioms does not settle a particular question, and it is proved by producing two worlds that obey the axioms and disagree about the question.

:::definition[Independence]{#independence-def}
Let $T$ be a first-order theory and $\varphi$ a sentence in its language. We say $\varphi$ is **independent of** $T$ if

$$T \nvdash \varphi \quad \text{and} \quad T \nvdash \neg\varphi.$$

Equivalently, $T$ **decides** $\varphi$ if it proves one of the two, and $\varphi$ is independent exactly when $T$ does not decide it.
:::

Written this way, independence is a claim about the non-existence of a proof, and non-existence claims about proofs are hopeless to attack directly: there are infinitely many candidate derivations and no way to inspect them all. The whole subject rests on converting the claim into a claim about *models*, where ordinary mathematical construction can get a grip.

:::theorem[Independence is a statement about models]{#independence-models}
Let $T$ be consistent. Then $\varphi$ is independent of $T$ if and only if both $T + \varphi$ and $T + \neg\varphi$ have models.
:::

:::proof
Suppose first that both $T + \varphi$ and $T + \neg\varphi$ have models. If $T \vdash \varphi$ then $\varphi$ is true in every model of $T$, by soundness; but the model of $T + \neg\varphi$ is a model of $T$ in which $\varphi$ fails, a contradiction. So $T \nvdash \varphi$, and the symmetric argument with the model of $T + \varphi$ gives $T \nvdash \neg\varphi$. Hence $\varphi$ is independent.

Conversely, suppose $\varphi$ is independent of $T$. If $T + \varphi$ had no model then, by Gödel's completeness theorem in its contrapositive form — a theory with no model is inconsistent — the theory $T + \varphi$ would be inconsistent, so $T + \varphi \vdash \bot$, and by the deduction theorem $T \vdash \neg\varphi$, contradicting independence. So $T + \varphi$ has a model, and likewise $T + \neg\varphi$.
:::

The proof used the completeness theorem in exactly one direction and soundness in the other, and it is worth being explicit about which does what. Soundness ("provable implies true in all models") is what lets a single model refute a proof. Completeness ("consistent implies satisfiable") is what lets a failure to prove be witnessed by a model. Without completeness, building models would establish nothing about provability.

:::intuition
Think of the axioms as the rules of a board game and $\varphi$ as a question about how a game can end. Independence means the rulebook permits both endings. Nobody is cheating in either play-through; the rulebook is simply silent, and no amount of re-reading it will produce an answer. To decide the question you must adopt a new rule — and then the interesting question becomes which new rule deserves to be adopted.
:::

:::example[Independence outside set theory]
Show that the sentence "for all $x, y$, $x \cdot y = y \cdot x$" is independent of the axioms of group theory.
:::

:::solution
1. The abelian group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a model of the group axioms in which commutativity holds.
2. The symmetric group $S_3$ is a model of the group axioms in which it fails, since $(12)(13) \neq (13)(12)$.
3. Two models of the axioms disagree about the sentence, so by the theorem *Independence is a statement about models* the sentence is independent of group theory.
4. Note how ordinary this is. Independence is only startling for set theory because we expected ZFC to be a description of one fixed universe rather than a list of constraints admitting many.
:::

:::remark
The group-theoretic example also shows what independence does **not** mean. It does not mean the question is meaningless, nor that it has no answer in any particular group. Every specific group either is or is not abelian. Likewise, if one believes in a single intended universe of sets, CH has a definite truth value there; independence says only that ZFC does not pin it down.
:::

## Gödel and Cohen: the two halves of the answer

<!-- section: godel-and-cohen -->

The independence of CH was proved in two installments, twenty-five years apart, by methods with nothing in common except the goal.

:::theorem[Gödel, 1938: the consistency of CH]{#godel-theorem}
If ZF is consistent, then so is $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH}$. Formally, the implication

$$\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF}) \implies \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH})$$

is a theorem of ZF — indeed of a very weak fragment of arithmetic.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
The full argument cannot fit in a chapter, and the honest thing is to say so and name its moving parts. It goes: define by transfinite recursion the class $L$ of constructible sets; prove in ZF that $L$ satisfies every ZF axiom (this is a check of eight axioms, each a separate closure argument); prove that $L$ carries a definable well-ordering, which gives AC inside $L$; prove the condensation lemma, that an elementary submodel of $L_\alpha$ collapses to some $L_\beta$; deduce from condensation that every constructible subset of $\omega$ appears by stage $\omega_1$, which gives CH in $L$, and the same argument one level up gives GCH. What is assumed is only $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF})$; what is produced is a model, namely $L$ as computed inside any model of ZF. The details occupy roughly a chapter of Kunen's *Set Theory* or Jech's *Set Theory*, chapter 13. Sections below prove the parts that are genuinely short — transitivity of $L$, the increasing hierarchy, the definable well-ordering — and label the rest as quoted.
:::

:::theorem[Cohen, 1963: the consistency of the negation of CH]{#cohen-theorem}
If ZFC is consistent, then so is $\mathrm{ZFC} + \neg\mathrm{CH}$. More precisely, for any cardinal $\kappa$ of the ground model with $\mathrm{cf}(\kappa) > \omega$ there is a forcing extension in which $2^{\aleph_0} = \kappa$.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
Take a countable transitive model $M$ of (enough of) ZFC; force with the poset of finite partial functions from $\kappa \times \omega$ to $2$; the generic filter codes $\kappa$ distinct reals; the poset has the countable chain condition, so cardinals are preserved and $\kappa$ is still a cardinal in the extension; a counting argument on *nice names* bounds $2^{\aleph_0}$ from above by $\kappa$. The assumptions are $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ and the forcing theorem, stated below and itself quoted. Cohen's original papers are the 1963 and 1964 *PNAS* notes; the modern treatment is Kunen, chapters IV–VII.
:::

Putting the two together is immediate, and the combination is what the word *independence* refers to in practice.

:::corollary[CH is independent of ZFC]{#ch-independent}
If ZFC is consistent, then CH is independent of ZFC.
:::

:::proof
Assume $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$. By the theorem *Gödel, 1938: the consistency of CH*, the theory $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{CH}$ is consistent, hence has a model by completeness. By the theorem *Cohen, 1963: the consistency of the negation of CH*, the theory $\mathrm{ZFC} + \neg\mathrm{CH}$ is consistent, hence also has a model. Two models of ZFC disagree about CH, so by the theorem *Independence is a statement about models*, CH is independent of ZFC.
:::

:::intuition
Gödel and Cohen move in opposite directions from the same starting point. Gödel shrinks the universe until the only subsets of $\omega$ left are the ones a formula can describe, and there are exactly $\aleph_1$ of those; CH becomes true by starvation. Cohen grows the universe by pouring in $\aleph_2$ new reals that no formula of the old universe describes; CH becomes false by flooding. Neither move breaks an axiom, which is the entire content of both theorems.
:::

:::remark
Cohen received the Fields Medal in 1966 for forcing — still the only one awarded for work in mathematical logic. The method turned out to be far more general than the problem that prompted it: within a decade it had settled Suslin's hypothesis, the Whitehead problem, the Kaplansky conjecture on Banach algebras, and Borel's conjecture, and today a set theorist meeting a new combinatorial question about $\omega_1$ expects independence as the default outcome.
:::

## The formal framework: theories, models and consistency

<!-- section: formal-framework -->

Before any of this can be made precise we need to be careful about a distinction that independence arguments live or die by: the difference between working *in* a theory and reasoning *about* it.

:::definition[Consistency and its arithmetisation]{#con-def}
A theory $T$ is **consistent** if there is no sentence $\varphi$ with $T \vdash \varphi$ and $T \vdash \neg\varphi$. When $T$ is recursively axiomatised, the relation "$d$ is a $T$-proof of $\varphi$" is decidable, so it can be expressed by a formula of arithmetic, and

$$\mathrm{Con}(T) \; :\equiv \; \neg \exists d \, \big( \mathrm{Prf}_T(d, \ulcorner \bot \urcorner) \big)$$

is a single arithmetical sentence. Because it is arithmetical, $\mathrm{Con}(T)$ is the kind of thing a theory can prove or fail to prove about itself.
:::

That last sentence is the hinge. Gödel numbering turns a claim about proofs into a claim about natural numbers, and a theory strong enough to talk about natural numbers is thereby strong enough to talk about itself. The consequence is the central limitation of the subject.

:::theorem[Gödel's second incompleteness theorem]{#second-incompleteness}
Let $T$ be a consistent, recursively axiomatised theory interpreting a modest amount of arithmetic (Robinson's $Q$ suffices, and ZFC vastly exceeds it). Then

$$T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T).$$
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
The argument constructs by the diagonal lemma a sentence $\gamma$ with $T \vdash \gamma \leftrightarrow \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner \gamma \urcorner)$, so $\gamma$ says "I am not provable". The first incompleteness theorem shows $T \nvdash \gamma$ when $T$ is consistent. One then verifies that this *proof* can itself be carried out inside $T$, which requires the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions on $\mathrm{Prov}_T$, and obtains $T \vdash \mathrm{Con}(T) \to \gamma$. Since $T \nvdash \gamma$, it follows that $T \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(T)$. What is assumed is the derivability conditions, whose verification is a long but routine formalisation; the standard reference is Boolos, *The Logic of Provability*, or Hájek–Pudlák.
:::

The consequence we actually need is immediate, and it is what makes every theorem in this chapter conditional.

:::corollary[ZFC cannot prove its own consistency]{#zfc-not-con}
If ZFC is consistent, then $\mathrm{ZFC} \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$.
:::

:::proof
ZFC is recursively axiomatised — its axioms are the finitely many individual axioms together with two decidable schemes — and it interprets arithmetic, since the finite von Neumann ordinals with their induced operations form a model of Peano arithmetic inside ZFC. So ZFC meets the hypotheses of the theorem *Gödel's second incompleteness theorem*, and if it is consistent then it does not prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$.
:::

:::corollary[No absolute consistency proof for a set theory]{#no-model-in-zfc}
If ZFC is consistent, then ZFC does not prove that there exists a model of ZFC.
:::

:::proof
Reason inside ZFC. Suppose ZFC proved the sentence "there exists a set $\mathcal{M}$ and a relation $E$ on it with $(\mathcal{M}, E) \models \mathrm{ZFC}$". The completeness theorem is provable in ZFC, and one of its consequences — soundness in the arithmetised form — gives that the existence of a model of a theory implies the consistency of that theory. Hence ZFC would prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$, contradicting the corollary *ZFC cannot prove its own consistency*.
:::

This is the reason the phrase "ZFC proves that CH is independent" is, read literally, false, and it is worth stating the correct version once, sharply, because almost every informal account of the subject gets it wrong.

:::proposition[What an independence theorem actually asserts]{#independence-really}
The sentence "CH is independent of ZFC" is not a theorem of ZFC. What is a theorem of ZFC is the implication

$$\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}) \implies \text{"CH is independent of ZFC"}.$$

Equivalently, the theory $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ proves that CH is independent of ZFC.
:::

:::proof
Independence of CH implies in particular that $\mathrm{ZFC} \nvdash \mathrm{CH} \wedge \neg\mathrm{CH}$, that is, it implies $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$. So if ZFC proved the independence of CH outright, ZFC would prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$, which the corollary *ZFC cannot prove its own consistency* forbids (assuming ZFC consistent). The positive half is the corollary *CH is independent of ZFC*, whose proof assumed exactly $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ and used nothing else beyond ZFC.
:::

:::pitfall
"Cohen proved CH is false" and "Gödel proved CH is true" are both wrong, and they are wrong in the same way: each takes a *relative consistency* result and reads it as a truth claim. Cohen produced a model where CH fails. Gödel produced a model where it holds. Neither model is distinguished as the real universe by anything either man proved.
:::

:::example[Reading a relative consistency statement]
The literature contains the statement $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \exists \text{ inaccessible}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$. Which direction does this go, and why is the converse not available?
:::

:::solution
1. The stated implication says: if the stronger theory has no contradiction, neither does the weaker. That direction is trivial in one sense — any model of the stronger theory is a model of ZFC — and it is provable in weak arithmetic by transforming a ZFC-contradiction into a contradiction of the stronger theory.
2. The converse would say $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \exists\text{ inaccessible})$. This is **not** provable, because, as shown below in the corollary *ZFC cannot prove an inaccessible exists*, an inaccessible yields a model of ZFC and hence gives $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$; the converse implication would therefore let $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}))$, violating the second incompleteness theorem applied to that theory.
3. The asymmetry is exactly what the phrase *consistency strength* names. Final point: implications go downward in strength for free and upward never.
:::

## A catalogue of independent statements

<!-- section: examples -->

CH is the famous case, but it is not an isolated pathology. Once forcing was available the list grew quickly, and it now covers questions from general topology, algebra, analysis and combinatorics that were posed with no suspicion that logic was involved.

:::definition[The continuum and its cardinal]{#continuum-def}
Write $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0} = |\mathbb{R}|$ for the **cardinality of the continuum**. The **continuum hypothesis** CH is the sentence $\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_1$; the **generalised continuum hypothesis** GCH is the sentence $2^{\aleph_\alpha} = \aleph_{\alpha+1}$ for every ordinal $\alpha$.
:::

Here is a representative sample of statements independent of ZFC, chosen to show the range.

1. **Suslin's hypothesis.** Every complete dense linear order without endpoints in which every family of disjoint intervals is countable is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$. It fails in $L$, where a Suslin tree can be constructed from $\diamondsuit$, and holds under Martin's axiom plus $\neg$CH.
2. **The Whitehead problem.** Every abelian group $A$ with $\mathrm{Ext}^1(A,\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ is free. Shelah proved in 1974 that it holds under $V = L$ and fails under MA $+ \neg$CH — the first independence result to land squarely inside mainstream algebra.
3. **The Borel conjecture.** Every strong measure zero set of reals is countable. Laver forced it in 1976; it fails under CH.
4. **Kaplansky's conjecture.** Every algebra homomorphism from $C(X)$ into a Banach algebra is continuous. Solovay and Woodin showed the answer depends on the model.
5. **$2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_{17}$.** By the theorem *Cohen, 1963: the consistency of the negation of CH* this is consistent, as is $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_{\omega+1}$, but *not* $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_\omega$ — see the theorem *König's restriction on the continuum* below.

:::example[Which values of the continuum are excluded]
Among $\aleph_1$, $\aleph_2$, $\aleph_\omega$, $\aleph_{\omega_1}$, which can consistently equal $2^{\aleph_0}$?
:::

:::solution
1. $\aleph_1$: yes, this is CH, consistent by Gödel.
2. $\aleph_2$: yes, by Cohen forcing with $\kappa = \aleph_2$, which has uncountable cofinality.
3. $\aleph_\omega$: **no**. Its cofinality is $\omega$, and the theorem *König's restriction on the continuum* proved below rules out $\mathrm{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) = \omega$.
4. $\aleph_{\omega_1}$: yes. Its cofinality is $\omega_1 > \omega$, so it is a permitted value.
5. Final answer: all except $\aleph_\omega$.
:::

:::remark
The pattern is worth noticing. Statements about *definable* or *small* objects — arithmetic, Borel sets, compact metric spaces — tend to be decided by ZFC. Statements that quantify over arbitrary subsets of $\mathbb{R}$, or over arbitrary structures of size $\aleph_1$, tend to be independent. Forcing is a technique for moving exactly those objects around while leaving the arithmetic of the ground model untouched.
:::

## Constructible sets: building a universe out of definitions

<!-- section: constructible-sets -->

Gödel's model is obtained by replacing the power-set operation with something far stingier. At each stage, instead of taking *all* subsets of what has been built, take only the subsets that a first-order formula can single out using parameters already present.

:::definition[The definable power set]{#def-power}
Let $X$ be a set. The **definable power set** of $X$ is

$$\mathrm{Def}(X) = \{\, Y \subseteq X : Y = \{ z \in X : (X, \in) \models \varphi(z, a_1, \dots, a_n) \} \ \text{for some formula } \varphi \text{ and } a_1, \dots, a_n \in X \,\}.$$

Only formulas with quantifiers ranging over $X$ itself are allowed, and only parameters drawn from $X$.
:::

:::definition[The constructible hierarchy]{#l-hierarchy}
Define by transfinite recursion

$$L_0 = \emptyset, \qquad L_{\alpha+1} = \mathrm{Def}(L_\alpha), \qquad L_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha < \lambda} L_\alpha \ \text{ for limit } \lambda,$$

and set $L = \bigcup_{\alpha \in \mathrm{Ord}} L_\alpha$. Members of $L$ are the **constructible sets**, and $V = L$ abbreviates the sentence "every set is constructible".
:::

:::intuition
$V_{\alpha+1} = \mathcal{P}(V_\alpha)$ takes every subset, including the ones nobody can describe; $L_{\alpha+1} = \mathrm{Def}(L_\alpha)$ takes only the describable ones. The two hierarchies agree at the finite stages, because every subset of a finite set is definable by listing its members. They part company at stage $\omega + 1$: $V_{\omega+1}$ has $2^{\aleph_0}$ members, while $L_{\omega+1}$ has only $\aleph_0$, since there are only countably many formulas with parameters from the countable set $L_\omega$.
:::

The first two facts about the hierarchy are the ones every later argument uses, and both are short.

:::proposition[The hierarchy is increasing and each level is transitive]{#l-increasing}
For all ordinals $\alpha \le \beta$ we have $L_\alpha \subseteq L_\beta$, and every $L_\alpha$ is a transitive set with $L_\alpha \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$.
:::

:::proof
We prove by induction on $\alpha$ that $L_\alpha$ is transitive and that $L_\alpha \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$; the general monotonicity then follows by a second induction on $\beta$.

*Base.* $L_0 = \emptyset$ is transitive vacuously.

*Successor.* Assume $L_\alpha$ is transitive. First, $L_\alpha \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$: given $x \in L_\alpha$, transitivity gives $x \subseteq L_\alpha$, and $x = \{ z \in L_\alpha : z \in x \}$ is defined over $L_\alpha$ by the formula $z \in a$ with the parameter $a = x \in L_\alpha$. So $x \in \mathrm{Def}(L_\alpha) = L_{\alpha+1}$. Next, $L_{\alpha+1}$ is transitive: any $Y \in L_{\alpha+1}$ satisfies $Y \subseteq L_\alpha$ by definition of $\mathrm{Def}$, and $L_\alpha \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$ as just shown, so $Y \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$.

*Limit.* If $\lambda$ is a limit and each $L_\alpha$ for $\alpha < \lambda$ is transitive, then $L_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda} L_\alpha$ is a union of transitive sets, hence transitive; and $L_\lambda \subseteq L_{\lambda+1}$ by the successor argument applied to $L_\lambda$.

For monotonicity, fix $\alpha$ and induct on $\beta \ge \alpha$: the case $\beta = \alpha$ is trivial, the successor step is $L_\beta \subseteq L_{\beta+1}$, and at a limit $\lambda > \alpha$ we have $L_\alpha \subseteq L_\lambda$ directly from the definition of the union.
:::

:::corollary[$L$ is a transitive class containing every ordinal]{#l-transitive}
$L$ is transitive, and $\alpha \in L_{\alpha+1}$ for every ordinal $\alpha$; consequently $\mathrm{Ord} \subseteq L$.
:::

:::proof
Transitivity: if $x \in L$ then $x \in L_\alpha$ for some $\alpha$, and $L_\alpha$ is transitive by the proposition *The hierarchy is increasing and each level is transitive*, so $x \subseteq L_\alpha \subseteq L$.

For the ordinals, induct on $\alpha$. Suppose $\beta \in L_{\beta+1}$ for all $\beta < \alpha$. Since the hierarchy is increasing, $\beta \in L_\alpha$ for every $\beta < \alpha$, so $\alpha \subseteq L_\alpha$. Now being an ordinal — being transitive and linearly ordered by $\in$ — is expressible by a formula whose quantifiers can be restricted to any transitive set, and $L_\alpha$ is transitive, so

$$\alpha = \{ z \in L_\alpha : z \text{ is an ordinal and } z \in \alpha \}$$

exhibits $\alpha$ as a member of $\mathrm{Def}(L_\alpha) = L_{\alpha+1}$, using the parameter $\alpha$ once it is known that $\alpha \subseteq L_\alpha$; alternatively, and without parameters, $\alpha = \{z \in L_\alpha : z \text{ is an ordinal}\}$, because an easy induction shows the ordinals in $L_\alpha$ are exactly those below $\alpha$.
:::

:::example[The first few levels]
Compute $L_0, L_1, L_2, L_3$ and their cardinalities.
:::

:::solution
1. $L_0 = \emptyset$, so $|L_0| = 0$.
2. $L_1 = \mathrm{Def}(\emptyset)$. The only subset of $\emptyset$ is $\emptyset$ itself, definable by $z \neq z$. So $L_1 = \{\emptyset\}$ and $|L_1| = 1$.
3. $L_2 = \mathrm{Def}(\{\emptyset\})$. Both subsets $\emptyset$ and $\{\emptyset\}$ are definable over a one-element set, so $L_2 = \{\emptyset, \{\emptyset\}\}$ and $|L_2| = 2$.
4. $L_3 = \mathrm{Def}(L_2)$ where $|L_2| = 2$; every one of the $4$ subsets of a two-element set is definable with parameters, so $|L_3| = 4$.
5. In general $|L_{n+1}| = 2^{|L_n|}$ for finite $n$, because on a finite set every subset is definable: the pattern is $0, 1, 2, 4, 16, 65536, \dots$. Final answer: $L_n$ has cardinality $0, 1, 2, 4$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3$.
:::

:::proposition[Finite levels agree with the cumulative hierarchy]{#l-finite}
For every $n < \omega$ we have $L_n = V_n$, and hence $L_\omega = V_\omega$ is the set of hereditarily finite sets.
:::

:::proof
Induct on $n$. Both are empty at $n = 0$. Suppose $L_n = V_n$; this set is finite, since $|V_{n+1}| = 2^{|V_n|}$ starting from $|V_0| = 0$. Every subset $Y$ of a finite transitive set $X$ is definable over $X$: writing $Y = \{a_1, \dots, a_k\}$, the formula $z = a_1 \vee \cdots \vee z = a_k$ with parameters $a_1, \dots, a_k \in X$ defines it, and $Y = \emptyset$ is defined by $z \neq z$. Hence $\mathrm{Def}(X) = \mathcal{P}(X)$ for finite $X$, giving $L_{n+1} = \mathcal{P}(L_n) = \mathcal{P}(V_n) = V_{n+1}$. Taking unions at $\omega$ gives $L_\omega = V_\omega$.
:::

:::pitfall
$L_{\omega+1} \neq V_{\omega+1}$, and the failure is total: $V_{\omega+1} = \mathcal{P}(\omega)$ has size $2^{\aleph_0}$, while $L_{\omega+1}$ is countable. The equality $\mathrm{Def} = \mathcal{P}$ is a purely finite phenomenon and evaporates at the first infinite stage.
:::

## Properties of $L$: absoluteness and the definable well-ordering

<!-- section: properties -->

Two features make $L$ useful. It is *canonical*: the construction, performed inside any model of ZF, yields the same thing again when performed inside its own output. And it is *orderly*: the construction hands you a well-ordering for free, so AC is not an extra assumption inside $L$ but a consequence.

:::definition[Absoluteness]{#absolute-def}
Let $M \subseteq N$ be transitive classes. A formula $\varphi(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is **absolute between $M$ and $N$** if for all $a_1,\dots,a_n \in M$,

$$M \models \varphi(a_1,\dots,a_n) \iff N \models \varphi(a_1,\dots,a_n).$$

A formula is $\Delta_0$ if all its quantifiers are bounded, of the form $\forall x \in y$ or $\exists x \in y$; every $\Delta_0$ formula is absolute between transitive classes.
:::

:::theorem[$V = L$ holds inside $L$]{#v-equals-l-absolute}
The construction of the hierarchy is absolute: for every transitive class $M$ satisfying ZF and containing all ordinals, and every ordinal $\alpha$, the set $L_\alpha$ computed inside $M$ equals the real $L_\alpha$. Consequently $L^L = L$, and $L \models V = L$.
:::

:::proof
The function $\alpha \mapsto L_\alpha$ is defined by transfinite recursion from the operation $X \mapsto \mathrm{Def}(X)$. Two things must be checked, and both are checks on complexity rather than on set theory.

First, $\mathrm{Def}$ is absolute between transitive models of ZF. Satisfaction for a *set* structure, $(X,\in) \models \varphi[\bar a]$, is defined by recursion on the finite formula $\varphi$ coded as a hereditarily finite object; the recursion only ever quantifies over subsets of $X$ and over elements of $V_\omega$, and its defining formula can be written in $\Delta_0$ form relative to parameters. Hence any transitive model of ZF computes the satisfaction relation of $(X, \in)$ correctly, and therefore computes $\mathrm{Def}(X)$ correctly. Note carefully that this is *not* the same as absoluteness of $\mathcal{P}(X)$, which fails badly; $\mathrm{Def}(X)$ is a set of subsets each of which is described by a formula, and describing is absolute while collecting all subsets is not.

Second, transfinite recursion is absolute when the underlying operation is and the model contains all ordinals. By induction on $\alpha$: if $M$ computes $L_\beta$ correctly for all $\beta < \alpha$, then at a successor it applies the absolute operation $\mathrm{Def}$ to a correct input, and at a limit it takes a union, which is absolute. Since $M$ has all ordinals, no stage is missed.

Applying this with $M = L$ — which is transitive by the corollary *$L$ is a transitive class containing every ordinal*, contains all ordinals, and satisfies ZF — gives $(L_\alpha)^L = L_\alpha$ for all $\alpha$, hence $L^L = \bigcup_\alpha (L_\alpha)^L = L$. So every set of $L$ is constructible from the point of view of $L$, that is, $L \models V = L$.
:::

The payoff is that $V = L$ can be adopted as an axiom without cost: it holds in a model that ZF builds for itself. The next theorem says that adopting it also buys choice.

:::theorem[$L$ satisfies the axiom of choice]{#l-satisfies-ac}
There is a formula $\varphi(x,y)$, with no parameters, which defines in $L$ a well-ordering $<_L$ of the whole class $L$. Consequently $L \models \mathrm{AC}$, and indeed $L \models$ "there is a definable well-ordering of the universe".
:::

:::proof
We construct $<_L$ by recursion on levels, well-ordering each $L_\alpha$ and arranging that each ordering extends the previous ones.

Fix a recursive enumeration $\varphi_0, \varphi_1, \varphi_2, \dots$ of all first-order formulas in the language of set theory, in order of their Gödel numbers. Suppose $<_\alpha$ is a well-ordering of $L_\alpha$. Every element of $L_{\alpha+1}$ arises as

$$Y = \{ z \in L_\alpha : (L_\alpha, \in) \models \varphi_i(z, a_1,\dots,a_n) \}$$

for at least one pair consisting of an index $i$ and a finite tuple $\bar a$ from $L_\alpha$. Assign to $Y$ the $<_\alpha$-least such description, where descriptions are compared first by the index $i$, then — among equal indices — by the tuple $\bar a$ in the lexicographic order induced by $<_\alpha$ on tuples of a fixed finite length, shorter tuples preceding longer ones. This is a well-ordering of the set of descriptions, being a finite lexicographic product of well-orderings, so a least description exists.

Now define $<_{\alpha+1}$ on $L_{\alpha+1}$ by: elements of $L_\alpha$ come first, ordered by $<_\alpha$; then the elements of $L_{\alpha+1} \setminus L_\alpha$, ordered by their least descriptions. Each piece is a well-ordering and the second is placed entirely after the first, so the sum is a well-ordering, and it extends $<_\alpha$ by construction.

At a limit $\lambda$ set $<_\lambda = \bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda} <_\alpha$. This is a linear order since the $<_\alpha$ form a chain under extension. It is a well-ordering: a nonempty $S \subseteq L_\lambda$ meets some $L_\alpha$ with $\alpha<\lambda$, and the $<_\alpha$-least element of $S \cap L_\alpha$ is $<_\lambda$-least in $S$, because every element of $S$ outside $L_\alpha$ was placed after all of $L_\alpha$.

Finally put $x <_L y$ if and only if $x$ appears before $y$ in $<_\alpha$ for the least $\alpha$ with $x, y \in L_\alpha$. The whole recursion is given by a single formula, so $<_L$ is definable without parameters, and by the theorem *$V = L$ holds inside $L$* it is computed the same way inside $L$.

For AC: given a set $x \in L$ of nonempty sets, the function sending each $u \in x$ to its $<_L$-least element is definable from $x$ and $<_L$, and $L$ satisfies enough of ZF to form it as a set. It is a choice function on $x$. Hence $L \models \mathrm{AC}$.
:::

:::intuition
The well-ordering comes from bookkeeping, not from magic. Every constructible set was built by some formula applied to some earlier sets, so tag each set with the cheapest recipe that produces it and sort by recipe. In $V$ this is impossible because most sets have no recipe at all; in $L$, having a recipe is the definition of membership.
:::

:::proposition[Levels of $L$ have the size of their index]{#l-cardinality}
For every infinite ordinal $\alpha$, $|L_\alpha| = |\alpha|$.
:::

:::proof
By induction. At $\alpha = \omega$: $L_\omega = V_\omega$ by the proposition *Finite levels agree with the cumulative hierarchy*, which is countably infinite, and $|\omega| = \aleph_0$.

Successor step. Suppose $|L_\alpha| = |\alpha|$ with $\alpha$ infinite. Each element of $L_{\alpha+1}$ is determined by a formula together with a finite tuple from $L_\alpha$. There are countably many formulas and $|L_\alpha|^{<\omega} = |\alpha|$ finite tuples, using the standard fact that $\kappa^{<\omega} = \kappa$ for infinite $\kappa$. So $|L_{\alpha+1}| \le \aleph_0 \cdot |\alpha| = |\alpha| = |\alpha+1|$. The reverse inequality holds because $L_\alpha \subseteq L_{\alpha+1}$.

Limit step. For limit $\lambda$, $|L_\lambda| \le \sum_{\alpha<\lambda} |L_\alpha| = \sum_{\alpha<\lambda}|\alpha| \le |\lambda| \cdot |\lambda| = |\lambda|$, and again $\lambda \subseteq L_\lambda$ gives the reverse.
:::

:::example[Why $L$ is not obviously small]
If $|L_{\omega_1}| = \aleph_1$, how can $L$ contain all the reals of any model in which $V = L$ holds?
:::

:::solution
1. Under $V = L$ every real is constructible, and the condensation lemma shows each constructible subset of $\omega$ appears in some $L_\alpha$ with $\alpha < \omega_1$.
2. Hence $\mathcal{P}(\omega) \cap L \subseteq L_{\omega_1}$, a set of size $\aleph_1$ by the proposition *Levels of $L$ have the size of their index*.
3. So there are at most $\aleph_1$ reals in $L$, and at least $\aleph_1$ since $\aleph_1 \le 2^{\aleph_0}$ always. Therefore $L \models 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$, which is CH.
4. Final answer: $L$ is small in cardinality but complete as a universe — it has *all* the reals it believes exist, because it does not believe in the others.
:::

## Relative consistency via inner models

<!-- section: consistency-results -->

The reason to build $L$ is the following transfer principle, which is the template for every inner-model consistency proof.

:::theorem[The inner model method]{#inner-model-method}
Suppose $M$ is a definable transitive class, containing all ordinals, and ZF proves "$M \models T$" for a theory $T$. Then

$$\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF}) \implies \mathrm{Con}(T),$$

and this implication is itself provable in a weak arithmetical theory.
:::

:::proof
Argue contrapositively and syntactically, which is what keeps the argument finitary. Suppose $T$ is inconsistent, so there is a finite derivation of $\bot$ from finitely many axioms $\tau_1, \dots, \tau_k$ of $T$. By hypothesis ZF proves $\tau_i^M$ for each $i$, where $\varphi^M$ denotes the relativisation of $\varphi$ to $M$. Relativisation commutes with the rules of first-order logic: if $\psi$ follows from $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_m$ by a rule, then $\psi^M$ follows from $\psi_1^M, \dots, \psi_m^M$ by the same rule, provided $M$ is nonempty — which it is, since it contains the ordinals. Translating the derivation of $\bot$ term by term therefore yields a derivation of $\bot^M$, which is $\bot$, from $\tau_1^M, \dots, \tau_k^M$. Since ZF proves each of those, ZF proves $\bot$. Hence ZF is inconsistent. Contraposing gives the statement, and every step was a finite manipulation of finite derivations, so the whole argument formalises in primitive recursive arithmetic.
:::

:::corollary[Gödel's consistency results]{#godel-consistency-corollary}
$\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF})$ implies $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$, and implies $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF} + V = L)$.
:::

:::proof
$L$ is a definable transitive class containing all ordinals, by the corollary *$L$ is a transitive class containing every ordinal*. ZF proves that $L$ satisfies every axiom of ZF — this is the eight-axiom verification quoted in the outline of the theorem *Gödel, 1938: the consistency of CH* — and ZF proves $L \models \mathrm{AC}$ by the theorem *$L$ satisfies the axiom of choice*, and $L \models V = L$ by the theorem *$V = L$ holds inside $L$*. Now apply the theorem *The inner model method* with $T = \mathrm{ZFC}$ and with $T = \mathrm{ZF} + V = L$.
:::

:::remark
Notice what this buys and what it does not. It shows that adding AC to ZF cannot create a contradiction that was not already there, so a mathematician who uses choice is taking no new logical risk. It does not show AC is true, and it does not produce a model of ZF from nothing — it converts a hypothetical model of ZF into a model of ZFC.
:::

:::example[Transferring a consistency result]
Assume it is known that ZF proves "$L \models \mathrm{GCH}$". Deduce that $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH}$ is consistent relative to ZF, and explain why one may not conclude that GCH is true.
:::

:::solution
1. $L$ is definable, transitive and contains all ordinals; ZF proves $L$ satisfies ZFC, and by assumption also GCH.
2. The theorem *The inner model method* with $T = \mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH}$ gives $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH})$.
3. Truth is a different matter: the argument produces a model where GCH holds, and the forcing results produce models where it fails. A sentence true in one model of ZFC and false in another is not settled by ZFC.
4. Final answer: consistency transfers; truth does not.
:::

## What $L$ cannot do

<!-- section: limitations -->

If $L$ settled every question, set theory would be finished: adopt $V = L$ and compute. The reason nobody does is that $L$ is too thin to be plausible, and it is thin in a way that can be made precise.

:::proposition[$V = L$ decides too much]{#v-l-decides}
$\mathrm{ZFC} + V = L$ proves CH, GCH, the diamond principle $\diamondsuit$, the existence of a Suslin tree, the existence of a $\Delta^1_2$ well-ordering of the reals, and the failure of every large cardinal axiom from measurables upward.
:::

:::remark[Quoted, not proved here]
Each item is a substantial theorem. CH and GCH come from condensation, as outlined for the theorem *Gödel, 1938: the consistency of CH*; $\diamondsuit$ is Jensen's refinement of that argument, and a Suslin tree is built from $\diamondsuit$ by a transfinite construction of length $\omega_1$; the $\Delta^1_2$ well-ordering is $<_L$ from the theorem *$L$ satisfies the axiom of choice* restricted to reals, with the complexity computed from the absoluteness of the construction; the failure of large cardinals is the theorem of Scott quoted immediately below. The point being made is structural: $V = L$ is not a modest extra axiom, it is a decision to work in the smallest universe compatible with the ordinals, and it settles essentially every question about which set theorists have an opinion — usually in the direction those opinions reject.
:::

:::theorem[Scott: a measurable cardinal implies $V \neq L$]{#scott-theorem}
If there exists a measurable cardinal, then $V \neq L$.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
From a measurable $\kappa$ one forms the ultrapower $\mathrm{Ult}(V, U)$ by a $\kappa$-complete nonprincipal ultrafilter $U$ on $\kappa$; completeness makes the ultrapower well-founded, so it collapses to a transitive class $M$ with a nontrivial elementary embedding $j : V \to M$ having critical point $\kappa$. If $V = L$ held, then $M = L$ as well by absoluteness of the construction, and one derives a contradiction by considering the least ordinal moved: $j(\kappa) > \kappa$ but elementarity forces $j$ to fix the $<_L$-structure below, and comparing the $\kappa$-th element of a definable sequence on both sides gives $\kappa \neq \kappa$. The assumptions are the existence of $U$ and Łoś's theorem for ultrapowers; the details are in Jech, chapter 17.
:::

:::intuition
$L$ is built from below by definitions, and a measurable cardinal is a statement of reflection so strong that no definition-by-recursion from below can produce its measure. Scott's theorem is the first sign that the large cardinal hierarchy and the constructible hierarchy pull in genuinely opposite directions: you can have a sharp knife or a thin universe, not both.
:::

:::example[Why "just take $L$" is not a solution to CH]
A student proposes settling CH by decree: adopt $V = L$ as a new axiom, since it is consistent and it proves CH. Give two reasons set theorists reject this.
:::

:::solution
1. *It is arbitrary.* $V = L$ is consistent, but so is $V \neq L$, and so is $\neg$CH. Consistency alone never selects an axiom; otherwise one could equally decree $\neg$CH by adopting a forcing axiom.
2. *It is restrictive.* By the theorem *Scott: a measurable cardinal implies $V \neq L$*, adopting $V = L$ refutes every large cardinal from a measurable upward, and hence discards the axioms which have the best claim to being natural extensions of ZFC and which settle definable questions about the reals in a coherent way.
3. Final answer: the two objections are arbitrariness and restrictiveness, and the second is the one that actually moved the field toward large cardinals and forcing axioms instead.
:::

## Forcing posets: conditions as partial information

<!-- section: forcing-posets -->

Forcing starts from a very plain object: a partially ordered set whose elements are thought of as *finite pieces of information* about an object we intend to create. The order encodes refinement. Everything else is machinery for making "an object compatible with infinitely many pieces at once" into a legitimate set.

:::definition[Forcing poset]{#poset-def}
A **forcing poset** is a partially ordered set $(\mathbb{P}, \le)$ with a largest element $\mathbb{1}$. Elements of $\mathbb{P}$ are called **conditions**. We read $q \le p$ as "$q$ is **stronger** than $p$", meaning $q$ carries all the information in $p$ and possibly more.
:::

:::pitfall
The order convention is upside down relative to intuition: *smaller* conditions are *stronger*, that is, more informative. Some authors (notably in the Israeli tradition) use the opposite convention. Always check which one a text uses before reading a single proof.
:::

:::definition[Compatibility and antichains]{#compat-def}
Conditions $p, q \in \mathbb{P}$ are **compatible** if some $r \in \mathbb{P}$ satisfies $r \le p$ and $r \le q$; otherwise they are **incompatible**, written $p \perp q$. A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ is an **antichain** if its elements are pairwise incompatible. The poset has the **countable chain condition** (ccc) if every antichain in $\mathbb{P}$ is countable.
:::

:::definition[Dense sets]{#dense-def}
A set $D \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ is **dense** if for every $p \in \mathbb{P}$ there is $q \in D$ with $q \le p$. It is **dense below $p$** if every $r \le p$ has an extension in $D$. It is **open** if $q \le p \in D$ implies $q \in D$.
:::

:::intuition
A condition is a promise: "the object I am building will look like *this* so far." Compatibility means two promises can be kept together. A dense set is a demand that can always be met: no matter what you have promised, you can promise a little more and satisfy the demand. Forcing works because meeting *all* the demands at once produces an object no single promise described.
:::

:::example[Finite partial functions]
Let $\mathbb{P}$ be the set of finite partial functions $p : \omega \rightharpoonup 2$ ordered by reverse inclusion, $q \le p$ iff $q \supseteq p$. Identify $\mathbb{1}$, decide when two conditions are compatible, and give an infinite antichain.
:::

:::solution
1. The largest element is the empty function $\emptyset$, since every $p$ extends it; it promises nothing.
2. $p$ and $q$ are compatible if and only if they agree on $\mathrm{dom}(p) \cap \mathrm{dom}(q)$: if they agree, $p \cup q$ is a function and is a common extension; if they disagree at some $n$, no function can extend both.
3. An infinite antichain: for $n \ge 1$ let $a_n$ be the condition with domain $\{0, 1, \dots, n\}$ taking value $0$ at $0, \dots, n-1$ and value $1$ at $n$. Any two of these disagree at the smaller of their two top indices, so they are pairwise incompatible.
4. Final answer: $\mathbb{1} = \emptyset$; compatibility is agreement on the common domain; $\{a_n : n \ge 1\}$ is an infinite antichain.
:::

:::proposition[Counting conditions with a bounded domain]{#counting-conditions}
Let $\mathbb{P} = \mathrm{Fn}(\omega, 2)$ be the poset of the previous example. The number of conditions with domain contained in $\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$ is

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} 2^k = 3^n.$$
:::

:::proof
A condition with domain inside an $n$-element set is determined by choosing its domain — a subset of size $k$, in $\binom{n}{k}$ ways — and then a value in $\{0,1\}$ at each of the $k$ points, in $2^k$ ways. Summing over $k$ and applying the binomial theorem to $(1+2)^n$ gives $3^n$. Equivalently, and more directly: each of the $n$ points is independently assigned one of three states — undefined, $0$, or $1$ — giving $3^n$ conditions.
:::

:::example[How many conditions extend a given one]
Inside $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$, fix a condition $p$ with $\mathrm{dom}(p) = \{0, 2\}$. How many conditions $q \le p$ have $\mathrm{dom}(q) \subseteq \{0,1,2,3,4\}$?
:::

:::solution
1. Such a $q$ must agree with $p$ on $\{0,2\}$ and must have those points in its domain, since $q \supseteq p$.
2. The remaining points $1, 3, 4$ are each free to be undefined, $0$, or $1$.
3. So the count is $3^3 = 27$. Final answer: $27$.
:::

## Generic filters

<!-- section: generic-filters -->

The object forcing adds is not a condition but a coherent, maximally demanding *set* of conditions.

:::definition[Filter]{#filter-def}
$F \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ is a **filter** if it is nonempty, upward closed ($p \in F$ and $p \le q$ imply $q \in F$), and directed downward ($p, q \in F$ implies some $r \in F$ has $r \le p$ and $r \le q$).
:::

:::definition[Generic filter]{#generic-def}
Let $M$ be a model of set theory with $\mathbb{P} \in M$. A filter $G \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ is **$\mathbb{P}$-generic over $M$** if for every dense set $D \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ with $D \in M$,

$$G \cap D \neq \emptyset.$$
:::

Two remarks on the definition before any theorem. First, genericity is *relative to $M$*: only dense sets that belong to $M$ must be met, and this restriction is exactly what makes generic filters possible to obtain. Second, the requirement is by definition, so the following is not a theorem but a restatement — and it is stated anyway because it is the move used dozens of times below.

:::proposition[Genericity is used by exhibiting dense sets]{#genericity-use}
If $G$ is $\mathbb{P}$-generic over $M$ and $D \in M$ is dense, then some $p \in G$ lies in $D$. Consequently, to prove that the object built from $G$ has a property $\Phi$, it suffices to find a set $D_\Phi \in M$ which is dense and all of whose conditions force $\Phi$ to hold.
:::

:::proof
The first sentence is the definition of genericity applied to $D$. For the second, suppose $D_\Phi \in M$ is dense and every $p \in D_\Phi$ guarantees $\Phi$ of the object built from any filter containing $p$. By genericity there is $p \in G \cap D_\Phi$, and since $p \in G$ the object built from $G$ satisfies $\Phi$.
:::

The next proposition is the reason genericity is not vacuous and the reason the extension is genuinely new.

:::theorem[A generic filter is not in the ground model]{#generic-not-in-m}
Let $\mathbb{P} \in M$ be a poset which is **separative in the weak sense** that every $p \in \mathbb{P}$ has two incompatible extensions. If $G$ is $\mathbb{P}$-generic over $M$, then $G \notin M$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose toward a contradiction that $G \in M$. Then the set

$$D = \mathbb{P} \setminus G = \{ p \in \mathbb{P} : p \notin G \}$$

is a member of $M$, since $M$ satisfies enough of ZF to form the difference of two of its sets. We claim $D$ is dense. Let $p \in \mathbb{P}$ be arbitrary. By hypothesis $p$ has two extensions $q_0 \perp q_1$. If both were in $G$, then, $G$ being downward directed, some $r \in G$ would extend both, making $q_0$ and $q_1$ compatible — contradiction. So at least one $q_i \notin G$, and that $q_i$ is an extension of $p$ lying in $D$. Hence $D$ is dense and belongs to $M$.

Genericity now gives $G \cap D \neq \emptyset$. But $D$ was defined as the complement of $G$, so $G \cap D = \emptyset$. This contradiction shows $G \notin M$.
:::

:::intuition
The proof is the whole trick of forcing in miniature. The ground model can *describe* the object it is missing — it knows the poset, it knows what genericity would mean — but any concrete candidate it produces can be diagonalised against by the dense set of conditions that refute it. The generic filter exists only from outside.
:::

Since no generic filter lives in $M$, one must ask whether any exists at all. For countable $M$ the answer is yes, and the proof is a direct construction.

:::theorem[Rasiowa–Sikorski: generic filters exist over countable models]{#rasiowa-sikorski}
Let $\mathbb{P}$ be a poset and let $\{D_n : n < \omega\}$ be a countable family of dense subsets of $\mathbb{P}$. Then for every $p \in \mathbb{P}$ there is a filter $G \subseteq \mathbb{P}$ with $p \in G$ and $G \cap D_n \neq \emptyset$ for all $n$. In particular, if $M$ is a countable transitive model of ZFC and $\mathbb{P} \in M$, then a $\mathbb{P}$-generic filter over $M$ exists.
:::

:::proof
Build a decreasing chain of conditions. Put $p_0 = p$. Given $p_n$, use density of $D_n$ to choose $p_{n+1} \le p_n$ with $p_{n+1} \in D_n$. This is a recursion of length $\omega$ with a choice at each step, so it requires only countable choice, available in ZFC.

Let $G = \{ q \in \mathbb{P} : q \ge p_n \text{ for some } n \}$, the upward closure of the chain. Then:

*Nonempty and contains $p$*: $p = p_0 \ge p_0$.

*Upward closed*: if $q \in G$, say $q \ge p_n$, and $q \le r$, then $r \ge p_n$, so $r \in G$.

*Downward directed*: if $q \ge p_m$ and $q' \ge p_n$ with, say, $m \le n$, then $p_n \le p_m \le q$ and $p_n \le q'$, and $p_n \in G$; so $p_n$ is a common extension inside $G$.

*Meets each $D_n$*: $p_{n+1} \in D_n$ and $p_{n+1} \in G$.

For the final claim, let $M$ be countable and transitive. Then $M$ has only countably many elements, so it contains only countably many dense subsets of $\mathbb{P}$; enumerate them as $\{D_n : n<\omega\}$ — this enumeration lives outside $M$, which is precisely why $M$ cannot carry out the construction itself — and apply the first part.
:::

:::pitfall
The enumeration of dense sets is *not* an element of $M$, and it must not be. If it were, $M$ could run the construction and produce $G$ itself, contradicting the theorem *A generic filter is not in the ground model*. Forcing arguments are always carried out in a metatheory that sees $M$ from outside.
:::

:::example[Building a generic filter by hand]
In $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$, take $D_n = \{ p : n \in \mathrm{dom}(p)\}$ and run the construction of the theorem *Rasiowa–Sikorski: generic filters exist over countable models* starting from $\mathbb{1}$. What object does the resulting filter determine?
:::

:::solution
1. Step $n$ extends the current finite condition so that $n$ enters its domain; after $\omega$ steps every natural number has been assigned a value.
2. The union $\bigcup G$ is therefore a total function $g : \omega \to 2$, because the conditions in $G$ are pairwise compatible functions whose domains exhaust $\omega$.
3. That function is a point of Cantor space, equivalently a subset of $\omega$, equivalently a real number in binary.
4. Final answer: the filter determines a single new real, the **Cohen real** $g = \bigcup G$.
:::

## The forcing relation and the extension $M[G]$

<!-- section: the-forcing-relation -->

We now have an object $G$ outside $M$. The problem is to build a model containing both $M$ and $G$ and to know in advance, from inside $M$, which sentences will hold there. Both problems are solved by *names*.

:::definition[$\mathbb{P}$-names and the extension]{#names-def}
A **$\mathbb{P}$-name** is a set $\tau$ of pairs $(\sigma, p)$ where $\sigma$ is a $\mathbb{P}$-name and $p \in \mathbb{P}$; the definition is by recursion on rank. Given a filter $G$, the **evaluation** of $\tau$ is

$$\tau_G = \{ \sigma_G : (\sigma, p) \in \tau \text{ for some } p \in G \},$$

and the **generic extension** is $M[G] = \{ \tau_G : \tau \in M \text{ is a } \mathbb{P}\text{-name} \}$.
:::

:::intuition
A name is a recipe with conditional clauses: "put $\sigma$ into me, provided $p$ turns out to be true." The ground model can write down every such recipe, because recipes are ordinary sets. It cannot evaluate them, because evaluation needs $G$. So $M$ owns the syntax and $G$ supplies the semantics.
:::

:::proposition[The extension contains what it should]{#extension-contains}
$M \subseteq M[G]$ and $G \in M[G]$, and $M[G]$ has the same ordinals as $M$.
:::

:::proof
For $M \subseteq M[G]$, define the **check name** $\check{x}$ by recursion: $\check{x} = \{(\check{y}, \mathbb{1}) : y \in x\}$. Then $\check{x} \in M$, and since $\mathbb{1} \in G$ for every filter, an induction on rank gives $(\check{x})_G = \{(\check y)_G : y \in x\} = x$. So every $x \in M$ is the evaluation of a name in $M$.

For $G \in M[G]$, take $\Gamma = \{ (\check{p}, p) : p \in \mathbb{P} \}$, a name in $M$. Its evaluation is $\Gamma_G = \{ (\check p)_G : p \in G\} = \{p : p \in G\} = G$.

For ordinals, $M \subseteq M[G]$ gives one inclusion. Conversely, an easy induction shows $\mathrm{rank}(\tau_G) \le \mathrm{rank}(\tau)$, so every element of $M[G]$ has rank below the height of $M$; since both classes are transitive and contain exactly the ordinals of that height, their ordinals agree.
:::

:::definition[The forcing relation]{#forcing-relation-def}
For $p \in \mathbb{P}$, a formula $\varphi$ and names $\tau_1,\dots,\tau_n \in M$, write

$$p \Vdash \varphi(\tau_1,\dots,\tau_n)$$

to mean: for every filter $G$ which is $\mathbb{P}$-generic over $M$ and contains $p$, the extension satisfies $M[G] \models \varphi((\tau_1)_G, \dots, (\tau_n)_G)$.
:::

:::theorem[The forcing theorem]{#forcing-theorem}
Let $M$ be a countable transitive model of ZFC and $\mathbb{P} \in M$.

1. *(Definability.)* For each formula $\varphi$ the relation $p \Vdash \varphi(\bar\tau)$ is definable in $M$, by a formula with $p, \mathbb{P}, \le, \bar\tau$ as parameters.
2. *(Truth.)* For every generic $G$, $M[G] \models \varphi(\bar\tau_G)$ if and only if some $p \in G$ has $p \Vdash \varphi(\bar\tau)$.
3. *(Preservation.)* $M[G]$ is a countable transitive model of ZFC.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
This is the technical core of the subject and cannot be proved in a chapter; what can be said is exactly what the proof does. Definability is established by defining a relation $p \Vdash^{*} \varphi$ *inside $M$*, by recursion on the structure of $\varphi$ and, for atomic formulas, by a simultaneous recursion on the rank of the names: $p \Vdash^* \sigma \in \tau$ is defined in terms of dense sets of conditions putting suitable pairs into $\tau$, negation is handled by "no extension of $p$ forces $\varphi$", and the existential quantifier by a density clause over names. One then proves by induction that $\Vdash^*$ and $\Vdash$ coincide, which is the truth lemma, using the genericity of $G$ at each induction step. Preservation of the axioms is a separate verification, one axiom at a time; extensionality and foundation are inherited from transitivity, pairing and union are name constructions, and replacement and power set require the definability clause to convert an unbounded search into a bounded one. What is assumed throughout is that $M$ is a set — countable and transitive — so that generic filters exist by the theorem *Rasiowa–Sikorski: generic filters exist over countable models*. The full development is Kunen, *Set Theory*, chapter IV, or Jech chapter 14.
:::

:::proposition[Forcing is monotone and decided by density]{#forcing-monotone}
If $p \Vdash \varphi$ and $q \le p$ then $q \Vdash \varphi$. Moreover for every $p$ and $\varphi$ there is $q \le p$ with $q \Vdash \varphi$ or $q \Vdash \neg\varphi$; consequently the set of conditions deciding $\varphi$ is dense.
:::

:::proof
Monotonicity: any generic $G$ containing $q$ contains $p$, since filters are upward closed and $q \le p$; so the defining condition for $p \Vdash \varphi$ applies to $G$ and gives $M[G] \models \varphi$.

For the second claim, suppose no $q \le p$ forces $\neg\varphi$. Let $G$ be generic with $p \in G$. If $M[G] \models \varphi$, the truth part of the theorem *The forcing theorem* gives some $r \in G$ with $r \Vdash \varphi$, and since $G$ is directed there is $q \in G$ with $q \le p$ and $q \le r$; monotonicity gives $q \Vdash \varphi$, as required. If instead $M[G] \models \neg\varphi$, the same argument produces $q \le p$ forcing $\neg\varphi$, contrary to assumption. Density follows because $p$ was arbitrary.
:::

:::remark
The last proposition is what makes forcing a *method* rather than a curiosity. To show that a sentence holds in the extension, one does not inspect the extension: one shows that the conditions forcing it are dense in the ground model, a statement about a partial order that $M$ can verify about itself.
:::

The first thing to check is that $g$ is a function defined everywhere. That is exactly a density statement, and it is the concrete heart of the whole method.

:::theorem[The domain-filling sets are dense]{#dn-dense}
For each $n < \omega$ put

$$D_n = \{ p \in \mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2) : n \in \mathrm{dom}(p) \}.$$

Then each $D_n$ is dense in $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$, and each $D_n$ belongs to any model $M$ containing $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$.
:::

:::proof
Let $p \in \mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$ be arbitrary and fix $n$. Two cases.

If $n \in \mathrm{dom}(p)$, then $p$ itself lies in $D_n$ and $p \le p$, so $p$ has an extension in $D_n$.

If $n \notin \mathrm{dom}(p)$, define $q = p \cup \{(n,0)\}$. Since $\mathrm{dom}(p)$ is finite, so is $\mathrm{dom}(q) = \mathrm{dom}(p) \cup \{n\}$, and $q$ is a function because the new point $n$ was not previously in the domain. Hence $q \in \mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$, and $q \supseteq p$ means $q \le p$. Also $n \in \mathrm{dom}(q)$, so $q \in D_n$.

In both cases $p$ has an extension in $D_n$, which is density. Finally $D_n$ is definable from $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$ and $n$ by a formula with bounded quantifiers, so separation inside $M$ produces it as an element of $M$.
:::

:::corollary[The generic object is a total function]{#g-total}
If $G$ is $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$-generic over $M$, then $g = \bigcup G$ is a function from $\omega$ to $\{0,1\}$.
:::

:::proof
$g$ is a function: any two conditions in $G$ are compatible, being members of a downward directed filter, and compatible finite partial functions agree on their common domain, so the union of $G$ has no conflicting pairs. $g$ is total: fix $n$. The set $D_n$ is dense and lies in $M$ by the theorem *The domain-filling sets are dense*, so genericity gives $p \in G \cap D_n$, and then $n \in \mathrm{dom}(p) \subseteq \mathrm{dom}(g)$. Values lie in $\{0,1\}$ because every condition takes values there.
:::

Now the payoff. The generic real is new, and the proof is three lines of density — the single most important computation in the subject.

:::theorem[The Cohen real is new]{#cohen-real-new}
Let $G$ be $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$-generic over $M$ and $g = \bigcup G$. Then $g \neq f$ for every $f : \omega \to 2$ with $f \in M$. Hence $g \in M[G] \setminus M$.
:::

:::proof
Fix $f : \omega \to 2$ with $f \in M$, and set

$$E_f = \{ p \in \mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2) : p(n) \neq f(n) \ \text{for some } n \in \mathrm{dom}(p) \}.$$

$E_f$ is an element of $M$, being definable from $f$ and the poset.

$E_f$ is dense. Let $p$ be any condition. Since $\mathrm{dom}(p)$ is finite and $\omega$ is not, choose $n \notin \mathrm{dom}(p)$ and put $q = p \cup \{(n, 1 - f(n))\}$. Then $q$ is a condition, $q \le p$, and $q(n) = 1 - f(n) \neq f(n)$, so $q \in E_f$.

By genericity pick $p \in G \cap E_f$, witnessed at some $n \in \mathrm{dom}(p)$. Since $p \subseteq g$ we get $g(n) = p(n) \neq f(n)$, so $g \neq f$.

As $f \in M$ was arbitrary, $g$ is not a ground-model real. It does lie in $M[G]$, since $g = \bigcup \Gamma_G$ for the canonical name $\Gamma$ of the corollary *The extension contains what it should*, and $M[G]$ is a model of ZFC closed under unions.
:::

:::intuition
Why can the ground model not defend itself? Because any $f \in M$ is a *single* target, and a finite condition has only finitely many commitments, so there is always an untouched coordinate where the generic can be made to disagree. The ground model has $2^{\aleph_0}$ reals and the generic dodges all of them simultaneously — but it does so one dense set at a time, and each dense set handles only one $f$. Genericity is what lets $\aleph_0$-many refutations be combined.
:::

:::example[A dense set forcing an infinite pattern]
Show that the generic real $g$ takes the value $1$ infinitely often.
:::

:::solution
1. For each $k$ let $F_k = \{ p : p(n) = 1 \text{ for some } n \ge k \text{ in } \mathrm{dom}(p)\}$, an element of $M$.
2. $F_k$ is dense: given $p$, pick $n \ge k$ outside the finite set $\mathrm{dom}(p)$ and extend by $(n,1)$.
3. Genericity gives some $p \in G \cap F_k$, so $g(n) = 1$ for some $n \ge k$.
4. Since $k$ was arbitrary, $\{n : g(n) = 1\}$ is unbounded, hence infinite. Final answer: yes, infinitely often — and by symmetry the same holds for the value $0$.
:::

:::example[Counting the conditions of a given domain size]
How many conditions in $\mathrm{Fn}(\omega,2)$ have domain of size exactly $3$ contained in $\{0,1,\dots,5\}$?
:::

:::solution
1. Choose the domain: $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ ways.
2. Choose values: $2^3 = 8$ ways.
3. Total $20 \cdot 8 = 160$. Final answer: $160$.
:::

## The continuum hypothesis stated precisely

<!-- section: ch-statement -->

CH is a statement about two cardinals, and almost every confusion about it comes from misreading which two.

:::proposition[Equivalent forms of CH]{#ch-equivalents}
The following are equivalent over ZFC:

1. Every infinite subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is either countable or of the same cardinality as $\mathbb{R}$.
2. $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$.
3. There is a well-ordering of $\mathbb{R}$ in which every proper initial segment is countable.
:::

:::proof
$(1) \Rightarrow (2)$: By AC, $|\mathbb{R}| = \aleph_\alpha$ for some $\alpha \ge 1$, since $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable by Cantor's theorem. If $\alpha \ge 2$ then $\aleph_1 < |\mathbb{R}|$, and a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ of size exactly $\aleph_1$ exists (take the image of an injection from $\omega_1$, available since $\aleph_1 \le |\mathbb{R}|$); that subset is uncountable and not of size $|\mathbb{R}|$, contradicting (1). So $\alpha = 1$.

$(2) \Rightarrow (3)$: If $|\mathbb{R}| = \aleph_1$, transport the canonical well-ordering of $\omega_1$ along a bijection. Every proper initial segment of $\omega_1$ is a countable ordinal, hence countable.

$(3) \Rightarrow (1)$: Let $\prec$ be such a well-ordering, of order type $\gamma$. Every proper initial segment being countable means $\gamma \le \omega_1$, and $\gamma$ is uncountable since $\mathbb{R}$ is, so $\gamma = \omega_1$ and $|\mathbb{R}| = \aleph_1$. Now let $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be infinite. Under the bijection with $\omega_1$, either $A$ is bounded in $\omega_1$, hence contained in a countable initial segment and therefore countable, or it is unbounded, in which case it has size $\aleph_1 = |\mathbb{R}|$.
:::

:::pitfall
CH is *not* the statement $|\mathbb{R}| > \aleph_0$ — that is Cantor's theorem, provable in ZFC. Nor is it the statement that $\aleph_1$ exists, which is also a theorem. CH asserts that two independently defined cardinals, the first uncountable aleph and the cardinality of the power set of $\omega$, happen to coincide.
:::

:::example[Locating CH among related statements]
Classify each as a theorem of ZFC, independent of ZFC, or refutable in ZFC: (a) $\aleph_1 \le 2^{\aleph_0}$; (b) $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$; (c) $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_0$; (d) $2^{\aleph_1} = \aleph_2$.
:::

:::solution
1. (a) A theorem. $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable and by AC its cardinality is an aleph, so it is at least the least uncountable aleph.
2. (b) Independent — this is CH.
3. (c) Refutable: Cantor's diagonal argument proves $2^{\aleph_0} > \aleph_0$ outright.
4. (d) Independent: it follows from GCH, and Easton-style forcing makes it fail.
5. Final answer: theorem, independent, refutable, independent.
:::

## Gödel's half: CH cannot be refuted

<!-- section: godel-consistency -->

The inner-model half is now assembled from parts already proved plus one quoted lemma.

:::theorem[Condensation]{#condensation}
If $X \prec L_\alpha$ is an elementary submodel of $(L_\alpha, \in)$ for a limit ordinal $\alpha$, then the transitive collapse of $X$ is $L_\beta$ for some $\beta \le \alpha$, and $\beta = \mathrm{otp}(X \cap \mathrm{Ord})$.
:::

:::remark[Quoted, not proved here]
The proof needs the fine structure of the definition of $L$: one shows the statement "$V = L$" is expressible by a $\Sigma_1$ formula whose relativisation to $L_\alpha$ is correct for limit $\alpha$, then applies the Mostowski collapse and $\Sigma_1$-absoluteness. It is the one genuinely technical ingredient in Gödel's proof, and it is where the choice of $\mathrm{Def}$ rather than $\mathcal{P}$ pays off. See Jech, Theorem 13.18.
:::

:::theorem[CH holds in $L$]{#ch-in-l}
$\mathrm{ZF} \vdash L \models \mathrm{CH}$. Hence $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZF}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{CH})$.
:::

:::remark[Outline using the quoted condensation lemma]
Work inside $L$, so that $V = L$. Let $A \subseteq \omega$. Choose a limit $\alpha$ large enough that $A \in L_\alpha$ and $L_\alpha$ satisfies enough of ZF, then take a countable $X \prec L_\alpha$ with $\omega \cup \{A\} \subseteq X$; such $X$ exists by the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem. By the theorem *Condensation* the collapse of $X$ is some $L_\beta$ with $\beta$ countable, and the collapse fixes $A$ because $A \subseteq \omega \subseteq X$ and $X$ contains each element of $\omega$. So $A \in L_\beta$ with $\beta < \omega_1$. Therefore

$$\mathcal{P}(\omega) \cap L \subseteq L_{\omega_1},$$

which has cardinality $\aleph_1$ by the proposition *Levels of $L$ have the size of their index*. Combined with $\aleph_1 \le 2^{\aleph_0}$ this gives $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1$ in $L$. The relative consistency statement then follows from the theorem *The inner model method*. What is assumed: condensation, and Löwenheim–Skolem inside $L$.
:::

:::corollary[ZFC does not refute CH]{#zfc-not-refute-ch}
If ZF is consistent, then $\mathrm{ZFC} \nvdash \neg\mathrm{CH}$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $\mathrm{ZFC} \vdash \neg\mathrm{CH}$. By the theorem *CH holds in $L$* the theory $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{CH}$ is consistent, so it has a model $\mathcal{M}$ by completeness. Then $\mathcal{M} \models \mathrm{ZFC}$, so $\mathcal{M} \models \neg\mathrm{CH}$ by soundness, while also $\mathcal{M} \models \mathrm{CH}$ — a contradiction. Hence no such proof exists.
:::

:::example[Why the argument gives GCH too]
Sketch how the same method yields $2^{\aleph_\alpha} = \aleph_{\alpha+1}$ in $L$.
:::

:::solution
1. Replace $\omega$ by $\omega_\alpha$ throughout: given $A \subseteq \omega_\alpha$ in $L$, take an elementary submodel $X \prec L_\gamma$ of size $\aleph_\alpha$ with $\omega_\alpha \cup \{A\} \subseteq X$.
2. Condensation collapses $X$ to some $L_\beta$ with $|\beta| = \aleph_\alpha$, hence $\beta < \omega_{\alpha+1}$, and $A$ is unmoved because $\omega_\alpha \subseteq X$.
3. So $\mathcal{P}(\omega_\alpha) \cap L \subseteq L_{\omega_{\alpha+1}}$, of size $\aleph_{\alpha+1}$.
4. Final answer: $2^{\aleph_\alpha} \le \aleph_{\alpha+1}$ in $L$, and $\ge$ is a theorem of ZFC, so GCH holds in $L$.
:::

## Cohen's half: CH cannot be proved

<!-- section: cohen-independence -->

For the other half we force with many copies of the Cohen poset at once and then must verify that the cardinals we are counting with survive.

:::definition[The poset for $\neg$CH]{#neg-ch-poset}
For a cardinal $\kappa$ of $M$, let $\mathrm{Fn}(\kappa \times \omega, 2)$ be the finite partial functions $p$ from $\kappa \times \omega$ into $2$, ordered by reverse inclusion. For $\xi < \kappa$ put $g_\xi(n) = g(\xi, n)$, where $g = \bigcup G$.
:::

:::theorem[The columns are distinct reals]{#columns-distinct}
Let $G$ be generic for $\mathrm{Fn}(\kappa\times\omega,2)$ over $M$. Then each $g_\xi$ is a function $\omega \to 2$, and $g_\xi \neq g_\eta$ whenever $\xi \neq \eta$. Hence $M[G] \models 2^{\aleph_0} \ge |\kappa|$.
:::

:::proof
Totality of each $g_\xi$ is the corollary *The generic object is a total function* applied to the dense sets $D_{\xi,n} = \{p : (\xi,n) \in \mathrm{dom}(p)\}$, whose density is proved exactly as in the theorem *The domain-filling sets are dense*: a finite condition omits some $(\xi,n)$ only if it can be extended by assigning a value there.

For distinctness, fix $\xi \neq \eta$ and let

$$E_{\xi\eta} = \{ p : \text{for some } n, \ (\xi,n),(\eta,n) \in \mathrm{dom}(p) \text{ and } p(\xi,n) \neq p(\eta,n) \}.$$

This set lies in $M$. It is dense: given $p$, its domain is finite, so choose $n$ with neither $(\xi,n)$ nor $(\eta,n)$ in $\mathrm{dom}(p)$ — possible since only finitely many $n$ are used — and extend $p$ by setting the first to $0$ and the second to $1$. The result is a condition below $p$ in $E_{\xi\eta}$. By genericity some $p \in G$ lies in $E_{\xi\eta}$, and since $p \subseteq g$ the functions $g_\xi$ and $g_\eta$ differ at that $n$.

So $\xi \mapsto g_\xi$ is an injection of $\kappa$ into the reals of $M[G]$, giving $2^{\aleph_0} \ge |\kappa|$ there.
:::

The inequality is worthless unless $\kappa$ is still a cardinal in $M[G]$; otherwise $\aleph_2^M$ could collapse to $\aleph_1$ and we would have proved nothing. This is what the chain condition is for.

:::theorem[ccc forcing preserves cardinals and cofinalities]{#ccc-preserves}
If $\mathbb{P} \in M$ has the countable chain condition in $M$ and $G$ is generic, then for every ordinal $\gamma$, $\gamma$ is a cardinal in $M$ if and only if it is a cardinal in $M[G]$; likewise cofinalities are unchanged.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
The engine is the following lemma, which is where ccc is used: if $f \in M[G]$ is a function from an ordinal $\gamma$ into $M$, then in $M$ there is a function $F$ with domain $\gamma$ such that $f(\alpha) \in F(\alpha)$ and $|F(\alpha)| \le \aleph_0$ for all $\alpha$. To see it, take a name $\dot f$ and, for each $\alpha$, consider a maximal antichain of conditions deciding the value $\dot f(\alpha)$; by ccc that antichain is countable, so it names at most countably many possible values, and $F(\alpha)$ is that countable set — which $M$ can compute by the definability part of the theorem *The forcing theorem*. Now if $\gamma$ were a cardinal in $M$ but not in $M[G]$, there would be a surjection $f : \delta \to \gamma$ in $M[G]$ with $\delta < \gamma$; applying the lemma covers $\gamma$ by $\delta$ countable sets inside $M$, so $|\gamma| \le |\delta| \cdot \aleph_0 < \gamma$ in $M$ whenever $\gamma$ is uncountable and regular, contradiction. The regular case handles the rest since cardinals are limits of regular ones. The assumptions are the forcing theorem and AC in $M$; details in Kunen, chapter VII.
:::

:::theorem[Cohen's theorem, with the count]{#cohen-full}
Let $M \models \mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{GCH}$ be countable transitive and let $\kappa \in M$ be a cardinal of $M$ with $\mathrm{cf}(\kappa)^M > \omega$. Forcing with $\mathbb{P} = \mathrm{Fn}(\kappa\times\omega,2)$ gives $M[G] \models 2^{\aleph_0} = \kappa$.
:::

:::remark[Outline, assembling the proved parts with two quoted ones]
The lower bound $2^{\aleph_0} \ge \kappa$ is the theorem *The columns are distinct reals*, fully proved above. That $\kappa$ is still a cardinal uses the theorem *ccc forcing preserves cardinals and cofinalities*, together with the fact — a $\Delta$-system argument on finite domains — that $\mathrm{Fn}(\kappa\times\omega,2)$ is ccc. The upper bound $2^{\aleph_0} \le \kappa$ is the nice-name count: every real of $M[G]$ has a *nice name* $\bigcup_{n} \{\check n\} \times A_n$ with each $A_n$ an antichain, hence countable by ccc; under GCH in $M$ there are at most $\kappa^{\aleph_0} = \kappa$ such names when $\mathrm{cf}(\kappa)>\omega$. Finally $\mathrm{cf}(\kappa) > \omega$ is needed so that $\kappa^{\aleph_0} = \kappa$; the theorem *König's restriction on the continuum* below shows the hypothesis cannot be dropped.
:::

:::corollary[ZFC does not prove CH]{#zfc-not-prove-ch}
If ZFC is consistent, then $\mathrm{ZFC} \nvdash \mathrm{CH}$.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Cohen's theorem, with the count* with $\kappa = \aleph_2^M$, the theory $\mathrm{ZFC} + 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$ is consistent, and that theory proves $\neg\mathrm{CH}$. If ZFC proved CH, then this consistent theory would prove both CH and $\neg$CH, so it would be inconsistent — contradiction.
:::

:::example[Reading off the aleph subscript]
In a forcing extension satisfying $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$, what is the subscript $\beta$ with $\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_\beta$, and what is the value of $2^{\aleph_0}$ raised to the power $\aleph_0$ in that model?
:::

:::solution
1. By hypothesis $\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_2$, so $\beta = 2$.
2. For the second, $(2^{\aleph_0})^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0 \cdot \aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$, using cardinal exponentiation rules valid in ZFC.
3. Final answer: $\beta = 2$, and the power is again $\aleph_2$.
:::

## What forcing can and cannot do to the continuum

<!-- section: implications -->

Forcing gives enormous freedom over the value of $2^{\aleph_0}$, but not total freedom. One ZFC theorem survives every extension and constrains the answer.

:::theorem[König's restriction on the continuum]{#konig-continuum}
In ZFC, $\mathrm{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) > \aleph_0$. In particular $2^{\aleph_0} \neq \aleph_\omega$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose toward a contradiction that $\mathrm{cf}(2^{\aleph_0}) = \aleph_0$, and write $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0} = \sup_n \kappa_n$ with $\kappa_n < \mathfrak{c}$ strictly increasing. Identify the reals with the set $F$ of all functions $\omega \to \mathfrak{c}$ restricted appropriately; more simply, partition a set $S$ of size $\mathfrak{c}$ as $S = \bigcup_n S_n$ with $|S_n| = \kappa_n$.

Take $X$ of cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$, say $X = {}^{\omega}2$, and suppose for contradiction that $X = \bigcup_n X_n$ with $|X_n| = \kappa_n < \mathfrak{c}$. We produce an element of $X$ outside every $X_n$, contradicting the union. Since $|X_n| = \kappa_n < \mathfrak{c} = |{}^\omega 2|$ and there is a bijection of $\omega$ with $\omega \times \omega$, view each element of $X$ as a function $h : \omega \times \omega \to 2$ and let $h_n$ be its $n$-th row. For each $n$, the set of $n$-th rows of members of $X_n$ has size at most $\kappa_n < \mathfrak{c}$, so it is not all of ${}^{\omega}2$; choose $r_n \in {}^\omega 2$ not occurring as the $n$-th row of any member of $X_n$. Let $h$ be the function whose $n$-th row is $r_n$. Then $h \in X$, and $h \notin X_n$ for every $n$, because $h$ and any member of $X_n$ differ in row $n$. So $X \neq \bigcup_n X_n$, contradiction.

Therefore $\mathfrak{c}$ is not the supremum of countably many smaller cardinals, that is, $\mathrm{cf}(\mathfrak{c}) > \aleph_0$. Since $\mathrm{cf}(\aleph_\omega) = \aleph_0$, the value $\aleph_\omega$ is excluded.
:::

:::remark
The diagonal in that proof is Cantor's, performed once per row instead of once overall. That is the general shape of König's theorem, of which this is the special case $\sum_n \kappa_n < \prod_n \mathfrak{c}$.
:::

:::theorem[Easton: the continuum function on regulars is almost arbitrary]{#easton}
Let $M \models \mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{GCH}$ and let $E$ be a function from regular cardinals of $M$ to cardinals of $M$ such that $E$ is non-decreasing and $\mathrm{cf}(E(\kappa)) > \kappa$ for all $\kappa$. Then there is a forcing extension preserving cardinals in which $2^{\kappa} = E(\kappa)$ for every regular $\kappa$.
:::

:::remark[Quoted, not proved here]
Easton's construction is a class-length product of Cohen-style posets with support restricted by an "Easton support" condition, and the verification that cardinals are preserved splits the product into a small ccc-like part and a highly closed part at each stage. The two hypotheses on $E$ are exactly the ZFC constraints: monotonicity of the continuum function, and König's theorem in the form $\mathrm{cf}(2^\kappa)>\kappa$, which is the theorem *König's restriction on the continuum* generalised. See Jech, chapter 15. Singular cardinals behave completely differently — Silver's theorem and the whole of PCF theory live there — and Easton's freedom does not extend to them.
:::

:::example[Deciding which continuum functions are admissible]
Is there a model of ZFC with $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_3$ and $2^{\aleph_1} = \aleph_2$?
:::

:::solution
1. The continuum function is non-decreasing: $\aleph_0 < \aleph_1$ forces $2^{\aleph_0} \le 2^{\aleph_1}$.
2. Here that would require $\aleph_3 \le \aleph_2$, which is false.
3. Final answer: no such model exists — this is refutable in ZFC, before any forcing is attempted.
:::

## Large cardinals: axioms of infinity

<!-- section: large-cardinals -->

Forcing moves the continuum sideways; large cardinal axioms extend the universe upward. They assert that certain enormous cardinals exist, and each such assertion is strictly stronger than ZFC in a sense made precise below.

:::definition[Large cardinal axiom]{#lca-def}
A **large cardinal axiom** is a sentence asserting the existence of a cardinal $\kappa$ with a closure or reflection property so strong that $\mathrm{ZFC} + \text{the axiom}$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$. By the corollary *ZFC cannot prove its own consistency*, no such axiom is provable in ZFC.
:::

:::intuition
Each axiom of infinity says the universe is closed under one more operation. The axiom of infinity itself says "$\omega$ exists", which is what lifts finite set theory to ZFC; an inaccessible says "a stage exists that is closed under everything ZFC can do"; a measurable says "a stage exists that can be reflected by an elementary embedding". Each is the previous demand raised one level of abstraction.
:::

## Inaccessible cardinals

<!-- section: inaccessible-cardinals -->

:::definition[Inaccessible cardinal]{#inaccessible-def}
An uncountable cardinal $\kappa$ is **(strongly) inaccessible** if it is regular — $\mathrm{cf}(\kappa) = \kappa$ — and a strong limit: $2^\lambda < \kappa$ for every $\lambda < \kappa$.
:::

:::theorem[An inaccessible gives a model of ZFC]{#vkappa-models-zfc}
If $\kappa$ is inaccessible, then $(V_\kappa, \in) \models \mathrm{ZFC}$.
:::

:::proof
Extensionality, foundation and choice are inherited: $V_\kappa$ is transitive, so extensionality and foundation hold, and any well-ordering of a set $x \in V_\kappa$ has rank below $\kappa$, so it lies in $V_\kappa$.

Pairing and union: if $x, y \in V_\kappa$ then $\mathrm{rank}(\{x,y\}) = \max(\mathrm{rank} x, \mathrm{rank} y) + 1 < \kappa$ since $\kappa$ is a limit ordinal; and $\mathrm{rank}(\bigcup x) \le \mathrm{rank}(x)$.

Infinity: $\omega \in V_\kappa$ because $\kappa > \omega$.

Power set: if $x \in V_\kappa$ with $\mathrm{rank}(x) = \alpha < \kappa$, then $|x| \le |V_\alpha| < \kappa$ — here the strong limit property is used, by induction on $\alpha$ showing $|V_\alpha| < \kappa$ for $\alpha<\kappa$ — so $|\mathcal{P}(x)| \le 2^{|x|} < \kappa$ and $\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{P}(x)) = \alpha+1 < \kappa$. Hence $\mathcal{P}(x) \in V_\kappa$.

Separation: a subset of $x \in V_\kappa$ has rank at most that of $x$, so it lies in $V_\kappa$.

Replacement: let $f : x \to V_\kappa$ be definable over $V_\kappa$ with $x \in V_\kappa$. Then $|x| < \kappa$, and $\{\mathrm{rank}(f(u))+1 : u \in x\}$ is a set of fewer than $\kappa$ ordinals below $\kappa$. Regularity of $\kappa$ makes it bounded, say by $\beta < \kappa$, so the image lies in $V_\beta$ and hence is an element of $V_{\beta+1} \subseteq V_\kappa$.

All axioms hold, so $V_\kappa \models \mathrm{ZFC}$.
:::

:::corollary[ZFC cannot prove an inaccessible exists]{#no-inaccessible}
If ZFC is consistent, then $\mathrm{ZFC} \nvdash$ "there exists an inaccessible cardinal". Moreover $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ does not imply $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \exists\text{ inaccessible})$ provably in ZFC.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *An inaccessible gives a model of ZFC*, ZFC proves "if an inaccessible exists then there is a set model of ZFC", and hence proves "if an inaccessible exists then $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$", using the arithmetised soundness theorem as in the corollary *No absolute consistency proof for a set theory*. So a ZFC proof of the existence of an inaccessible would yield a ZFC proof of $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$, contradicting the corollary *ZFC cannot prove its own consistency*.

For the second claim, suppose ZFC proved that implication. Then $\mathrm{ZFC} + \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$ would prove $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\exists\text{ inaccessible})$, which by the first paragraph implies $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}))$ — a violation of the second incompleteness theorem applied to the consistent theory $\mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$.
:::

:::example[Inaccessibility is not just largeness]
Is $\aleph_\omega$ inaccessible? Is $\beth_{\omega}$?
:::

:::solution
1. $\aleph_\omega$ is a limit cardinal but $\mathrm{cf}(\aleph_\omega) = \omega < \aleph_\omega$, so it is singular, hence not inaccessible.
2. $\beth_\omega = \sup_n \beth_n$ is a strong limit, since $2^{\beth_n} = \beth_{n+1} < \beth_\omega$, but again its cofinality is $\omega$.
3. Final answer: neither — both fail regularity. Inaccessibility needs regularity *and* strong limit, and no cardinal ZFC can name from below has both.
:::

## Measurable cardinals

<!-- section: measurable-cardinals -->

:::definition[Measurable cardinal]{#measurable-def}
An uncountable cardinal $\kappa$ is **measurable** if there is a $\kappa$-complete nonprincipal ultrafilter $U$ on $\kappa$: a family $U \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\kappa)$ closed under supersets and under intersections of fewer than $\kappa$ of its members, containing exactly one of $A$ and $\kappa\setminus A$ for each $A$, and containing no singleton.
:::

:::theorem[Measurable implies inaccessible]{#measurable-inaccessible}
Every measurable cardinal is inaccessible.
:::

:::proof
Let $U$ be a $\kappa$-complete nonprincipal ultrafilter on $\kappa$.

*Regularity.* Suppose $\kappa = \bigcup_{i<\lambda} A_i$ with $\lambda < \kappa$ and each $|A_i| < \kappa$. Nonprincipality plus $\kappa$-completeness gives that every set of size less than $\kappa$ is *not* in $U$: a singleton is not in $U$, so its complement is, and intersecting fewer than $\kappa$ such complements — legitimate by completeness — shows the complement of any small set is in $U$. So each $A_i \notin U$, that is $\kappa \setminus A_i \in U$. By $\kappa$-completeness the intersection $\bigcap_{i<\lambda}(\kappa\setminus A_i)$ is in $U$, but that intersection is $\kappa \setminus \bigcup_i A_i = \emptyset$, and $\emptyset \notin U$ since $U$ is a proper filter. Contradiction, so $\kappa$ is regular.

*Strong limit.* Suppose $\lambda < \kappa \le 2^\lambda$. Fix an injection $\xi \mapsto f_\xi$ from $\kappa$ into ${}^{\lambda}2$. For each $i < \lambda$ exactly one of $\{\xi : f_\xi(i) = 0\}$, $\{\xi : f_\xi(i)=1\}$ lies in $U$; call the chosen value $e(i)$ and the chosen set $B_i \in U$. By $\kappa$-completeness $B = \bigcap_{i<\lambda} B_i \in U$, so $B$ is nonempty and in fact has size $\kappa$ by the previous paragraph. But every $\xi \in B$ satisfies $f_\xi(i) = e(i)$ for all $i<\lambda$, so $f_\xi = e$ — and $\xi \mapsto f_\xi$ was injective, forcing $|B| \le 1$. Contradiction. Hence $2^\lambda < \kappa$ for all $\lambda<\kappa$.

Being uncountable, regular and a strong limit, $\kappa$ is inaccessible.
:::

:::corollary[Measurables are far above the first inaccessible]{#measurable-above}
If $\kappa$ is measurable then $\kappa$ is the $\kappa$-th inaccessible cardinal, and $\mathrm{ZFC} + \exists \text{ measurable}$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC} + \exists\text{ inaccessible})$.
:::

:::proof
For the consistency claim: if $\kappa$ is measurable it is inaccessible, so by the theorem *An inaccessible gives a model of ZFC* the set $V_\kappa$ models ZFC; a standard reflection argument using $\kappa$-completeness shows the set of inaccessibles below $\kappa$ is in $U$, hence unbounded, so $V_\kappa$ also believes inaccessibles exist. That provides a model of $\mathrm{ZFC}+\exists\text{ inaccessible}$, and soundness converts a model into a consistency statement. The claim that $\kappa$ is the $\kappa$-th inaccessible follows from that same unboundedness together with regularity.
:::

:::proposition[The ladder is strictly increasing]{#ladder-strict}
If $A$ and $B$ are large cardinal axioms with $\mathrm{ZFC}+B \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+A)$, then $\mathrm{ZFC}+A \nvdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+B)$, assuming $\mathrm{ZFC}+B$ is consistent.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $\mathrm{ZFC}+A \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+B)$. Combined with the hypothesis, $\mathrm{ZFC}+B$ proves $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+A)$, and a consistency statement carries with it the provable implications of the theory it is about; formalising, $\mathrm{ZFC}+B \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+A)$ and $\mathrm{ZFC}+A \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+B)$ together give $\mathrm{ZFC}+B \vdash \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+B)$ by formalised soundness. That contradicts the theorem *Gödel's second incompleteness theorem* applied to the consistent theory $\mathrm{ZFC}+B$.
:::

:::remark
Empirically the hierarchy is *linearly* ordered by consistency strength, and no one knows why. Axioms invented for unrelated reasons — from combinatorics, from infinitary logic, from the theory of elementary embeddings — always turn out to be comparable. There is no theorem saying this must happen; it is the most striking unexplained regularity in the subject.
:::

## Incompleteness as the source of all of this

<!-- section: incompleteness -->

:::theorem[Gödel's first incompleteness theorem]{#first-incompleteness}
Every consistent, recursively axiomatised theory $T$ interpreting arithmetic is incomplete: there is a sentence $\gamma$ with $T \nvdash \gamma$ and $T \nvdash \neg\gamma$.
:::

:::remark[Outline, not a proof — what it assumes and where the details live]
The diagonal lemma supplies $\gamma$ with $T \vdash \gamma \leftrightarrow \neg\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner\gamma\urcorner)$. If $T \vdash \gamma$ then $T$ proves both $\gamma$ and its own unprovability of $\gamma$, contradicting consistency via provable $\Sigma_1$-completeness. If $T \vdash \neg\gamma$ then $T$ proves $\mathrm{Prov}_T(\ulcorner\gamma\urcorner)$, from which $\omega$-consistency (Rosser's refinement weakens this to plain consistency) gives a contradiction. What is assumed is the representability of recursive relations in $T$; see any text on incompleteness.
:::

:::corollary[Independence is unavoidable]{#independence-unavoidable}
No consistent recursively axiomatised extension of ZFC decides every sentence. In particular, adding CH, or $\neg$CH, or any large cardinal axiom, leaves infinitely many sentences independent.
:::

:::proof
Any such extension $T$ is still recursively axiomatised, still interprets arithmetic and, by assumption, is consistent. The theorem *Gödel's first incompleteness theorem* then supplies a sentence independent of $T$. Iterating — pass to $T + \gamma$, which is again consistent and recursively axiomatised — produces infinitely many.
:::

:::intuition
CH's independence is often told as a story about set theory being strange. The corollary says the opposite: incompleteness is the default for any theory that can talk about arithmetic, and the surprise is not that some sentence is independent but that the independent sentence turned out to be one mathematicians already cared about.
:::

## Consistency strength as an ordering

<!-- section: consistency-strength -->

:::definition[The consistency-strength preorder]{#con-strength-def}
For theories $S, T$ extending a fixed weak base, write $S \le_{\mathrm{Con}} T$ if the base theory proves $\mathrm{Con}(T) \to \mathrm{Con}(S)$. Write $S \equiv_{\mathrm{Con}} T$ if both directions hold, and $S <_{\mathrm{Con}} T$ if $S \le_{\mathrm{Con}} T$ but not conversely.
:::

:::proposition[$\le_{\mathrm{Con}}$ is a preorder and extensions climb it]{#con-preorder}
$\le_{\mathrm{Con}}$ is reflexive and transitive, and if $S \subseteq T$ then $S \le_{\mathrm{Con}} T$.
:::

:::proof
Reflexivity is the tautology $\mathrm{Con}(S)\to\mathrm{Con}(S)$. Transitivity chains two implications. For the last claim, a proof of a contradiction from $S$ is already a proof from $T$ when $S \subseteq T$, so $\mathrm{Con}(T) \to \mathrm{Con}(S)$ is verified by a primitive recursive transformation of derivations.
:::

:::proposition[Forcing does not change consistency strength]{#forcing-equiconsistent}
$\mathrm{ZFC} \equiv_{\mathrm{Con}} \mathrm{ZFC}+\mathrm{CH} \equiv_{\mathrm{Con}} \mathrm{ZFC}+\neg\mathrm{CH}$.
:::

:::proof
Each theory extends ZFC, so $\mathrm{ZFC}\le_{\mathrm{Con}}$ each by the proposition *$\le_{\mathrm{Con}}$ is a preorder and extensions climb it*. The other direction is the theorem *CH holds in $L$* for the first and the theorem *Cohen's theorem, with the count* for the second: each converts a model of ZFC into a model of the stronger theory, and that conversion is the content of $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}) \to \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\cdots)$.
:::

:::example[Placing statements on the ladder]
Order by consistency strength: ZFC, $\mathrm{ZFC}+\neg\mathrm{CH}$, $\mathrm{ZFC}+\exists$ inaccessible, $\mathrm{ZFC}+\exists$ measurable.
:::

:::solution
1. The first two are equiconsistent by the proposition *Forcing does not change consistency strength*.
2. An inaccessible gives a model of ZFC, so $\mathrm{ZFC} <_{\mathrm{Con}} \mathrm{ZFC}+\exists$ inaccessible by the corollary *ZFC cannot prove an inaccessible exists*.
3. A measurable gives a model of "ZFC + an inaccessible exists" by the corollary *Measurables are far above the first inaccessible*, so it is strictly stronger again.
4. Final answer: $\mathrm{ZFC} \equiv \mathrm{ZFC}+\neg\mathrm{CH} < \mathrm{ZFC}+\exists\text{ inacc} < \mathrm{ZFC}+\exists\text{ meas}$, with exactly two strict jumps.
:::

## What independence means for mathematical truth

<!-- section: philosophical-questions -->

Two positions dominate, and they differ about whether "the universe of sets" names one thing.

The **universe view** holds that there is a single intended $V$, that CH has a truth value in it, and that ZFC is simply an incomplete description. On this reading, independence is a challenge to find the missing axioms. Gödel held it explicitly, expected CH to be false, and hoped large cardinals would decide it — a hope frustrated by Levy and Solovay, who showed that small forcing preserves large cardinals and hence that no large cardinal axiom of the usual kind decides CH.

The **multiverse view**, developed by Hamkins among others, holds that there are many equally legitimate universes of sets, related by forcing and inner-model constructions, and that CH is simply a parameter that varies across them, much as commutativity varies across groups. On this reading, the search for the missing axiom is a category error, and the right object of study is the network of models itself.

:::remark
A third, more technical position runs through Woodin's programme: CH may be decided not by intuition but by *structural* criteria — maximality of the theory of $H(\omega_2)$, or the existence of a canonical inner model for supercompact cardinals. Woodin has argued at different times both for $\neg\mathrm{CH}$ and, later, under the Ultimate-$L$ programme, for CH. The conclusion has not stabilised, which is itself informative.
:::

:::example[What a new axiom must do]
Why is "adopt CH because it is simpler" not accepted as a resolution?
:::

:::solution
1. Simplicity does not survive scrutiny: $\neg$CH follows from forcing axioms such as MA $+ \neg$CH and PFA, which are themselves simple to state and have wide structural consequences.
2. An axiom is accepted when it is *fruitful and coherent*: it should decide many open questions in a uniform way, cohere with the large cardinal hierarchy, and be justified by reflection or maximality principles rather than convenience.
3. Final answer: candidate axioms are judged by consequences and coherence, and on that test CH and $\neg$CH both have serious support.
:::

## Where the subject is now

<!-- section: current-research -->

Three programmes dominate contemporary work, and each is a direct descendant of the material above.

The **inner model programme** seeks canonical models, like $L$ but large enough to contain supercompact cardinals. The current target is **Ultimate-$L$**: Woodin's conjecture that there is an inner model which satisfies GCH, admits a $\Sigma_2$-definable well-ordering, and is compatible with every large cardinal. Two conjectures gate it — the **HOD conjecture** and the $\Omega$-conjecture — and either could fail.

**Forcing axioms** push in the opposite direction. MA, PFA and Martin's maximum assert that generic filters already exist in $V$ for suitable posets, making the universe as *rich* as possible rather than as thin. PFA implies $2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2$ and settles a long list of combinatorial questions; its consistency strength is around a supercompact cardinal, and pinning it down exactly remains open.

**Descriptive set theory under determinacy** shows what large cardinals buy at the level of definable sets: from infinitely many Woodin cardinals one proves that all projective sets are Lebesgue measurable, have the Baire property and the perfect set property. Here independence *stops* — the projective statements are decided, uniformly and in the direction analysts always wanted — which is the strongest existing argument that large cardinal axioms are tracking something real.

:::remark
The techniques have also travelled. Cardinal invariants of the continuum, the structure of $\mathcal{P}(\omega)/\mathrm{fin}$, applications to $C^*$-algebras and to Banach space geometry, and Shelah's PCF theory for singular cardinals — where, remarkably, ZFC proves much more than forcing intuition suggests, such as $2^{\aleph_\omega} < \aleph_{\omega_4}$ when $\aleph_\omega$ is a strong limit — all descend from the two constructions in this chapter.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Saying Cohen proved CH is false.** He proved $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC}+\neg\mathrm{CH})$. Truth is not on the table; see the proposition *What an independence theorem actually asserts*.
- **Dropping the consistency hypothesis.** "ZFC proves CH is independent" is false as stated. What is a theorem is the implication from $\mathrm{Con}(\mathrm{ZFC})$, by the corollary *ZFC cannot prove its own consistency*.
- **Getting the poset order backwards.** $q \le p$ means $q$ is *stronger* and carries *more* information. Reading it as "weaker" inverts every density argument.
- **Forgetting that genericity is relative.** $G$ must meet every dense set *that lies in $M$*, not every dense set. Requiring all of them makes generic filters impossible for nontrivial posets.
- **Expecting the generic filter to be in the ground model.** By the theorem *A generic filter is not in the ground model* it never is, for any poset splitting below every condition — and if it were, the extension would add nothing.
- **Confusing $\mathrm{Def}(X)$ with $\mathcal{P}(X)$.** They agree for finite $X$ only. At $\omega$ the definable power set is countable while the real one has size $2^{\aleph_0}$.
- **Assuming cardinals survive forcing automatically.** They do not. Collapse forcing makes $\aleph_1$ countable. Preservation is a theorem with a hypothesis — ccc, or closure — as in the theorem *ccc forcing preserves cardinals and cofinalities*.
- **Claiming $2^{\aleph_0}$ can be any cardinal.** Not $\aleph_\omega$: the theorem *König's restriction on the continuum* forbids countable cofinality, and monotonicity forbids much else.
- **Treating $V = L$ as a harmless simplifying assumption.** It kills every large cardinal from a measurable up, by the theorem *Scott: a measurable cardinal implies $V \neq L$*.
- **Confusing independence with undecidability.** Independence is about one sentence and one theory; undecidability in the recursion-theoretic sense is about an algorithm for a class of problems. The two words have separate meanings and separate theorems.
