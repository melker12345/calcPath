# Number Theory

Number theory is the study of the integers and their properties — especially divisibility, primes, and modular arithmetic. Though ancient, it powers modern cryptography (RSA, Diffie-Hellman), hashing, random number generation, and error-correcting codes.

We focus on the computational side: recognizing primes, computing gcd efficiently, solving linear congruences, and using the Chinese Remainder Theorem to combine modular information. These algorithms are efficient and form the backbone of public-key cryptography.

## Prime Numbers

<!-- section: primes -->

Prime numbers are the atoms out of which every integer is built by multiplication. Their distribution and arithmetic properties have fascinated mathematicians for millennia and today underwrite the security of internet commerce through RSA and related cryptosystems.

A prime is an integer greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic asserts that every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes (up to order). Euclid's ancient proof that there are infinitely many primes is still the simplest and most elegant; modern proofs use analysis, topology, and probability as well.

In this section you will learn the definition, Euclid's proof of infinitude, the fundamental theorem, basic properties of divisibility and gcd, and the computational techniques (trial division, sieves, Euclidean algorithm) that make number theory practical.

A positive integer $p > 1$ is **prime** if it has no positive divisors other than $1$ and itself. Otherwise it is **composite**. Note the hypothesis $p > 1$: it is part of the definition, not an afterthought.

$1$ is neither prime nor composite. It is excluded on purpose — if $1$ were prime, factorizations would no longer be unique (you could paste in extra copies of $1$), and the theorem below would fail.

There are infinitely many primes. **Euclid's proof by contradiction:** suppose there were only finitely many, $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$. Form $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1$. Dividing $N$ by any $p_i$ leaves remainder $1$, so no $p_i$ divides $N$. But every integer $> 1$ has at least one prime divisor, so $N$ has a prime factor not in our list — contradiction. Hence no finite list can contain all primes.

The **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (FTA):** every integer $n > 1$ factors uniquely (up to the order of the factors) as a product of prime powers:

$$n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}.$$

**Prime factorization** is the key to many divisibility questions: gcd, lcm, divisor counts, and perfect-square tests all fall out of the exponents $e_i$.

**Trial division for primality.** To test whether $n$ is prime, you only need to try dividing by primes up to $\sqrt{n}$. Why $\sqrt{n}$? If $n = a \cdot b$ with $a \le b$, then $a \le \sqrt{n}$ — a composite number always has a factor at or below its square root, so if none is found, $n$ is prime.

:::intuition
Primes are the "atoms" of the integers — you can't break them into smaller integer factors greater than $1$.

Every number is built by multiplying these atoms (with repeats allowed). The factorization is like the unique "ingredient list" with quantities: $180$ is "two 2's, two 3's, and one 5," and no other recipe makes $180$.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Is 91 prime?]
Step 1: Compute the search bound. $\sqrt{91} \approx 9.54$, so we only need to test prime divisors $\le 9$: that is $2, 3, 5, 7$.
:::

:::solution
1. Test each. $91$ is odd (not divisible by $2$); its digit sum is $9+1 = 10$, not a multiple of $3$; it doesn't end in $0$ or $5$; but $91 = 7 \times 13$.
2. We found a factor, so $91$ is **not prime**.
3. Sanity check: $7 \times 13 = 91$ ✓, and both $7$ and $13$ are prime, so $91 = 7 \times 13$ is its full factorization.
:::

:::example[Prime factorization of 180]
Step 1: Peel off the smallest prime repeatedly. $180 = 2 \times 90$; $90 = 2 \times 45$; $45$ is odd, so we move on from $2$.
:::

:::solution
1. $45 = 3 \times 15$; $15 = 3 \times 5$; and $5$ is prime.
2. Collect the factors: $180 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5$.
3. Sanity check: $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 4 \times 9 \times 5 = 180$ ✓.
:::

## Divisors and the Greatest Common Divisor

<!-- section: divisors -->

If $d$ divides $n$ (written $d \mid n$), then $n = d \cdot k$ for some integer $k$; $d$ is a **divisor** (factor) of $n$. Read the bar as "divides": $d \mid n$ is a true/false statement, not the fraction $d/n$.

The **greatest common divisor** $\gcd(a, b)$ is the largest positive integer that divides both $a$ and $b$. When $\gcd(a,b) = 1$ we call $a$ and $b$ **coprime** (relatively prime).

The **least common multiple** $\operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$ is the smallest positive integer that both $a$ and $b$ divide. For positive $a, b$ the two are linked by

$$\gcd(a, b) \cdot \operatorname{lcm}(a, b) = a \cdot b.$$

**Reading gcd and lcm off the factorizations.** If $a = p_1^{a_1} p_2^{a_2} \cdots$ and $b = p_1^{b_1} p_2^{b_2} \cdots$ (using the same list of primes, with a zero exponent when a prime is absent), then the gcd takes the **minimum** exponent of each prime and the lcm takes the **maximum**:

$$\gcd(a,b) = \prod_i p_i^{\min(a_i, b_i)}, \qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \prod_i p_i^{\max(a_i, b_i)}.$$

**Counting divisors.** The number of positive divisors of $n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$ is

$$\prod_{i=1}^{k} (e_i + 1),$$

because each divisor independently chooses an exponent from $0$ up to $e_i$ for prime $p_i$ — that is $e_i + 1$ choices per prime.

:::intuition
Divisors of $12$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$. The common divisors of $12$ and $18$ are $1, 2, 3, 6$; the greatest is $6$.

The gcd is the largest "shared building block" you can make from the primes both numbers own; the lcm is the smallest number roomy enough to contain both as building blocks. Every prime a number owns "min" for the gcd (only the overlap) and "max" for the lcm (whatever either needs).
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Divisor count of 60]
Step 1: Factor. $60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5$, so the exponents are $2, 1, 1$.
:::

:::solution
1. Apply the formula. Number of divisors $= (2+1)(1+1)(1+1) = 3 \times 2 \times 2 = 12$.
2. List them to confirm: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60$ — that is $12$ divisors ✓.
:::

:::example[gcd and lcm via prime factorizations]
Step 1: Factor both. $48 = 2^4 \times 3$ and $180 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. gcd = minimum exponent on each shared prime. For $2$: $\min(4, 2) = 2$. For $3$: $\min(1, 2) = 1$. The prime $5$ appears only in $180$, so its min exponent is $0$. Thus $\gcd(48, 180) = 2^2 \times 3 = 12$.
2. lcm = maximum exponent on each prime. For $2$: $\max(4,2)=4$; for $3$: $\max(1,2)=2$; for $5$: $\max(0,1)=1$. Thus $\operatorname{lcm}(48,180) = 2^4 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 16 \times 9 \times 5 = 720$.
3. Sanity check: $\gcd \cdot \operatorname{lcm} = 12 \times 720 = 8640$ and $48 \times 180 = 8640$ ✓.
:::

## The Euclidean Algorithm

<!-- section: euclid -->

Factoring big numbers is slow, so we rarely compute gcd from prime factorizations. The **Euclidean algorithm** computes $\gcd(a, b)$ efficiently without any factoring. For $a > b > 0$ it uses the identity

$$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, \; a \bmod b).$$

Repeat, replacing the pair $(a, b)$ by $(b, \; a \bmod b)$, until the remainder is $0$; the **last nonzero remainder is the gcd**.

**Why it works:** write $a = qb + r$ where $r = a \bmod b$. Any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ also divides $r = a - qb$, and any common divisor of $b$ and $r$ also divides $a = qb + r$. So the pair $(a,b)$ and the pair $(b, r)$ have exactly the same set of common divisors — and therefore the same greatest one.

The **extended Euclidean algorithm** additionally finds integers $x, y$ (the **Bézout coefficients**) with

$$\gcd(a, b) = a x + b y.$$

Such $x, y$ exist for every pair of integers (this is **Bézout's identity**), and you recover them by back-substituting the remainders from the ordinary Euclidean steps. This is exactly how you find modular inverses in the next sections.

Running time is $O(\log \min(a, b))$ — very fast even for numbers with hundreds of digits.

:::intuition
Instead of listing all divisors of both numbers and hunting for the biggest shared one, you repeatedly replace the bigger number by the remainder it leaves against the smaller — like the ancient "keep subtracting the smaller" game, but sped up by using $\bmod$ to subtract many copies at once.

The extended version rewinds those remainder steps to express the gcd as a combination $a x + b y$ — like figuring out how many of each of two coin denominations add up to a target amount.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[gcd(1071, 462)]
Step 1: $1071 = 2 \times 462 + 147$ (remainder $147$).
:::

:::solution
1. $462 = 3 \times 147 + 21$ (remainder $21$).
2. $147 = 7 \times 21 + 0$ (remainder $0$ — stop).
3. The last nonzero remainder is $21$, so $\gcd(1071, 462) = 21$.
4. Sanity check: $21 \mid 1071$ since $1071 = 21 \times 51$, and $21 \mid 462$ since $462 = 21 \times 22$; and $\gcd(51, 22) = 1$, so $21$ is indeed the *greatest* common divisor ✓.
:::

:::example[Extended Euclidean for gcd(48, 18)]
Step 1: Run the forward algorithm, keeping every equation.
:::

:::solution
$$48 = 2 \times 18 + 12, \qquad 18 = 1 \times 12 + 6, \qquad 12 = 2 \times 6 + 0.$$

The last nonzero remainder is $6$, so $\gcd(48, 18) = 6$.

Back-substitute, starting from the equation that produced the gcd. From the second equation, $6 = 18 - 1 \times 12$.

From the first equation, $12 = 48 - 2 \times 18$. Substitute:

$$6 = 18 - 1 \times (48 - 2 \times 18) = 18 - 48 + 2 \times 18 = 3 \times 18 - 1 \times 48.$$

So $6 = (-1)\times 48 + 3 \times 18$, giving Bézout coefficients $x = -1$, $y = 3$.

Sanity check: $(-1)(48) + (3)(18) = -48 + 54 = 6$ ✓.
:::

## Modular Arithmetic and Congruences

<!-- section: modular -->

Fix a positive integer $m$, called the **modulus**. We say $a \equiv b \pmod{m}$ (read "$a$ is congruent to $b$ modulo $m$") if $m$ divides $a - b$ — equivalently, $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by $m$.

Congruence modulo $m$ is an equivalence relation, so it partitions the integers into $m$ **residue classes** represented by $0, 1, \dots, m-1$. Working "mod $m$" means working with these classes rather than with individual integers.

**Arithmetic respects congruence.** If $a \equiv b \pmod m$ and $c \equiv d \pmod m$, then

$$a + c \equiv b + d, \qquad a - c \equiv b - d, \qquad a c \equiv b d \pmod{m},$$

and consequently $a^k \equiv b^k \pmod{m}$ for every positive integer $k$. This is what lets you reduce numbers *before* adding or multiplying, keeping intermediate values small.

**Warning — division is different.** You may *not* freely cancel a common factor: $a c \equiv b c \pmod m$ does **not** imply $a \equiv b \pmod m$ unless $\gcd(c, m) = 1$. (For example $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 0 \pmod 6$ but $3 \not\equiv 0 \pmod 6$.)

**Linear congruences.** The equation $a x \equiv b \pmod{m}$ has a solution **if and only if** $d = \gcd(a, m)$ divides $b$. When it does, there are exactly $d$ solutions among the residues $0, 1, \dots, m-1$.

**Multiplicative inverse.** The special case $a x \equiv 1 \pmod m$ has a solution — the **inverse** $a^{-1}$ of $a$ modulo $m$ — if and only if $\gcd(a, m) = 1$, and then that inverse is unique modulo $m$. You can compute it with the extended Euclidean algorithm: if $a x + m y = 1$ then $a x \equiv 1 \pmod m$, so $x$ is the inverse.

:::intuition
Modular arithmetic is "clock arithmetic": $10$ o'clock $+ 5$ hours $= 3$ o'clock (mod $12$). You only care about the remainder, so the clock wraps around.

Two numbers are "the same mod $m$" if they differ by a whole number of full laps around the clock (a multiple of $m$).

An inverse of $a$ is the number of laps that undoes multiplying by $a$ — and it only exists when $a$ shares no factor with the clock size, so multiplying by $a$ visits every hour instead of getting stuck on a few.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Compute $7^3 \bmod 5$]
Step 1: Reduce the base first. $7 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$, so $7^3 \equiv 2^3 \pmod 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. $2^3 = 8 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$ (since $8 = 1 \times 5 + 3$).
2. Sanity check by direct computation: $7^3 = 343 = 68 \times 5 + 3$, remainder $3$ ✓.
:::

:::example[Solve $5x \equiv 3 \pmod 7$]
Step 1: Check solvability. $\gcd(5, 7) = 1$, and $1 \mid 3$, so a (unique) solution exists.
:::

:::solution
1. Find the inverse of $5$ modulo $7$. We need $5y \equiv 1 \pmod 7$; testing, $5 \times 3 = 15 = 2\times 7 + 1 \equiv 1$, so $5^{-1} \equiv 3 \pmod 7$.
2. Multiply both sides of $5x \equiv 3$ by $3$: $x \equiv 3 \times 3 = 9 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$.
3. Sanity check: $5 \times 2 = 10 = 7 + 3 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$ ✓.
:::

## The Chinese Remainder Theorem

<!-- section: chinese -->

Sometimes we know a number only by its remainders against several moduli, and want to pin down the number itself. When the moduli are **pairwise coprime**, the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) guarantees this is always possible, uniquely, modulo their product.

**Statement.** Let $m_1, m_2, \dots, m_k$ be **pairwise coprime** (that is, $\gcd(m_i, m_j) = 1$ whenever $i \neq j$). Then the system

$$x \equiv a_1 \pmod{m_1}, \quad x \equiv a_2 \pmod{m_2}, \quad \dots, \quad x \equiv a_k \pmod{m_k}$$

has a **unique solution modulo** $M = m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k$.

The pairwise-coprime hypothesis is essential: without it the system may have no solution at all, or several incompatible ones. (For instance $x \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ and $x \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is impossible, since $\gcd(2,4) \neq 1$.)

**Explicit construction.** Let $M_i = M / m_i$ (the product of all the other moduli). Since the moduli are pairwise coprime, $\gcd(M_i, m_i) = 1$, so $M_i$ has an inverse $y_i$ modulo $m_i$. Then

$$x \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \, M_i \, y_i \pmod{M}.$$

The trick works because $M_i \equiv 0 \pmod{m_j}$ for $j \neq i$, so the $i$-th term contributes to exactly the $i$-th congruence and vanishes in the others. In practice, **successive substitution** (solve two congruences into one, then fold in the next) is often quicker by hand.

CRT lets us break a big-modulus problem into smaller coprime pieces, solve each, then recombine — a technique used heavily in RSA decryption and fast modular arithmetic.

:::intuition
If you know a number's remainder mod $3$, mod $5$, and mod $7$ (all coprime), you can reconstruct the one number between $0$ and $104$ that matches all three — like reading three gears of different tooth counts and figuring out the single position that lines them all up.

Coprimeness is what makes the gears mesh without a shared factor, so the combined cycle has full length $M = 3\times 5 \times 7 = 105$ and no position repeats early.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[$x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, $\;x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$, $\;x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$]
Step 1: Check the hypothesis. $3, 5, 7$ are pairwise coprime, so a unique solution exists modulo $M = 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 105$.
:::

:::solution
1. Fold the first two congruences together by successive substitution. From $x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$ write $x = 5t + 3$. Substitute into $x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$: $5t + 3 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$. Since $5 \equiv 2$ and $3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$, this is $2t \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $t \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ (multiplying by $2^{-1} \equiv 2 \pmod 3$). Write $t = 3s + 1$, giving $x = 5(3s+1) + 3 = 15s + 8$, i.e. $x \equiv 8 \pmod{15}$.
2. Fold in the third congruence. From $x = 15s + 8$, require $15s + 8 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$. Since $15 \equiv 1$ and $8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, this is $s + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$, so $s \equiv 1 \pmod 7$. Write $s = 7u + 1$: $x = 15(7u + 1) + 8 = 105u + 23$, i.e. $x \equiv 23 \pmod{105}$.
3. Sanity check: $23 = 7\times 3 + 2$ so $23 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ ✓; $23 = 4 \times 5 + 3$ so $23 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$ ✓; $23 = 3 \times 7 + 2$ so $23 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$ ✓. The full solution set is $\{23 + 105k\}$.
:::

:::example[Two moduli, $x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, $\;x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$]
Step 1: $3$ and $5$ are coprime, so there is a unique solution mod $M = 15$.
:::

:::solution
1. Write $x = 5k + 3$ from the second congruence. Then $5k + 3 \equiv 2 \pmod 3 \Rightarrow 2k \equiv 2 \pmod 3 \Rightarrow k \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so $k = 3m + 1$ and $x = 15m + 8$.
2. The unique solution is $x \equiv 8 \pmod{15}$.
3. Sanity check: $8 \bmod 3 = 2$ ✓ and $8 \bmod 5 = 3$ ✓. The next few solutions are $8, 23, 38, \dots$ (adding $15$ each time).
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Thinking $1$ is prime (it is not — and if it were, unique factorization would break).
- Forgetting that gcd via primes requires factoring first; the Euclidean algorithm is usually faster and needs no factorization.
- Confusing the "divides" statement $a \mid b$ ("$a$ divides $b$", true/false) with the fraction $a / b$.
- Assuming every linear congruence $ax \equiv b \pmod m$ has a solution — it needs $\gcd(a, m) \mid b$.
- Forgetting that a modular inverse of $a$ exists only when $\gcd(a, m) = 1$.
- Cancelling a common factor in a congruence: $ac \equiv bc \pmod m$ gives $a \equiv b$ only when $\gcd(c, m) = 1$.
- Applying CRT when the moduli are **not** pairwise coprime (there may be no solution, or several modulo the lcm).
- Reporting $a \bmod m$ as a negative remainder instead of normalizing to $\{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$.
- Believing unique factorization holds in every number system — it holds for the integers, but fails in some other rings.
- Running the Euclidean algorithm wrong by not replacing the larger number with the remainder at each step.
- Sign errors when back-substituting the Bézout coefficients in the extended Euclidean algorithm.
