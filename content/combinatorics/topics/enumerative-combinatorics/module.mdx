# Enumerative Combinatorics

Young tableaux, the hook-length formula, Robinson–Schensted correspondence, and systematic enumeration.

Enumerative combinatorics is the part of combinatorics whose central question is "how many are there?" — how many objects of a given type and size exist, often with the goal of finding a closed formula, a generating function, a recurrence, or a bijection with a better-understood set.

It is both an art (finding the right bijection or decomposition) and a science (systematic use of species, GFs, and algebraic combinatorics).

## What Enumerative Combinatorics Studies

<!-- section: what-is-enumerative -->

Typical objects: subsets, permutations, partitions, trees, graphs with given properties, lattice paths, tableaux, matchings, tilings, words avoiding patterns, etc.

The output is usually a formula, a generating function, or an algorithm that computes the count.

:::intuition
"How many ways are there to parenthesize a product of n+1 factors?" (Catalan)

"How many ways can n distinct people sit at a round table?" ((n-1)!)

The subject turns these questions into precise, often beautiful, answers.
:::

:::example
Count the subsets of an $n$-element set. Two clean ways to count give the same answer, which is the heart of enumerative combinatorics.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (direct). Build a subset by deciding, for each of the $n$ elements independently, "in" or "out." That is $2$ choices per element, so $2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2 = 2^n$ subsets.

Step 2 (by size). A subset of size $k$ is a choice of $k$ elements from $n$, counted by the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$. Summing over all possible sizes,
$$\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} = 2^n.$$

Step 3 (sanity check). For $n = 3$ the direct count gives $2^3 = 8$, and the size-by-size count gives $\binom{3}{0} + \binom{3}{1} + \binom{3}{2} + \binom{3}{3} = 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8$. They agree.

The identity $\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ is exactly the kind of result the subject produces: one set of objects (subsets), counted two ways, forces an algebraic identity.

If instead repetitions are allowed and we choose a multiset of size $k$ from $n$ types, the count is the "stars and bars" formula $\binom{n + k - 1}{k}$.
:::

## The Role of Generating Functions

<!-- section: generating-functions-role -->

Ordinary and exponential generating functions turn combinatorial constructions into algebraic operations.

The exponential formula, the symbolic method (Flajolet & Sedgewick), and species theory provide a dictionary: 'sequence', 'set', 'cycle' constructions have direct GF translations.

An **ordinary generating function** (OGF) of a sequence $a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots$ is the formal power series $A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n$; the count $a_n$ is recovered as the coefficient $[x^n] A(x)$. We treat $x$ as a bookkeeping symbol — convergence is not required, since the series is manipulated formally. Use an OGF for unlabeled objects; use an **exponential generating function** (EGF) $\sum_n a_n \frac{x^n}{n!}$ for labeled objects, where relabeling matters.

:::intuition
A generating function is a single "clothesline" that hangs the whole counting sequence on one string: the coefficient of $x^n$ is the $n$th count.

Building objects out of smaller pieces becomes multiplying their clotheslines together, so a counting construction turns into ordinary algebra. You read off answers by extracting coefficients.
:::

:::example
Compute the number of ways to make change for $n$ cents using only $1$-cent and $2$-cent coins (order does not matter), via generating functions.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (build the recipe). Choosing how many $1$-cent coins to use contributes $1 + x + x^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-x}$ (the exponent tracks total value). Choosing how many $2$-cent coins contributes $1 + x^2 + x^4 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-x^2}$.

Step 2 (multiply, because the two choices are independent):
$$A(x) = \frac{1}{1-x}\cdot\frac{1}{1-x^2}.$$

Step 3 (extract a coefficient). The number of ways for value $n$ is $[x^n]A(x) = \lfloor n/2\rfloor + 1$. For $n=4$: $\lfloor 4/2\rfloor + 1 = 3$.

Step 4 (sanity check by hand). The ways to write $4$ using $1$s and $2$s: $2{+}2$, $2{+}1{+}1$, $1{+}1{+}1{+}1$ — exactly $3$. The formula matches.

For integer partitions the same idea gives the classic product $\prod_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{1 - x^k}$, where the $k$th factor $\frac{1}{1-x^k}$ chooses how many parts equal to $k$ to use; the product directly encodes the additive structure.
:::

## Bijective Proofs and Other Techniques

<!-- section: bijective-and-other -->

A bijective proof establishes an identity $a_n = b_n$ by constructing an explicit one-to-one correspondence between the two families of objects.

Other tools: inclusion-exclusion for overcount correction, the reflection principle for ballot/Catalan-type problems, the cycle lemma, LGV lemma for determinants counting non-intersecting paths, transfer matrices for linear recurrences on paths/tilings, and algebraic combinatorics (symmetric functions, representation theory) for more advanced counts.

:::intuition
Instead of computing two ugly sums and showing they equal, you say "here is a perfect matching between the red objects and the blue objects; therefore same number."

The best proofs explain *why* the numbers are equal at a structural level.
:::

:::example
Prove the identity $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1}$ by counting one set of objects two ways. The objects: a committee (any subset) together with a designated chair chosen from the committee.
:::

:::solution
1. Step 1 (count by committee first — the right-hand side of the sum). Pick the committee of size $k$ in $\binom{n}{k}$ ways, then pick its chair in $k$ ways. Summing over all sizes $k$ gives $\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k}$.
2. Step 2 (count by chair first — the closed form). Pick the chair among the $n$ people ($n$ ways). Each of the other $n-1$ people is independently in or out of the committee ($2^{n-1}$ ways). Total $n\,2^{n-1}$.
3. Step 3 (same objects, so equal). Both procedures count exactly the (committee, chair) pairs, hence $\sum_{k} k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1}$.
4. Step 4 (sanity check, $n = 3$). Left side: $0\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 3 + 3\cdot 1 = 0 + 3 + 6 + 3 = 12$. Right side: $3 \cdot 2^{2} = 12$. Agreed.
5. Term by term this is the algebraic identity $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ ("chair-then-rest"), which is itself a two-way count of the same pairs restricted to committee size $k$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking enumeration is 'just listing'**: For large n the point is closed forms or efficient computation, not enumeration by hand.
- **Confusing OGF and EGF**: Choose the right one for the symmetry (labeled vs unlabeled).
- **Giving only a recurrence or GF without extracting the formula**: Sometimes the GF or recurrence *is* the answer; often one wants the explicit [x^n].
- **Overlooking that bijections are the gold standard**: They give understanding; algebraic proofs may be shorter but less explanatory.
- **Ignoring asymptotics**: Many enumerative results are accompanied by asymptotic analysis (singularity analysis, saddle point) giving the growth rate of the counts.

A Young tableau is a filling of a Young diagram (the shape given by an integer partition) with numbers satisfying increasing conditions in rows and columns. Standard Young tableaux (SYT) use each number from 1 to n exactly once and are strictly increasing in rows and columns.

They are central objects in the representation theory of the symmetric group and in many areas of algebraic and enumerative combinatorics.

## Young Diagrams

<!-- section: young-diagrams -->

A partition λ ⊢ n is written in decreasing order λ_1 ≥ λ_2 ≥ … ≥ λ_ℓ > 0 with sum n.

The Young diagram consists of λ_i left-justified boxes in the ith row.

Conjugate partition λ' is obtained by reading column lengths (transposing the diagram).

:::intuition
The diagram is a "Ferrers diagram" of dots or boxes visualizing how the integer n is split into parts.

(4,2,1) looks like four boxes, then two, then one, like a staircase missing some steps.
:::

:::example
Find the conjugate of the partition $\lambda = (5,3,3,1) \vdash 12$.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (draw it). The diagram has rows of lengths $5, 3, 3, 1$ (top to bottom), left-justified.

Step 2 (read column heights). The conjugate $\lambda'$ has $\lambda'_j$ equal to the number of boxes in column $j$, i.e. the number of rows $i$ with $\lambda_i \ge j$.
Column 1: rows with length $\ge 1$ are all four rows $\to 4$.
Column 2: rows with length $\ge 2$ are rows $1,2,3 \to 3$.
Column 3: rows with length $\ge 3$ are rows $1,2,3 \to 3$.
Column 4: rows with length $\ge 4$ is row $1 \to 1$.
Column 5: rows with length $\ge 5$ is row $1 \to 1$.

So $\lambda' = (4,3,3,1,1)$.

Step 3 (sanity checks). Transposing preserves the box count: $4+3+3+1+1 = 12 = |\lambda|$. And conjugation is an involution — transposing $(4,3,3,1,1)$ returns $(5,3,3,1)$.
:::

## Standard Young Tableaux

<!-- section: standard-tableaux -->

An SYT of shape $\lambda \vdash n$ is a filling of the boxes of the diagram with the numbers $1, 2, \dots, n$, each used exactly once, such that entries strictly increase left-to-right along every row and top-to-bottom down every column.

The number $f^\lambda$ of SYT of shape $\lambda$ is the dimension of the irreducible representation of $S_n$ corresponding to $\lambda$ (the Specht module). This is why counting tableaux is more than a curiosity: the same integer measures a shape's tableaux and its representation's size.

:::intuition
You place the numbers 1 through n into the boxes so that everything gets bigger as you go right or down — like a Young diagram version of a sorted matrix.

Different shapes correspond to different "types" of permutations or representations.
:::

:::example
List every SYT of shape $(2,1)$ (two boxes on top, one below the left box), $n = 3$.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (fix what is forced). The entry $1$, being smallest, must sit in the top-left corner: any other position would have a smaller entry to its left or above, breaking strict increase.

Step 2 (place the rest). The remaining $2$ and $3$ go into the top-right box and the bottom-left box. Either order keeps both a row and a column strictly increasing, so both are valid:
$$\begin{array}{ll}\boxed{1}\ \boxed{2}\\ \boxed{3}\end{array} \qquad \begin{array}{ll}\boxed{1}\ \boxed{3}\\ \boxed{2}\end{array}$$

Step 3 (sanity check). Any other placement puts $2$ or $3$ ahead of $1$, or breaks the column, so exactly $\mathbf{2}$ SYT. This matches the hook-length formula $f^{(2,1)} = 3!/(3\cdot 1\cdot 1) = 2$ computed later.
:::

## The Hook-Length Formula and Algorithms

<!-- section: hook-and-other -->

The hook length h(u) of a cell u is 1 + (cells strictly to the right) + (cells strictly below).

The hook-length formula:

$$f^\lambda = \frac{n!}{\prod_{u \in \lambda} h(u)}.$$

There is a beautiful probabilistic "hook-walk" algorithm that samples an SYT uniformly by a sequence of random hook moves; its analysis proves the formula.

:::intuition
The denominator "penalizes" each cell by the size of its hook (the L-shape of cells that "see" it).

The formula magically gives an integer even though it is written as a huge factorial divided by many integers.
:::

:::example
Count the SYT of shape $(2,2)$ ($n = 4$) with the hook-length formula.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (hook length of each cell = 1 + cells strictly to the right + cells strictly below):
$(1,1)$: $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$
$(1,2)$: $1 + 0 + 1 = 2$
$(2,1)$: $1 + 1 + 0 = 2$
$(2,2)$: $1 + 0 + 0 = 1$

Laid on the diagram the hooks read $\begin{smallmatrix}3 & 2\\ 2 & 1\end{smallmatrix}$.

Step 2 (multiply): product of hooks $= 3\cdot 2\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 12$.

Step 3 (apply the formula): $f^{(2,2)} = \dfrac{4!}{12} = \dfrac{24}{12} = 2$.

Step 4 (sanity check by listing). The two SYT are $\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{smallmatrix}$ and $\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 3\\ 2 & 4\end{smallmatrix}$. The filling $\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 4\\ 2 & 3\end{smallmatrix}$ fails (the right column $4 > 3$ decreases), confirming exactly $2$.

The jeu de taquin (sliding) and RSK algorithm (next topic) are algorithmic companions that manipulate tableaux.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing standard with semi-standard**: Semi-standard allow repeats (weakly increasing) and use numbers up to k; they count Kostka numbers and Schur functions.
- **Wrong hook definition**: The hook includes the cell itself; arm + leg + 1.
- **Forgetting that f^λ is always an integer**: The formula proves it, but it is not obvious a priori.
- **Thinking only rectangular shapes matter**: Hooks work for arbitrary shapes; the most famous are for hooks, staircases, rectangles.
- **Mixing row and column reading**: Partitions are usually drawn with longest row on top; columns give the conjugate.

The hook-length formula is a strikingly simple and explicit product formula for the number of standard Young tableaux of a given shape. It is one of the most elegant results in enumerative combinatorics and has numerous proofs (combinatorial, probabilistic, algebraic, bijective).

## Hook Lengths

<!-- section: hook-definition -->

For a cell in row i, column j of the diagram of λ, the hook consists of the cell, the arm (cells to the right in row i), and the leg (cells below in column j).

Hook length h(i,j) = λ_i − j + λ'_j − i + 1 = arm + leg + 1.

:::intuition
Imagine a hook-shaped polyomino sitting at the cell and reaching right and down to the edge of the diagram.

The length is how many cells that hook covers, including the corner cell.
:::

:::example
Compute all hook lengths of shape $(4,2)$. Here $\lambda = (4,2)$, so the conjugate (column heights) is $\lambda' = (2,2,1,1)$. Use $h(i,j) = (\lambda_i - j) + (\lambda'_j - i) + 1$, i.e. arm $+$ leg $+ 1$.
:::

:::solution
Row 1 (length 4):
$(1,1)$: arm $= 4-1 = 3$, leg $= \lambda'_1 - 1 = 2-1 = 1 \Rightarrow 3+1+1 = 5$.
$(1,2)$: arm $= 4-2 = 2$, leg $= 2-1 = 1 \Rightarrow 2+1+1 = 4$.
$(1,3)$: arm $= 4-3 = 1$, leg $= \lambda'_3 - 1 = 1-1 = 0 \Rightarrow 1+0+1 = 2$.
$(1,4)$: arm $= 0$, leg $= 1-1 = 0 \Rightarrow 0+0+1 = 1$.

Row 2 (length 2):
$(2,1)$: arm $= 2-1 = 1$, leg $= 2-2 = 0 \Rightarrow 1+0+1 = 2$.
$(2,2)$: arm $= 2-2 = 0$, leg $= 2-2 = 0 \Rightarrow 0+0+1 = 1$.

On the diagram the hooks read $\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 4 & 2 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{smallmatrix}$.

Sanity check: every corner cell (no cell to its right or below) must have hook $1$ — cells $(1,4)$ and $(2,2)$ do. And the counting of "right $+$ below" agrees with the arm-plus-leg formula in every case.
:::

## The Formula

<!-- section: the-formula -->

If λ ⊢ n, then the number f^λ of standard Young tableaux of shape λ is

$$f^\lambda = \frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j) \in \lambda} h(i,j)}.$$

The product runs over all cells of the diagram.

:::intuition
Take n!, which would be the number of ways to fill the boxes with 1..n in any order.

Divide by the product of hook lengths; each hook "accounts for" the overcounting or the constraints along that L-shape.

The result is exactly the number of the increasing fillings.
:::

:::example
A rectangular case and then a non-rectangular one.
:::

:::solution
Shape $(2,2)$, $n = 4$. Hooks are $3, 2, 2, 1$, product $12$, so $f^{(2,2)} = 4!/12 = 24/12 = 2$. Indeed there are exactly two SYT.

Shape $(4,2)$, $n = 6$. Using the hooks computed in the previous section, $\begin{smallmatrix}5 & 4 & 2 & 1\\ 2 & 1\end{smallmatrix}$, the product is
$$5\cdot 4\cdot 2\cdot 1\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 80,$$
so
$$f^{(4,2)} = \frac{6!}{80} = \frac{720}{80} = 9.$$

Sanity check: $9$ is a positive integer, as any count of tableaux must be — the factorial-over-hook-product expression is guaranteed to come out whole, even though that is not obvious from the fraction $720/80$.
:::

## Proofs and Algorithms

<!-- section: proofs-and-algos -->

- Original proof (Frame–Robinson–Thrall 1954) by induction or manipulation of factorials.
- Probabilistic hook-walk proof (Greene–Nijenhuis–Wilf 1979): a random process that generates SYT with the exact probability given by the formula, thereby proving it.
- Algebraic proofs via the hook-content formula for semi-standard tableaux or via representation theory (Weyl dimension formula in type A).
- There are also bijective proofs and q-analogues (q-hook formula).

The hook walk also gives an efficient practical way to generate a random SYT of given shape uniformly.

:::intuition
The walk: pick a cell at random; while not at a corner, jump to a uniformly random cell in the current hook (including current). When you stop at a corner, remove it and repeat on the smaller diagram, recording the order of removal in reverse to get the filling.

Analysis shows the probability for any particular standard filling is exactly the reciprocal of the hook product times 1/n! .
:::

:::example
Check the hook-walk claim numerically on shape $(2,1)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Step 1 (the claim). The Greene–Nijenhuis–Wilf analysis shows that the corner removed first is cell $(i,j)$ with probability $\prod h / n!$ times an appropriate factor; the net effect is that each complete SYT is produced with probability $\dfrac{\prod_{u} h(u)}{n!}$.
2. Step 2 (plug in). Shape $(2,1)$ has hooks $3, 1, 1$ (product $3$) and $n = 3$, so the predicted probability of any single SYT is $\dfrac{3}{3!} = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac12$.
3. Step 3 (consistency with the count). There are $f^{(2,1)} = 2$ tableaux, each generated with probability $\tfrac12$, and $2 \times \tfrac12 = 1$: the probabilities sum to $1$, as they must. Equivalently, uniform generation ($1/f^\lambda = 1/2$) forces $f^\lambda = n!/\prod h$, which is the hook-length formula. This is the sense in which the sampling algorithm *proves* the formula.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the +1 for the cell itself**: Hook length is never just arm+leg.
- **Computing hooks on the conjugate instead of the shape**: Use the given diagram.
- **Thinking the formula only works for partitions with at most 2 rows**: It is completely general.
- **Confusing with the content hook formula**: The hook-content formula counts semi-standard tableaux: product over cells of (content + k choices) / hook.
- **Expecting the product to obviously divide n!**: The integrality is a non-trivial consequence proved by the existence of the SYT or by the proof techniques.

The Robinson–Schensted (RS) correspondence is a beautiful bijection between permutations of [n] and pairs of standard Young tableaux of the same shape. It provides a combinatorial explanation for many phenomena in the representation theory of the symmetric group and gives a powerful tool for studying the longest increasing and decreasing subsequences of a permutation.

## The Correspondence via Insertion

<!-- section: the-correspondence -->

Given a permutation π = π_1 … π_n , one builds two tableaux P (insertion) and Q (recording) of the same shape by the following process:

- Start with empty P and Q.
- For i = 1 to n: insert the value π_i into the current P using Schensted row-insertion (bumping procedure): place it in the first row by displacing the smallest larger entry to the right; the displaced entry is bumped to the next row, and so on, possibly creating a new box at the end of some row.
- Add the number i to Q in the position of the newly created box in P.

At the end both P and Q are standard Young tableaux of identical shape λ ⊢ n, and every such pair arises from exactly one permutation.

:::intuition
P "sorts" the permutation values according to a bumping rule that maintains the tableau property.

Q records the "history" of when each new box was added.

The pair (P,Q) uniquely encodes the permutation.
:::

:::example
Run RS on $\pi = 2\,3\,1$ step by step. The insertion rule: to insert a value into a row, if it is larger than every entry, append it; otherwise it replaces (bumps) the smallest entry strictly greater than it, and that bumped entry is inserted into the next row down.
:::

:::solution
insert $2$. Row 1 is empty, so $2$ starts a new box.
$$P = \boxed{2}\qquad Q = \boxed{1}$$

insert $3$. Row 1 is $2$; since $3 > 2$ it appends. A new box is created at position $(1,2)$, so we record the step number $2$ there in $Q$.
$$P = \boxed{2}\ \boxed{3}\qquad Q = \boxed{1}\ \boxed{2}$$

insert $1$. Row 1 is $2\,3$; the smallest entry strictly greater than $1$ is $2$, so $1$ bumps $2$: row 1 becomes $1\,3$. The bumped $2$ goes to row 2, which is empty, so it starts a new box at $(2,1)$. Record step number $3$ there in $Q$.
$$P = \begin{smallmatrix}1 & 3\\ 2\end{smallmatrix}\qquad Q = \begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3\end{smallmatrix}$$

Result: $P$ and $Q$ are both SYT of the same shape $(2,1) \vdash 3$, as guaranteed.

Sanity check (via Schensted's theorem, next section): $\pi = 2\,3\,1$ has longest increasing subsequence $2\,3$ of length $2$, matching $\lambda_1 = 2$ (first row of $P$); its longest decreasing subsequence $3\,1$ (or $2\,1$) has length $2$, matching the number of rows $\lambda'_1 = 2$. Both check out.

The process is deterministic and fully reversible: reverse-bumping from $(P, Q)$ recovers $\pi$ uniquely, which is why RS is a bijection.
:::

## Connection to Increasing Subsequences

<!-- section: increasing-subseq -->

Schensted's theorem: the length of the longest increasing subsequence of π is equal to the length of the first row λ_1 of the shape (or of P).

Dually, the length of the longest decreasing subsequence is the length of the first column λ'_1.

This gives an O(n log n) algorithm for LIS via patience sorting (which is essentially the insertion process).

:::intuition
The first row of P at the end "holds" the smallest possible tail of all increasing subsequences of each length; its length is the longest one you can build.

The bumping records the "patience" piles in the solitaire game that models the LIS.
:::

:::example
Take $\pi = 3\,1\,4\,2$ and verify Schensted's theorem directly.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (find the LIS by hand). The increasing subsequences of maximal length are $1\,4$, $1\,2$, and $3\,4$, all of length $2$; no increasing subsequence of length $3$ exists. So $\operatorname{lis}(\pi) = 2$.

Step 2 (build $P$ by insertion):
Insert $3$: $P = \boxed{3}$.
Insert $1$: $1$ bumps $3$ to row 2 $\to P = \begin{smallmatrix}1\\ 3\end{smallmatrix}$.
Insert $4$: $4 > 1$, appends to row 1 $\to P = \begin{smallmatrix}1 & 4\\ 3\end{smallmatrix}$.
Insert $2$: $2$ bumps $4$ in row 1; $4$ appends after $3$ in row 2 $\to P = \begin{smallmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\end{smallmatrix}$.

Step 3 (read off the shape). The shape is $(2,2)$, so $\lambda_1 = 2$. This equals $\operatorname{lis}(\pi) = 2$, confirming Schensted's theorem.

Step 4 (the dual check). The number of rows is $\lambda'_1 = 2$, which should equal the longest *decreasing* subsequence. Indeed $3\,1$, $4\,2$, and $3\,2$ are decreasing of length $2$, and there is no decreasing triple, so $\operatorname{lds}(\pi) = 2$. Consistent.

Because the shape fits in an $r \times s$ box exactly when $\operatorname{lis} \le r$ and $\operatorname{lds} \le s$, a permutation of length $n > rs$ cannot fit any such box, so it must have $\operatorname{lis} > r$ or $\operatorname{lds} > s$ — this is the Erdős–Szekeres theorem.
:::

## Further Properties and Generalizations

<!-- section: properties -->

- The correspondence restricts to a bijection between involutions and SYT (when P=Q).
- The number of permutations with given RS shape λ is (f^λ)^2.
- Hence sum_λ (f^λ)^2 = n! (a representation-theoretic identity proved combinatorially).
- RSK generalization to matrices / biwords gives the combinatorial proof of the Cauchy identity for Schur functions s_λ(x) s_λ(y) = product 1/(1-x_i y_j).

The inverse is obtained by reverse bumping (deletion) from the recording tableau.

:::intuition
When the permutation is an involution the insertion and recording tableaux coincide.

The square (f^λ)^2 counts the "fiber" over each shape — the permutations that "grow" that shape.
:::

:::example
Verify the identity $\sum_{\lambda \vdash n} (f^\lambda)^2 = n!$ for $n = 3$ using RS.
:::

:::solution
Step 1 (why it holds). RS is a bijection between the $n!$ permutations and pairs $(P,Q)$ of SYT of a common shape $\lambda$. For a fixed shape there are $f^\lambda$ choices for $P$ and independently $f^\lambda$ for $Q$, giving $(f^\lambda)^2$ pairs; summing over shapes counts all permutations.

Step 2 (compute each $f^\lambda$ for $n = 3$). The partitions of $3$ are $(3)$, $(2,1)$, $(1,1,1)$:
$f^{(3)} = 1$ (only the increasing row $1\,2\,3$),
$f^{(2,1)} = 2$ (shown earlier),
$f^{(1^3)} = 1$ (only the increasing column).

Step 3 (sum the squares): $1^2 + 2^2 + 1^2 = 1 + 4 + 1 = 6 = 3!$. The identity holds.

Two edge cases worth noting: the increasing permutation is the *unique* permutation of shape $(n)$ (so $(f^{(n)})^2 = 1$), and the decreasing permutation is the unique one of shape $(1^n)$ (so $(f^{(1^n)})^2 = 1$).

Restricting RS to involutions ($\pi = \pi^{-1}$, equivalently $P = Q$) gives $\sum_{\lambda \vdash n} f^\lambda$ = the number of involutions in $S_n$; for $n = 3$ that is $1 + 2 + 1 = 4$ involutions.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing P and Q**: P receives the values of the permutation; Q receives the time steps 1..n.
- **Thinking the shape is always a rectangle**: Most permutations produce irregular shapes.
- **Forgetting that the correspondence is for all permutations, not just involutions**: Involutions are the special case P=Q.
- **Misstating the LIS theorem**: It is the *first row*, not the number of rows (which gives LDS).
- **Believing RSK is only for counting**: It is a fundamental tool that proves deep identities in symmetric function theory via bijective proofs.
