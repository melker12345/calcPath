# Generating Functions & Recurrences

Ordinary and exponential generating functions, and recurrence relations for encoding and solving counting problems.

A generating function packages an entire infinite sequence into a single formal power series. By manipulating the series algebraically we can often extract the nth coefficient — i.e., solve the original counting problem — without listing terms.

Ordinary generating functions (OGFs) are the workhorse for unlabeled or unordered structures; they turn addition of sizes into multiplication of series.

## Ordinary Generating Functions

<!-- section: ordinary-generating -->

For a sequence $a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots$ the ordinary generating function is

$$A(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n x^n = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots$$

The exponent marks the parameter we care about (usually size $n$); the coefficient $a_n$ is the answer to the counting question for that $n$.

:::intuition
Imagine a vending machine that for each possible "size" $n$ drops out $a_n$ different candy combinations. The generating function is the "master list" written as a polynomial you can add and multiply.

The beauty is that algebraic operations on the master list correspond to combining choices.
:::

:::example
How many ways are there to make $n$ cents using only 1-cent coins? For every $n \ge 0$ there is exactly one way (use $n$ pennies), so $a_n = 1$ for all $n$.
:::

:::solution
Write the sequence into a series. The coefficient of $x^n$ must be $a_n = 1$:

$$A(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty 1 \cdot x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$$

Recognize the closed form. This is the geometric series, which as a formal power series equals

$$A(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}.$$

Sanity check: multiply back out. $(1-x)(1 + x + x^2 + \cdots) = 1$, since every term $x^k$ for $k \ge 1$ cancels against $-x \cdot x^{k-1}$. So $\frac{1}{1-x}$ really does have all coefficients equal to $1$. ✓
:::

## Operations and Their Combinatorial Meaning

<!-- section: operations-on-gfs -->

- Sum $A(x) + B(x)$: disjoint union of two classes (an object is *either* an $A$-object *or* a $B$-object).
- Product $A(x)B(x)$: ordered pairs (or sequences of two independent structures) whose sizes add. The coefficient of $x^n$ is the **convolution** $\sum_{k=0}^n a_k\, b_{n-k}$.
- $\frac{1}{1 - A(x)}$: any number (including zero) of copies of structures from $A$, sizes adding (sequences/compositions). This requires $a_0 = 0$ (an atom of size zero would let you insert infinitely many, and the geometric expansion would diverge as a formal series).

These turn recursive or compositional counting into algebra.

:::intuition
Multiplying two GFs is like "first choose a red item of size $k$ (there are $a_k$ ways) and independently a blue item of size $n-k$ (there are $b_{n-k}$ ways)." To land on total size $n$ you sum over every way to split $n = k + (n-k)$, which is exactly the convolution.

The geometric series $\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_n x^n$ corresponds to "any number of 1-cent coins."
:::

:::example
Count the ways to hold a total of $n$ fruits when you have an unlimited supply of identical apples and identical oranges.
:::

:::solution
GF for one fruit type. You can hold $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ apples, one way each, so the apple GF is $1 + x + x^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-x}$; likewise for oranges.

Combine independent choices with a product. Choosing apples *and* oranges are independent and their sizes add, so

$$\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)\!\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right) = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}.$$

Extract the coefficient. Differentiating $\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_n x^n$ term by term gives $\frac{1}{(1-x)^2} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (n+1)\, x^n$. So the coefficient of $x^n$ is $n+1$.

Sanity check: to hold $n$ fruits you pick $k$ apples with $k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ and take the remaining $n-k$ as oranges — that is $n+1$ choices, matching the coefficient. ✓
:::

## Solving Classic Counting Problems

<!-- section: solving-counting-problems -->

Many classic problems become routine once the GF is written:

- Integer partitions → a product over part sizes $\prod_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{1-x^k}$ (each factor supplies "any number of parts of size $k$").
- Compositions (ordered) with part sizes in $\{1, 2, \ldots, m\}$ → $\frac{1}{1 - (x + x^2 + \cdots + x^m)}$.
- Tilings, paths, matchings → often lead to linear recurrences whose characteristic equation appears in the denominator.

After writing the GF one can expand, use partial fractions, or extract $[x^n]$ (the coefficient of $x^n$) via a recurrence or other tools.

:::intuition
"How many ways to pay exactly $n$ cents with standard US coins?" Write the product of geometric series for each denomination; the coefficient of $x^n$ is the answer. Each factor $\frac{1}{1-x^{d}}$ says "use any number of coins of value $d$," and multiplying enforces that the values add up to $n$.

The algebra does the heavy casework for you.
:::

:::example
Count the ways to tile a $2 \times n$ board with $1 \times 2$ dominoes (each covers two squares).
:::

:::solution
Set up a recurrence by looking at the last column. Consider the leftmost column. Either it is covered by one **vertical** domino, leaving a $2 \times (n-1)$ board ($f_{n-1}$ tilings), or the column is covered by the left halves of two **horizontal** dominoes, which forces the next column too and leaves a $2 \times (n-2)$ board ($f_{n-2}$ tilings). These cases are disjoint and exhaustive, so

$$f_n = f_{n-1} + f_{n-2}.$$

Base cases. The empty board has one (empty) tiling, $f_0 = 1$; a $2 \times 1$ board admits only a single vertical domino, $f_1 = 1$. This is the Fibonacci recurrence.

Encode as a GF. Multiplying the recurrence by $x^n$ and summing (as done in detail in the "Solving Techniques" section) gives

$$F(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x - x^2},$$

whose coefficients are exactly the $f_n$.

Sanity check: expanding the recurrence gives $f_0, f_1, f_2, f_3, f_4 = 1, 1, 2, 3, 5$. A $2 \times 2$ board indeed has $2$ tilings (two verticals, or two horizontals), matching $f_2 = 2$. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing ordinary with exponential generating functions**: OGFs for unlabeled/unordered; EGFs (with /n!) for labeled or ordered structures like permutations.
- **Treating the series as convergent real functions too early**: In combinatorics we usually work formally; analytic questions come later.
- **Forgetting the constant term**: The empty structure almost always contributes the $1$ (the $x^0$ term).
- **Misidentifying what the exponent represents**: Make sure $x$ marks exactly the parameter you want to count (size, weight, etc.).
- **Writing the wrong factor for "unlimited supply"**: Unlimited identical items of one type → $\frac{1}{1-x^k}$, not $(1+x^k)$. The factor $(1+x^k)$ means "use that item at most once."

Exponential generating functions (EGFs) are the natural tool when the objects being counted are *labeled* — distinct people, numbered positions, or distinguishable atoms.

The division by n! in the definition cancels the n! ways to label an n-element structure, yielding clean algebraic expressions for sets, sequences, and cycles of labeled components.

## Definition of EGFs

<!-- section: definition-egf -->

The exponential generating function for the sequence $a_n$ (number of structures on an $n$-element labeled set) is

$$A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!} x^n.$$

Here $[x^n]\,A(x)$ denotes the coefficient of $x^n$ in $A(x)$; to recover the count multiply it back by $n!$: $a_n = n!\, [x^n]\, A(x)$.

:::intuition
Because the $n$ labels can be permuted in $n!$ ways, we "pre-divide" by $n!$ so that the messy factorials cancel when we multiply EGFs, leaving clean expressions like $e^x$. Think of it as measuring counts "per labeling."

For permutations of $[n]$ there are $n!$ of them; their EGF simplifies to $\sum_n x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}$ — beautiful.
:::

:::example
Find the EGF for the number of permutations of an $n$-element labeled set, then recover $a_n$.
:::

:::solution
Count the structures. A permutation of $[n]$ is any ordering of the $n$ elements, so $a_n = n!$.

Form the EGF by dividing each term by $n!$:

$$A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{a_n}{n!}\, x^n = \sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{n!}{n!}\, x^n = \sum_{n \ge 0} x^n = \frac{1}{1 - x}.$$

Recover the count. The coefficient of $x^n$ in $\frac{1}{1-x}$ is $1$, so $a_n = n! \cdot [x^n]\,A(x) = n! \cdot 1 = n!$.

Sanity check: for $n = 3$ we get $a_3 = 3! = 6$, and there are indeed $6$ orderings of $\{1,2,3\}$. ✓ Note the contrast with the OGF $\sum n!\, x^n$, which diverges for every $x \ne 0$ — the EGF's $n!$ in the denominator is what tames it.
:::

## Labeled Structures and Set Constructions

<!-- section: labeled-structures -->

For labeled objects the basic building blocks translate to (here $A(x)$ is the EGF of the component class, and each rule requires the components to have positive size, i.e. $a_0 = 0$, so the constructions are well defined):

- A single labeled atom: $x$
- A non-empty set of labeled atoms: $e^x - 1$ (the constant term $1$, meaning the empty set, is subtracted off)
- A sequence of labeled structures (order matters): $\frac{1}{1 - A(x)}$
- A **set** of labeled structures (order does not matter): $\exp\!\big(A(x)\big)$

The intuition for the "set" rule: a set of $k$ components has EGF $\frac{A(x)^k}{k!}$ (the $k!$ divides out the ordering of the $k$ interchangeable components), and summing over $k \ge 0$ gives $\sum_k \frac{A(x)^k}{k!} = \exp(A(x))$. These are far cleaner than the corresponding ordinary generating functions for the same labeled counts.

:::intuition
"A set of cycles" *is* a permutation. A cycle on a labeled set has EGF $-\ln(1-x)$, so the set-of-cycles EGF is $\exp\big(-\ln(1-x)\big) = \frac{1}{1-x}$ — recovering the permutation count.

"Set partitions": a partition is a *set* of non-empty *sets* (the blocks). A non-empty block has EGF $e^x - 1$, so a set of them has EGF $\exp(e^x - 1)$.
:::

:::example
Find the Bell number $B_4$ (the number of ways to partition a $4$-element labeled set into non-empty blocks) from the EGF.
:::

:::solution
Build the EGF. A block is a non-empty set of atoms, EGF $e^x - 1$; a partition is a set of blocks, so $B(x) = \exp(e^x - 1)$.

It is usually easier to use the Bell recurrence $B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} B_k$ (add element $n+1$; choose the $k$ others sharing its block). Starting from $B_0 = 1$:

$B_1 = \binom{0}{0}B_0 = 1$
$B_2 = \binom{1}{0}B_0 + \binom{1}{1}B_1 = 1 + 1 = 2$
$B_3 = \binom{2}{0}B_0 + \binom{2}{1}B_1 + \binom{2}{2}B_2 = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5$
$B_4 = \binom{3}{0}B_0 + \binom{3}{1}B_1 + \binom{3}{2}B_2 + \binom{3}{3}B_3 = 1 + 3 + 6 + 5 = 15$

So $B_4 = 15$, meaning $4! \, [x^4]\, \exp(e^x - 1) = 15$.

Sanity check: the partitions of $\{1,2,3,4\}$ split by block-size pattern as $1$ (all together) $+ 7$ (a $3{+}1$ split, $\binom{4}{1}=4$; a $2{+}2$ split, $3$) $+ 6$ ($2{+}1{+}1$, $\binom{4}{2}=6$) $+ 1$ (all singletons) $= 15$. ✓
:::

## Relation to Ordinary Generating Functions

<!-- section: relation-to-ogf -->

When structures are unlabeled, OGFs are preferred. When labels matter (or when we count permutations, functions, mappings), EGFs usually produce simpler closed forms and composition rules that mirror the combinatorial constructions directly.

Many species in combinatorial enumeration theory are defined via their EGFs.

:::intuition
If the people are distinguishable, EGFs "factor out" the labeling so the algebra sees only the grouping.

Converting between OGF and EGF is possible via Borel or other transforms, but usually we choose the right one from the start: unlabeled → OGF, labeled → EGF.
:::

:::example
Count the derangements $d(n)$ — permutations of $[n]$ with no fixed point — using the EGF.
:::

:::solution
Get the EGF. A permutation is a set of cycles; a derangement is a set of cycles of length $\ge 2$. Dropping fixed points (cycles of length $1$, which contribute $x$ to the cycle EGF) changes the cycle EGF from $-\ln(1-x)$ to $-\ln(1-x) - x$, so

$$D(x) = \exp\big(-\ln(1-x) - x\big) = \frac{e^{-x}}{1 - x}.$$

Extract the coefficient. Since $e^{-x} = \sum_j \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}x^j$ and $\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_i x^i$, their product's coefficient of $x^n$ is $\sum_{j=0}^n \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}$. Therefore

$$d(n) = n!\,[x^n]\,D(x) = n! \sum_{j=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!}.$$

Evaluate at $n = 4$:

$$d(4) = 4!\left(1 - 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{24}\right) = 24 \cdot \tfrac{9}{24} = 9.$$

Sanity check: $\frac{4!}{e} = \frac{24}{2.718\ldots} \approx 8.83$, and $d(n)$ is the nearest integer to $n!/e$, which is $9$. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the $n!$ multiplier when extracting counts from EGF**: $a_n = n!\, [x^n]\, A(x)$, not just the coefficient.
- **Using EGF for unlabeled objects like integer partitions**: The /n! would be meaningless or wrong; use OGF instead.
- **Confusing the constructions**: "set of" is $\exp(A)$, "sequence of" is $\frac{1}{1-A}$; swapping them is a common error.
- **Treating EGFs as probability generating functions**: They are formal counting tools; the /n! is combinatorial, not probabilistic.
- **Ignoring the empty structure**: The constant term is almost always 1 for the empty labeled set.

A recurrence relation defines a sequence by expressing later terms in terms of earlier ones. Many combinatorial counts — tiling numbers, tree counts, path counts, partition numbers — naturally satisfy simple recurrences that can be solved for closed forms or generating functions.

Solving recurrences is the bridge between recursive thinking and explicit formulas.

## Linear Recurrences with Constant Coefficients

<!-- section: linear-recurrences -->

A recurrence is linear homogeneous constant-coefficient of order $d$ if $a_n = c_1 a_{n-1} + \cdots + c_d a_{n-d}$ with $c_d \ne 0$ (so the order is genuinely $d$). "Linear" means each term appears to the first power with no products of terms; "homogeneous" means there is no extra additive term.

The characteristic equation $r^d - c_1 r^{d-1} - \cdots - c_d = 0$ comes from guessing $a_n = r^n$ and dividing through by $r^{n-d}$. Its roots determine the form of the solution: a linear combination of $r_i^n$ over the distinct roots (or, for a root $r$ of multiplicity $m$, the terms $r^n, n r^n, \ldots, n^{m-1} r^n$).

Initial conditions $a_0, \ldots, a_{d-1}$ fix the $d$ free constants.

:::intuition
The Fibonacci rule "each term is sum of previous two" is a linear recurrence. Its solutions are mixtures of the golden-ratio powers.

Once you have the closed form you can compute the 1000th term without iterating 1000 steps.
:::

:::example
Solve $a_n = 2 a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$ with $a_0 = 0$, $a_1 = 1$ (the Pell numbers).
:::

:::solution
Characteristic equation. Substitute $a_n = r^n$: $r^n = 2r^{n-1} + r^{n-2}$; divide by $r^{n-2}$ to get $r^2 - 2r - 1 = 0$.

Roots. By the quadratic formula $r = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 4}}{2} = 1 \pm \sqrt{2}$. Two distinct roots, so the general solution is

$$a_n = A(1+\sqrt2)^n + B(1-\sqrt2)^n.$$

Fit the initial conditions. From $a_0 = 0$: $A + B = 0$, so $B = -A$. From $a_1 = 1$: $A(1+\sqrt2) + B(1-\sqrt2) = 1$. Substituting $B = -A$ gives $A\big[(1+\sqrt2) - (1-\sqrt2)\big] = A \cdot 2\sqrt2 = 1$, so $A = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}$ and $B = -\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}$. Hence

$$a_n = \frac{(1+\sqrt2)^n - (1-\sqrt2)^n}{2\sqrt2}.$$

Sanity check: $a_2 = \frac{(1+\sqrt2)^2 - (1-\sqrt2)^2}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{(3+2\sqrt2) - (3-2\sqrt2)}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{4\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2} = 2$, and directly $a_2 = 2a_1 + a_0 = 2$. ✓
:::

## Combinatorial Recurrences

<!-- section: combinatorial-recurrences -->

Many counting problems yield a recurrence when you condition on the "last piece" or a "root structure": remove one distinguished part, and what remains is a smaller instance of the same problem.

Classic examples: the number of binary trees, polygon triangulations, Dyck paths, tilings, and matchings all obey Catalan- or Fibonacci-style recurrences obtained by case analysis on a distinguished element.

:::intuition
A balanced parentheses string of length $2n$ decomposes uniquely as $(\,w\,)\,u$, where $w$ is balanced of length $2k$ and $u$ is balanced of length $2(n-1-k)$. Summing over $k$ gives the Catalan recurrence $C_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} C_k\, C_{n-1-k}$.

The case split directly writes the recurrence (or the corresponding GF equation).
:::

:::example
Let $a_n$ be the number of ways to climb $n$ stairs taking $1$ or $2$ steps at a time.
:::

:::solution
Condition on the last move. The final move is either a single step (preceded by a climb of $n-1$ stairs, $a_{n-1}$ ways) or a double step (preceded by a climb of $n-2$ stairs, $a_{n-2}$ ways). These are disjoint, so

$$a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}.$$

Base cases. There is one way to climb $0$ stairs (do nothing), $a_0 = 1$, and one way to climb $1$ stair, $a_1 = 1$. This is Fibonacci.

Iterate: $a_2 = 2$, $a_3 = 3$, $a_4 = 5$, $a_5 = 8$.

Sanity check: the ways to climb $3$ stairs are $1{+}1{+}1$, $1{+}2$, $2{+}1$ — exactly $3 = a_3$. ✓
:::

## Solving Techniques and Generating Functions

<!-- section: solving-techniques -->

Besides the characteristic-root method, one can use generating functions: multiply the recurrence by $x^n$, sum over the valid range of $n$, solve the resulting algebraic equation for the GF, then expand or partial-fraction to read off coefficients.

For non-linear or combinatorial recurrences the GF often yields algebraic or functional equations whose solution gives the count via coefficient extraction (or Lagrange inversion for tree-like cases).

:::intuition
The recurrence becomes an equation for the unknown GF; algebra solves for the GF; then you read off coefficients.

For Catalan numbers the GF $C(x)$ satisfies $C = 1 + x C^2$, solved by the quadratic formula; then the binomial series gives the closed form $C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$.
:::

:::example
Derive the GF for the recurrence $a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2}$ (valid for $n \ge 2$) with $a_0 = 1$, $a_1 = 1$.
:::

:::solution
Set up sums. Let $A(x) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n x^n$. Multiply the recurrence by $x^n$ and sum over $n \ge 2$:

$$\sum_{n \ge 2} a_n x^n = \sum_{n \ge 2} a_{n-1} x^n + \sum_{n \ge 2} a_{n-2} x^n.$$

Rewrite each sum in terms of $A(x)$. The left side is $A(x) - a_0 - a_1 x = A(x) - 1 - x$. Re-indexing the right-hand sums:

$$\sum_{n \ge 2} a_{n-1} x^n = x\sum_{m \ge 1} a_m x^m = x\big(A(x) - a_0\big) = x\big(A(x) - 1\big),$$
$$\sum_{n \ge 2} a_{n-2} x^n = x^2 \sum_{m \ge 0} a_m x^m = x^2 A(x).$$

Solve for $A(x)$. Combining,

$$A(x) - 1 - x = x A(x) - x + x^2 A(x).$$

The $-x$ terms cancel, leaving $A(x) - 1 = (x + x^2)A(x)$, so $A(x)\big(1 - x - x^2\big) = 1$ and

$$A(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x - x^2}.$$

Sanity check: expand as a geometric-type series. Since $\frac{1}{1-(x+x^2)} = \sum_k (x+x^2)^k$, the low-order terms give $1 + x + 2x^2 + 3x^3 + \cdots$, whose coefficients $1, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ are the Fibonacci numbers, as expected. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting initial conditions**: The recurrence describes many sequences; without $a_0, \ldots, a_{d-1}$ you cannot determine a unique sequence.
- **Assuming all recurrences are linear constant coeff**: Many natural ones (e.g. involving min or max, or convolutions) are not; they may still have GFs.
- **Wrong characteristic equation**: Sign errors when moving terms: $a_n - c_1 a_{n-1} - \cdots = 0$.
- **Ignoring multiplicity of roots**: Repeated roots require the extra n, n^2, … polynomial factors.
- **Computing only the recurrence without verifying small values**: Always compute the first few terms by hand to check the recurrence setup.
