# Coordinate Geometry

The coordinate plane, distance and midpoint, slope, lines, circles, parabolas, and ellipses.

The Cartesian coordinate system assigns to each point in the plane an ordered pair of real numbers $(x, y)$ relative to two perpendicular axes intersecting at the origin.

It allows geometry to be translated into algebra and is the foundation of analytic geometry, calculus, and computer graphics.

## Axes, Origin, and Coordinates

<!-- section: axes-origin -->

Two perpendicular lines (x-axis horizontal, y-axis vertical) cross at the origin $(0,0)$. The x-coordinate is the *signed* distance to the y-axis (positive to the right, negative to the left); the y-coordinate is the signed distance to the x-axis (positive up, negative down). "Signed" is the key word: the sign is what encodes direction, so $(3,4)$ and $(-3,4)$ are mirror images across the y-axis, not the same point.

The two axes cut the plane into four regions called **quadrants**, numbered counter-clockwise starting from the top-right. Their sign patterns are: I $(+,+)$, II $(-,+)$, III $(-,-)$, IV $(+,-)$. Points that sit *on* an axis (a zero coordinate) belong to no quadrant.

:::intuition
The grid is like a city map with numbered streets (x) and avenues (y). The origin is the downtown crossing.

Positive x is right, positive y is up; signs tell you which "neighborhood" (quadrant) the point is in.
:::

:::example
Locate $(3,-4)$ and name its quadrant.
:::

:::solution
read the x-coordinate: $x = 3 > 0$, so the point is 3 units to the *right* of the y-axis.
Step 2 — read the y-coordinate: $y = -4 < 0$, so the point is 4 units *below* the x-axis.
Step 3 — match the sign pattern $(+,-)$ to the quadrant table: that is quadrant IV.

Sanity check: right-and-down lands in the bottom-right region, which is indeed quadrant IV. By the same reading, $(-2,5)$ has pattern $(-,+)$ (left and up), so it is in quadrant II.
:::

## Distance, Midpoint, and Slope from Coordinates

<!-- section: distance-mid-slope -->

These three formulas all come from the *same* right triangle you can draw between any two points, using the horizontal gap $\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$ and the vertical gap $\Delta y = y_2 - y_1$ as the legs.

Distance between $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$: $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$ (the hypotenuse, by the Pythagorean theorem).

Midpoint: $\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ (average each coordinate).

Slope $m = \dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$, defined only when $x_2 \neq x_1$ (a vertical segment has no run, so its slope is undefined).

:::intuition
Distance is the straight-line "as the crow flies" using the grid legs as a right triangle.

Midpoint is the average address. Slope is how many y-units you rise per x-unit you run (steepness).
:::

:::example
Take the points $(1,2)$ and $(4,6)$ and compute all three quantities.
:::

:::solution
the gaps: $\Delta x = 4 - 1 = 3$ and $\Delta y = 6 - 2 = 4$.
Step 2 — distance: $d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
Step 3 — midpoint: $\left(\dfrac{1+4}{2}, \dfrac{2+6}{2}\right) = (2.5, 4)$.
Step 4 — slope: $m = \dfrac{6-2}{4-1} = \dfrac{4}{3}$.

Sanity check on the midpoint: it should be equidistant from both ends. From $(2.5,4)$ to $(1,2)$ is $\sqrt{1.5^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{2.25 + 4} = \sqrt{6.25} = 2.5$, exactly half of the full distance $5$. Consistent.
:::

## Lines and Circles in Coordinates

<!-- section: lines-circles -->

Line: $y = mx + b$ or $ax + by + c = 0$.

Circle: $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$, with center $(h,k)$ and radius $r > 0$.

These equations turn geometric questions into algebraic ones. The key idea is that a curve *is* the set of all points whose coordinates satisfy its equation, so "does this figure pass through that point?" becomes "does plugging in make the equation true?" and "where do two figures meet?" becomes "solve the two equations at once."

:::intuition
An equation is a rule that every point on the figure obeys. Plug in the coordinates; if true, the point is on the figure.

Solving two equations finds intersection points (where two figures cross).
:::

:::example
Find where the line $y = 2x + 1$ meets the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 5$.
:::

:::solution
substitute the line into the circle so only $x$ remains: $x^2 + (2x+1)^2 = 5$.
Step 2 — expand: $x^2 + 4x^2 + 4x + 1 = 5$, which tidies to $5x^2 + 4x - 4 = 0$.
Step 3 — solve the quadratic. Discriminant $= 4^2 - 4(5)(-4) = 16 + 80 = 96 > 0$, so there are two real intersections. $x = \dfrac{-4 \pm \sqrt{96}}{10} = \dfrac{-4 \pm 4\sqrt{6}}{10} = \dfrac{-2 \pm 2\sqrt{6}}{5}$.
Step 4 — recover $y$ from the line for each root: $y = 2x + 1$.

Sanity check: a positive discriminant means the line cuts the circle at two points, matching the geometry of a line passing through a circle's interior. Numerically $x \approx 0.58$ or $x \approx -1.38$; plugging $x \approx 0.58$ back gives $y \approx 2.16$, and $0.58^2 + 2.16^2 \approx 0.34 + 4.67 \approx 5$. Consistent.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Swapping x and y**: (3,4) is different from (4,3); x is always first (horizontal).
- **Forgetting signs when plotting**: Negative coordinates go left or down.
- **Assuming slope is always defined**: Vertical lines have undefined slope (x = constant).
- **Mixing distance formula with midpoint formula**: One has square root and squares; the other is just averages.

The distance formula gives the Euclidean distance between two points in the plane: $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$.

It is a direct application of the Pythagorean theorem to the differences in coordinates.

## Derivation

<!-- section: derivation -->

Given points $P_1(x_1,y_1)$ and $P_2(x_2,y_2)$, drop a horizontal segment and a vertical segment to the corner point $(x_2, y_1)$. This builds a right triangle with legs $|x_2-x_1|$ (horizontal run) and $|y_2-y_1|$ (vertical rise). The Pythagorean theorem says the hypotenuse squared equals the sum of the squares of the legs, and the hypotenuse *is* the straight-line distance $P_1P_2$:

$$d^2 = |x_2-x_1|^2 + |y_2-y_1|^2 = (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2.$$

The absolute-value bars vanish because squaring already discards sign, so the order of subtraction inside each difference does not matter. Taking the (nonnegative) square root gives the distance formula.

:::intuition
Draw a horizontal and vertical line between the two points; they form the legs of a right triangle. The straight line between the points is the hypotenuse.

Square, add, square-root — the same 3-4-5 idea for any numbers.
:::

:::example
From $(0,0)$ to $(3,4)$: the legs are $3$ and $4$, so $d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. From $(1,2)$ to $(4,6)$: $\Delta x = 3$, $\Delta y = 4$, so $d = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ again — the same triangle, just shifted.
2. Sanity check: $5$ is longer than either leg ($3$ or $4$) but shorter than their sum ($7$), exactly as the triangle inequality requires for a genuine hypotenuse.
:::

## In 3D and Higher

<!-- section: higher-d -->

In 3D: $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}$.

The pattern generalizes to any number of dimensions $n$: subtract corresponding coordinates, square, sum, and take the square root. In vector language this is the Euclidean norm of the difference vector, $d = \lVert \mathbf{P_2} - \mathbf{P_1} \rVert$. (This is the *Euclidean* distance specifically — it assumes flat, straight-line space; on a curved surface such as a sphere the shortest path is different.)

:::intuition
In 3D you just add the squared difference in the third direction (up/down) before taking square root.

It is the "ordinary" straight-line distance we experience in our space.
:::

:::example
From $(0,0,0)$ to $(1,2,2)$:
:::

:::solution
coordinate differences: $\Delta x = 1$, $\Delta y = 2$, $\Delta z = 2$.
Step 2 — square and sum: $1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9$.
Step 3 — square root: $d = \sqrt{9} = 3$.

Sanity check: the largest single coordinate gap is $2$, and the full 3D distance $3$ is larger than any one leg but no larger than their sum $5$. Consistent.
:::

## Applications

<!-- section: applications-dist -->

Used everywhere: length of vectors, collision detection, GPS (great-circle is different but Euclidean is local approximation), computer graphics, physics (displacement magnitude).

:::intuition
Any time you want "how far apart are these two spots on the map or in the game world," you use the distance formula (or its 3D version).

It tells you the length of the arrow from one point to the other.
:::

:::example
Two game characters stand at $(10,20)$ and $(13,24)$, and an attack has range $4$ units.
:::

:::solution
the gaps: $\Delta x = 13 - 10 = 3$, $\Delta y = 24 - 20 = 4$.
Step 2 — distance: $d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
Step 3 — compare to range: $5 > 4$, so the target is out of reach.

Efficiency note used in real game engines: because the square root is the expensive part, engines often compare *squared* distances instead. Here $d^2 = 25$ versus range$^2 = 16$; since $25 > 16$, same conclusion, no square root needed.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting the square root**: The sum of squares is not the distance; you must take the root to get length.
- **Mixing up which is x and y**: Order inside the deltas does not matter (squared), but you must subtract corresponding coordinates.
- **Using Manhattan distance (|dx| + |dy|) by mistake**: That is grid-path distance, not straight-line.
- **Applying in non-Euclidean spaces without adjustment**: On a sphere or in taxicab geometry the formula is different.

The midpoint of a line segment is the point that divides the segment into two equal parts. It is the average of the endpoints.

In coordinate geometry the midpoint of $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ is $\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$.

## Definition and Coordinate Formula

<!-- section: definition-formula -->

The midpoint $M$ of segment $AB$ is the unique point on $AB$ such that $AM = MB$.

In vectors, $M = \dfrac{A + B}{2}$ — the average of the two position vectors. This form is coordinate-free, so it works in 2D, 3D, or any dimension.

In coordinates, that vector average is just the average of the corresponding components, taken independently for $x$ and $y$ (and $z$, if present).

:::intuition
The midpoint is the exact middle of the segment; if you fold the segment in half the crease is at the midpoint.

On a map, the midpoint is the average address of the two ends.
:::

:::example[(forward):]
Endpoints $(2,3)$ and $(8,11)$. Average each coordinate: $x = \dfrac{2+8}{2} = 5$, $y = \dfrac{3+11}{2} = 7$. So $M = (5,7)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Sanity check: the midpoint should be equidistant from both ends. From $(5,7)$ to $(2,3)$ is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, and to $(8,11)$ is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$. Equal, as required.
:::

:::example[(reverse):]
Sometimes you know the midpoint and one endpoint and must find the other. Suppose $M = (3,4)$ is the midpoint of $A = (1,2)$ and unknown $B = (x,y)$.
:::

:::solution
write the midpoint equation coordinate-wise: $\dfrac{1+x}{2} = 3$ and $\dfrac{2+y}{2} = 4$.
Step 2 — solve each: $1 + x = 6 \Rightarrow x = 5$, and $2 + y = 8 \Rightarrow y = 6$.
Step 3 — so $B = (5,6)$.

Shortcut and sanity check: the endpoint is a "reflection" of $A$ through $M$, i.e. $B = 2M - A = (6-1, 8-2) = (5,6)$. Same answer.
:::

## Properties and Uses

<!-- section: properties-uses -->

The midpoint is the center of symmetry of the segment. The line segment joining midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third and half as long (midline theorem).

Midpoints are used in the definition of medians, in vector geometry, and in computer graphics for subdivision.

:::intuition
Connecting the midpoints of a triangle gives a smaller triangle similar and parallel-sided (Varignon's for quads too).

It is the balance point if the segment had uniform mass.
:::

:::example
Verify the midline theorem with concrete coordinates. Take $\triangle ABC$ with $A = (0,0)$, $B = (6,0)$, $C = (2,4)$.
:::

:::solution
midpoint of $AB$: $M = \left(\dfrac{0+6}{2}, \dfrac{0+0}{2}\right) = (3,0)$.
Step 2 — midpoint of $AC$: $N = \left(\dfrac{0+2}{2}, \dfrac{0+4}{2}\right) = (1,2)$.
Step 3 — slope of $MN$: $\dfrac{2-0}{1-3} = \dfrac{2}{-2} = -1$. Slope of $BC$: $\dfrac{4-0}{2-6} = \dfrac{4}{-4} = -1$. Equal slopes, so $MN \parallel BC$.
Step 4 — lengths: $MN = \sqrt{(1-3)^2 + (2-0)^2} = \sqrt{4+4} = 2\sqrt{2}$, and $BC = \sqrt{(2-6)^2 + (4-0)^2} = \sqrt{16+16} = 4\sqrt{2}$.

Sanity check: $MN = 2\sqrt{2}$ is exactly half of $BC = 4\sqrt{2}$, and the slopes match — parallel and half length, exactly as the midline (midsegment) theorem promises.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the distance formula instead of averaging**: Midpoint is not the distance; it is the coordinate average.
- **Forgetting to average both coordinates**: You must do x and y separately.
- **Thinking the midpoint is always the origin**: Only if the segment is centered at 0.
- **Confusing with median**: The median is the segment from vertex to midpoint of opposite side; the midpoint is the end point of the median on the side.

The slope of a line is the ratio of the vertical change (rise) to the horizontal change (run) between any two points on the line: $m = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$.

It measures the steepness and direction of the line.

## Definition and Interpretation

<!-- section: definition-interp -->

For non-vertical lines, $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$.

Positive slope: line rises left to right. Negative: falls. Zero: horizontal (all rise cancels). Undefined: vertical (run is $0$, so the ratio divides by zero — this is why we say "undefined," not "infinite").

The slope is *constant* for any pair of points on a straight line — that constancy is precisely what makes a line straight. Pick any two points and you get the same $m$.

:::intuition
Slope is "how many units up or down you go for each unit you go right."

Positive like climbing a hill to the right; negative like going down; zero flat; undefined a straight up wall.
:::

:::example
Slope through $(1,2)$ and $(3,8)$:
:::

:::solution
$\Delta x = 3 - 1 = 2$, $\Delta y = 8 - 2 = 6$.
Step 2 — $m = \dfrac{6}{2} = 3$.

Interpretation: for every $1$ unit right, the line climbs $3$ units. Sanity check — swap the point order and the sign of both differences flips, leaving the ratio unchanged: $\dfrac{2-8}{1-3} = \dfrac{-6}{-2} = 3$. Slope does not depend on which point you call first.
:::

## Slope-Intercept and Point-Slope Forms

<!-- section: forms -->

Line: $y = mx + b$ (slope $m$, y-intercept $b$).

Point-slope: $y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$, where $(x_0, y_0)$ is any known point on the line.

These make it easy to write the equation from the information you happen to have: use slope-intercept when you know the slope and where the line crosses the y-axis, and point-slope when you know the slope and one arbitrary point (not necessarily on an axis). Both describe non-vertical lines only.

:::intuition
y = m x + b is the "rule": start at b on the y-axis, then for each x step, add m times that step to y.

Point-slope says "from this known point, the change in y is m times change in x."
:::

:::example
Write the line with slope $2$ and y-intercept $1$, then convert it to point-slope form using the point $(3,7)$.
:::

:::solution
slope-intercept: $y = 2x + 1$.
Step 2 — check it passes through $(3,7)$: $2(3) + 1 = 7$. Yes.
Step 3 — point-slope through $(3,7)$: $y - 7 = 2(x - 3)$.
Step 4 — verify the two forms agree by expanding step 3: $y = 2x - 6 + 7 = 2x + 1$. Identical.

Sanity check: at $x = 0$, both forms give $y = 1$ (the y-intercept), confirming they describe the same line.
:::

## Parallel and Perpendicular Slopes

<!-- section: parallel-perp-slopes -->

Parallel lines have equal slopes ($m_1 = m_2$). Two vertical lines are also parallel, even though neither has a defined slope.

Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$ (that is, $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$, equivalently each is the *negative reciprocal* of the other), provided neither line is vertical. The vertical/horizontal pair is the one perpendicular case this rule cannot express, because a vertical line has no slope to multiply.

:::intuition
Same slope = same steepness, never meet (parallel).

Negative reciprocal slopes = "opposite steepness" that make perfect 90° corners.
:::

:::example
The line $y = \tfrac{1}{3}x + 2$ has slope $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
:::

:::solution
Parallel line: keep the same slope, change the intercept — e.g. $y = \tfrac{1}{3}x - 4$.
Perpendicular line: take the negative reciprocal of $\tfrac{1}{3}$, which is $-3$ — e.g. $y = -3x + 5$.

Sanity check on perpendicularity: $\tfrac{1}{3} \cdot (-3) = -1$. The product is $-1$, so the two lines meet at a right angle.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Division by zero for vertical**: Vertical lines have undefined slope; do not say "infinite slope" in calculations.
- **Reversing rise and run**: Slope is rise/run (y over x), not run/rise.
- **Assuming slope 0 means no line**: Horizontal line has slope 0.
- **Forgetting that slope is the same everywhere on the line**: Unlike curves, straight lines have constant slope.

The equation of a line is an algebraic relation satisfied by exactly the points on that line.

Common forms: slope-intercept y = mx + b, point-slope y - y0 = m(x - x0), general ax + by + c = 0, and two-point form.

## Forms and Conversions

<!-- section: forms-conversion -->

From two points, first compute the slope $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$, then substitute into point-slope. Equivalently, use the two-point form directly: $\dfrac{y - y_1}{y_2 - y_1} = \dfrac{x - x_1}{x_2 - x_1}$ (valid when $x_1 \neq x_2$ and $y_1 \neq y_2$).

Special cases the slope forms cannot capture: a vertical line is $x = k$ (undefined slope), and a horizontal line is $y = k$ (slope $0$). The general form $ax + by + c = 0$ *can* represent every line, vertical ones included (take $b = 0$).

:::intuition
The equation is the "membership test": plug in (x,y); if it makes the equation true, the point is on the line.

Different forms are convenient for different information you are given (slope + intercept, point + slope, two points).
:::

:::example
Find the equation of the line through $(1,3)$ and $(4,9)$.
:::

:::solution
slope: $m = \dfrac{9 - 3}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{6}{3} = 2$.
Step 2 — point-slope with $(1,3)$: $y - 3 = 2(x - 1)$.
Step 3 — simplify to slope-intercept: $y = 2x - 2 + 3 = 2x + 1$.

Sanity check: substitute the *other* point $(4,9)$, which we did not use to build the equation: $2(4) + 1 = 9$. It lands on the line, confirming the result.
:::

## Intercepts and Graphing

<!-- section: intercepts-graph -->

x-intercept: set y=0, solve for x.

y-intercept: set x=0, solve for y.

To graph from general form, find intercepts or solve for y.

:::intuition
x-intercept is where the line crosses the horizontal axis (y=0 "no height").

y-intercept is where it crosses the vertical axis (x=0 "no run yet").
:::

:::example
Graph $2x + 3y = 6$ using its intercepts.
:::

:::solution
x-intercept: set $y = 0$, giving $2x = 6$, so $x = 3$. Point $(3,0)$.
Step 2 — y-intercept: set $x = 0$, giving $3y = 6$, so $y = 2$. Point $(0,2)$.
Step 3 — plot $(3,0)$ and $(0,2)$ and draw the line through them.

Sanity check: the slope between the two intercepts is $\dfrac{2 - 0}{0 - 3} = -\dfrac{2}{3}$. Solving the original equation for $y$ gives $y = -\tfrac{2}{3}x + 2$, whose slope is also $-\tfrac{2}{3}$. Consistent.
:::

## Parallel, Perpendicular, and Distance

<!-- section: parallel-perp-dist -->

Parallel: same slope (or both vertical).

Perpendicular: product of slopes $-1$ (excluding the vertical/horizontal pair).

Distance from a point $(x_0,y_0)$ to the line $ax + by + c = 0$ is $d = \dfrac{|ax_0 + by_0 + c|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}$. The numerator measures how far the point is from satisfying the equation, and dividing by $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ (the length of the normal vector $(a,b)$) rescales that into a true perpendicular distance. This requires $(a,b) \neq (0,0)$, which always holds for a genuine line.

:::intuition
Parallel lines have identical "tilt."

Perpendicular lines have "opposite tilts" whose product is -1.

The distance formula is the length of the perpendicular from the point to the line.
:::

:::example
Distance from the origin $(0,0)$ to the line $3x + 4y - 12 = 0$.
:::

:::solution
identify $a = 3$, $b = 4$, $c = -12$.
Step 2 — numerator: $|3(0) + 4(0) - 12| = |-12| = 12$.
Step 3 — denominator: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
Step 4 — distance: $\dfrac{12}{5} = 2.4$.

Sanity check: this line has x-intercept $(4,0)$ and y-intercept $(0,3)$, both at distance $4$ and $3$ from the origin respectively. The perpendicular distance $2.4$ is smaller than either (the perpendicular is the *shortest* route to the line), which is exactly what we expect.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing the general form with =0 but forgetting the constant term sign**: ax + by + c = 0, c can be positive or negative.
- **Assuming all lines have y = mx + b form**: Vertical lines do not.
- **Reversing the distance formula numerator/denominator**: The denominator normalizes by the length of the normal vector.
- **Forgetting to check if a point satisfies the equation when verifying**: Always plug back in.

The equation of a circle with center $(h, k)$ and radius $r > 0$ is $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$.

Every point $(x, y)$ on the circle satisfies this; it is precisely the set of points at exact distance $r$ from the center. In fact the equation is just the distance formula with the square root removed: "distance from $(x,y)$ to $(h,k)$ equals $r$" becomes "distance *squared* equals $r^2$."

## Standard and General Form

<!-- section: standard-general -->

Standard: $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$. The center $(h,k)$ and radius $r$ can be read off directly.

General: $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$. This is what you get after expanding the standard form and collecting terms; the center and radius are hidden until you complete the square. (Note the $x^2$ and $y^2$ coefficients are both $1$ and there is no $xy$ term — that is what marks the equation as a circle rather than another conic.)

:::intuition
The standard form says "distance to (h,k) is exactly r" in squared form (no square root needed).

The general form is the expanded polynomial; completing the square recovers the center and radius.
:::

:::example
Read center and radius from standard form: $(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 9$. Matching $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$ gives $h = 2$, $k = -1$ (note the sign flip: $y + 1 = y - (-1)$), and $r = \sqrt{9} = 3$. Center $(2,-1)$, radius $3$.
:::

:::solution
Now recover the same circle from general form $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 4 = 0$.

group: $(x^2 - 4x) + (y^2 + 2y) = 4$.
Step 2 — complete the square in $x$: half of $-4$ is $-2$, squared is $4$, so $x^2 - 4x = (x-2)^2 - 4$.
Step 3 — complete the square in $y$: half of $2$ is $1$, squared is $1$, so $y^2 + 2y = (y+1)^2 - 1$.
Step 4 — substitute: $(x-2)^2 - 4 + (y+1)^2 - 1 = 4$, i.e. $(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 9$.

Sanity check: this is the identical circle from the first part, center $(2,-1)$ and radius $3$.
:::

## Finding Center and Radius from Equation

<!-- section: find-center-radius -->

Given general form $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$, group the $x$ and $y$ terms, complete the square for each, and read off $h = -\dfrac{D}{2}$, $k = -\dfrac{E}{2}$, and $r = \sqrt{h^2 + k^2 - F}$. The equation represents a real circle only when $h^2 + k^2 - F > 0$; if it equals $0$ you get a single point, and if it is negative there are no real points at all.

:::intuition
Completing the square is 'fixing' the equation so it looks like distance squared.

The numbers that get added/subtracted when completing tell you how far the center is shifted from (0,0).
:::

:::example
Find the center and radius of $x^2 + 6x + y^2 - 4y = 12$.
:::

:::solution
complete the square in $x$: half of $6$ is $3$, squared is $9$, so $x^2 + 6x = (x+3)^2 - 9$.
Step 2 — complete the square in $y$: half of $-4$ is $-2$, squared is $4$, so $y^2 - 4y = (y-2)^2 - 4$.
Step 3 — substitute: $(x+3)^2 - 9 + (y-2)^2 - 4 = 12$, i.e. $(x+3)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 25$.
Step 4 — read off: center $(-3, 2)$, radius $r = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

Sanity check: the point $(2, 2)$ is $5$ units right of the center, so it should lie on the circle. Plugging in: $(2+3)^2 + (2-2)^2 = 25 + 0 = 25$. It does.
:::

## Intersection with Lines and Other Circles

<!-- section: intersections -->

To intersect a line with a circle, substitute the line into the circle equation to get a quadratic in one variable. Its discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ tells the number of intersections: $\Delta > 0$ gives two points (the line is a secant), $\Delta = 0$ gives one (tangent), and $\Delta < 0$ gives none (the line misses the circle).

For two circles, subtract one general equation from the other. The $x^2$ and $y^2$ terms cancel, leaving a linear equation — the *radical axis*, which passes through any common points. Solve that line against either circle.

:::intuition
A line can miss, touch (tangent), or cut the circle at two points — the quadratic tells which.

The points in common satisfy both equations simultaneously.
:::

:::example
Intersect the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ with the line $y = x$.
:::

:::solution
substitute $y = x$: $x^2 + x^2 = 25$, so $2x^2 = 25$.
Step 2 — solve: $x^2 = 12.5$, so $x = \pm\sqrt{12.5} = \pm\dfrac{5}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \pm 3.54$.
Step 3 — since $y = x$, the two intersection points are $\left(\tfrac{5}{\sqrt 2}, \tfrac{5}{\sqrt 2}\right)$ and $\left(-\tfrac{5}{\sqrt 2}, -\tfrac{5}{\sqrt 2}\right)$.

Sanity check: each point should be exactly $5$ from the origin (the radius). Distance $= \sqrt{12.5 + 12.5} = \sqrt{25} = 5$. Two real solutions confirm the line passes straight through the circle, as a line through the center must.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Forgetting to complete the square correctly (sign errors on h,k)**: h = -D/2, easy to drop the minus.
- **Using the equation for a sphere by mistake**: Sphere has three variables.
- **Assuming the general form always represents a circle**: If the radius squared comes out negative, it is an imaginary circle (no real points).
- **Confusing radius with diameter in the equation**: The r in the equation is always the radius.

A parabola is the set of points in the plane equidistant from a fixed point (the focus) and a fixed line (the directrix).

It is one of the conic sections and the graph of a quadratic function $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ (with $a \neq 0$).

## Definition and Standard Forms

<!-- section: definition-forms -->

Standard vertical: $(x - h)^2 = 4p(y - k)$, with focus $(h, k+p)$ and directrix $y = k - p$.

Standard horizontal: $(y - k)^2 = 4p(x - h)$, with focus $(h+p, k)$ and directrix $x = h - p$.

The vertex sits at $(h,k)$, and $p$ is the focal length — the signed distance from the vertex to the focus. Its sign controls the opening direction: for the vertical form, $p > 0$ opens upward and $p < 0$ opens downward.

:::intuition
Every point on the parabola is exactly as far from the focus "magnet" as from the directrix "wall."

The vertex is the "bottom" (or side) where it turns around.
:::

:::example
Find the focus and directrix of $y = \tfrac{1}{4}x^2$.
:::

:::solution
rewrite in standard form: multiply by $4$ to get $x^2 = 4y$. Comparing with $x^2 = 4p\,y$ (vertex at the origin, $h = k = 0$) gives $4p = 4$, so $p = 1$.
Step 2 — focus is $(h, k+p) = (0, 1)$.
Step 3 — directrix is $y = k - p = -1$.

Sanity check with the vertex $(0,0)$, which must be equidistant from focus and directrix: distance to the focus $(0,1)$ is $1$, and distance to the line $y = -1$ is $1$. Equal, as the definition demands.
:::

## Reflective Property and Applications

<!-- section: reflective-apps -->

Rays parallel to the axis reflect off the parabola and pass through the focus (and vice versa).

Used in satellite dishes, headlights, telescopes, and the paths of projectiles (under constant gravity, ignoring air resistance).

:::intuition
A parabolic mirror sends all parallel incoming rays (from a distant star or satellite) to one point (the focus), where you put your receiver.

Headlights do the reverse: light at the focus bounces into a parallel beam.
:::

:::example
Satellite dish shaped as paraboloid of revolution. Signal from satellite (essentially parallel rays) hits the dish and reflects to the feed horn at the focus. All paths have equal length, signals arrive in phase.
:::

## Vertex Form and Completing the Square

<!-- section: vertex-form -->

$y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ is vertex form, showing the vertex $(h,k)$ directly. The coefficient $a$ determines width and direction: $a > 0$ opens upward, $a < 0$ downward, and larger $|a|$ makes a narrower parabola.

Completing the square on $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ converts it to vertex form. Factor $a$ out of the first two terms first, since the leading coefficient must be $1$ inside the square before you complete it.

:::intuition
Vertex form shows the 'turning point' (h,k) and how 'wide' (a) the parabola opens directly.

Completing the square is the algebra trick that reveals the vertex without graphing.
:::

:::example
Convert $y = 2x^2 + 8x + 5$ to vertex form.
:::

:::solution
factor $2$ from the $x$-terms: $y = 2(x^2 + 4x) + 5$.
Step 2 — complete the square inside: half of $4$ is $2$, squared is $4$, so $x^2 + 4x = (x+2)^2 - 4$.
Step 3 — substitute: $y = 2\big((x+2)^2 - 4\big) + 5 = 2(x+2)^2 - 8 + 5 = 2(x+2)^2 - 3$.
Step 4 — read the vertex: $(h, k) = (-2, -3)$; since $a = 2 > 1$, it opens upward and is narrower than $y = x^2$.

Sanity check: plug $x = -2$ into the original equation: $2(4) + 8(-2) + 5 = 8 - 16 + 5 = -3$. The vertex $y$-value matches.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing focus/directrix with vertex**: The vertex is midway between focus and directrix.
- **Mixing vertical and horizontal parabolas**: y = x^{2} is vertical (opens up/down); x = y^{2} is horizontal.
- **Forgetting the 4p in the standard form**: The coefficient is 4p, not p.
- **Thinking all quadratic graphs are parabolas in the geometric sense**: The geometric definition (focus/directrix) is equivalent to the algebraic quadratic only after rotation/translation in the plane.

An ellipse is the set of points whose *sum* of distances to two fixed points (the foci) is constant.

It is a conic section (a stretched circle) and the shape of planetary orbits (Kepler's first law). Contrast it with the hyperbola, which fixes the *difference* of the two distances instead of the sum.

## Definition and Standard Equation

<!-- section: definition-standard -->

For foci at $(-c,0)$ and $(c,0)$ with major axis length $2a$, the equation is $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, where $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$ and $0 < c < a$ (so $0 < b \le a$). The center is the origin, midway between the foci.

The constant sum of distances is exactly $2a$. The relation $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$ is not arbitrary: evaluating the sum at the top co-vertex $(0,b)$ forces a right triangle with hypotenuse $a$ and legs $b$ and $c$.

:::intuition
Two pins (foci) and a string of fixed length; the pencil traces the ellipse as it keeps the string taut.

The sum of distances to the two pins is always the same (the string length).
:::

:::example
Build the equation of the ellipse with foci $(-3,0)$ and $(3,0)$ and constant distance-sum $10$.
:::

:::solution
from the sum: $2a = 10$, so $a = 5$.
Step 2 — from the foci: $c = 3$ (each focus is $3$ from the center).
Step 3 — find $b$: $b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$, so $b = 4$.
Step 4 — assemble: $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$.

Sanity check at the co-vertex $(0,4)$: distance to each focus is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, and $5 + 5 = 10$, matching the required sum.
:::

## Vertices, Co-vertices, Eccentricity

<!-- section: vertices-ecc -->

For an ellipse centered at the origin with foci on the x-axis, the vertices are $(\pm a, 0)$ (ends of the major axis) and the co-vertices are $(0, \pm b)$ (ends of the minor axis). Eccentricity $e = \dfrac{c}{a}$, and since $0 \le c < a$ it always satisfies $0 \le e < 1$ ($e = 0$ is a circle, where the foci coincide at the center).

The major axis is the longer one (length $2a$); the minor axis is the shorter (length $2b$).

:::intuition
Vertices are the 'ends' of the long direction; co-vertices the 'ends' of the short direction.

Eccentricity tells how 'squashed' the ellipse is: 0 perfect circle, close to 1 very flat.
:::

:::example
For the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$ from the previous section ($a = 5$, $b = 4$, $c = 3$):
:::

:::solution
Vertices: $(\pm 5, 0)$.
Co-vertices: $(0, \pm 4)$.
Eccentricity: $e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{3}{5} = 0.6$.

Sanity check: $0 < 0.6 < 1$, confirming a genuine (non-circular, non-parabolic) ellipse. At $0.6$ it is fairly round but visibly elongated — closer to $0$ would be nearly circular, closer to $1$ would look like a flattened cigar.
:::

## Reflective Property and Orbits

<!-- section: reflective-orbits -->

Rays from one focus reflect off the ellipse and go to the other focus.

Planets orbit in ellipses with the sun at one focus (Kepler). The other focus is 'empty.'

:::intuition
Sound or light from one focus bounces and converges at the other focus (whispering galleries).

Gravity from the sun at one focus keeps the planet on the ellipse; the math of inverse-square force produces conics.
:::

:::example
In an elliptical billiard table, a ball shot from one focus in any direction will pass through the other focus after one bounce.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing sum (ellipse) with difference (hyperbola)**: Ellipse: sum constant. Hyperbola: |difference| constant.
- **Swapping a and b**: a is always the semi-major (larger), b semi-minor; c^{2} = a^{2} - b ^{2}.
- **Thinking the center is at a focus**: The center is midway between the foci; foci are offset by c from center.
- **Forgetting e < 1**: Eccentricity of ellipse is between 0 and 1; =1 parabola, >1 hyperbola.
