# Secret Sharing and Secure Computation

Splitting a secret so that any t shares reveal it and any t−1 reveal nothing, and computing on data nobody is allowed to see.

## Threshold Secret Sharing

<!-- section: threshold-ss -->

:::definition[(k,n)-threshold secret sharing]{#threshold-scheme}
In a $(k,n)$-threshold scheme a dealer splits a secret $s$ into $n$ shares $s_1,\dots,s_n$ such that

- any set of $k$ or more shares determines $s$ uniquely, and
- any set of $k-1$ or fewer shares reveals no information about $s$: the distribution of those shares is independent of $s$.

The scheme is **perfect** when the second condition is information-theoretic, that is

$$I\bigl(s;\ \text{any } k-1 \text{ shares}\bigr) = 0.$$
:::

Perfection is expensive in one specific way: each share must be at least as large as the secret itself, in bits. That is an information-theoretic lower bound, proved in the properties section below.

:::intuition
Imagine a combination to a safe. You want any 3 out of 5 bank managers to be able to open it together, but any 2 should learn nothing that helps them guess the combination.

The dealer gives each manager a 'piece' of the combination. Only when 3 pieces are brought together do they fit into the full combination; any 2 pieces look like random garbage unrelated to the real combination.
:::

:::example
The simplest XOR construction is a $(2,2)$ scheme (both shares needed, one learns nothing); we build it first, then note why extending to a *perfect* $(2,3)$ needs a bit more care.
:::

:::solution
**Perfect $(2,2)$ scheme.** For a 128-bit secret $s$, the dealer picks a uniform random 128-bit $r$ and sets share1 $=r$, share2 $=s\oplus r$.
**Privacy:** each single share is uniform and independent of $s$ — share1 $=r$ is uniform by construction, and share2 $=s\oplus r$ is uniform because XOR-ing a fixed value with a uniform independent pad yields a uniform result. So one share gives zero information.
**Reconstruction:** share1 $\oplus$ share2 $=r\oplus(s\oplus r)=s$, since $r\oplus r=0$.
**Concrete 3-bit run.** $s=101$, dealer draws $r=011$. Then share1 $=011$, share2 $=101\oplus011=110$. Reconstruct: $011\oplus110=101=s$ ✓. Neither $011$ nor $110$ alone reveals anything: for each candidate secret there is exactly one pad making that share appear, so all secrets stay equally likely. **Sanity check:** recovers $101$, and each share is uniform ✓.
**Extending to $(2,3)$.** A common toy version writes share1 $=r$, share2 $=s\oplus r$, share3 $=s$; this is *not* perfect, because share3 alone equals $s$. A genuinely perfect $(2,3)$ threshold scheme is obtained from Shamir's polynomial scheme (next section) with $k=2$, where every single share is uniform and any two reconstruct.
:::

## Shamir's Polynomial Scheme

<!-- section: shamir -->

The idea: hide the secret as the y-intercept of a random curve, and hand each participant one point on it. Recovering a degree-$(k-1)$ curve needs $k$ points, so $k$ participants can find the intercept and fewer cannot — this is exactly the threshold property, achieved with pure linear algebra.

Shamir's (k,n) scheme works over a finite field F_q with q > n and secret s ∈ F_q. We need a *field* so that interpolation (which divides by coordinate differences) is well-defined, and $q>n$ so there are enough distinct nonzero evaluation points $1,\dots,n$ to give every participant one.

:::recipe[Shamir's dealing procedure]{#shamir-scheme}
1. Choose a random polynomial $p(x)$ of degree at most $k-1$ with $p(0) = s$ as its constant term; the other $k-1$ coefficients are drawn uniformly at random.
2. Hand participant $i$ the point $(i, p(i))$, for $i = 1, \dots, n$ — distinct nonzero field elements.
:::

:::theorem[Threshold property of Shamir's scheme]{#shamir-threshold}
Any $k$ shares determine $s$, and any $k-1$ shares leave it completely undetermined.
:::

:::proof
Any $k$ points determine a unique degree-$(k-1)$ polynomial, by Lagrange interpolation, and hence determine $p(0)=s$. Any $k-1$ points, on the other hand, are consistent with exactly $q$ degree-$(k-1)$ polynomials — one for each value of the missing coefficient, which sets $p(0)$ to any field element you please — so every candidate secret remains equally possible.
:::

:::intuition
The secret is the y-intercept of a random polynomial of degree k-1. Each share is one point on that hidden curve.

k points uniquely determine the whole curve (and therefore where it crosses the y-axis). k-1 points can be extended to a curve that crosses the y-axis at any value you like, so they give no clue.
:::

:::example
$(k=3, n=5)$, field $F_{17}$, secret $s=7$. Dealer picks random $a_1=3$, $a_2=12$; $p(x) = 7 + 3x + 12 x^2 \mod 17$.
:::

:::solution
1. Shares: $p(1)=7+3+12=22 \mod 17=5$.
2. $p(2)=7+6+48 \mod 17=7+6+14=27 \mod 17=10$.
3. $p(3)=7+9+108 \mod 17=7+9+6=22 \mod 17=5$ (calc: $12*9=108$, $108/17=6*17=102$, $108-102=6$).
4. Shares handed out: $(1,5),(2,10),(3,5)$ (and $p(4),p(5)$ for the other two participants).
5. **Reconstruct from any 3 points.** Take $(1,5),(2,10),(3,5)$ and recover $p(0)$ by Lagrange interpolation at $x=0$. The Lagrange weights at $x=0$ are $\ell_i(0)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{0-x_j}{x_i-x_j}$:
6. $\ell_1(0)=\frac{(0-2)(0-3)}{(1-2)(1-3)}=\frac{6}{2}=3$.
7. $\ell_2(0)=\frac{(0-1)(0-3)}{(2-1)(2-3)}=\frac{3}{-1}=-3\equiv 14 \pmod{17}$.
8. $\ell_3(0)=\frac{(0-1)(0-2)}{(3-1)(3-2)}=\frac{2}{2}=1$.
9. Then $s=p(0)=\sum_i y_i\,\ell_i(0)=5\cdot 3+10\cdot 14+5\cdot 1=15+140+5=160 \bmod 17$. Since $160=9\cdot17+7=153+7$, we get $s=7$. **Sanity check:** recovers the original secret $7$ ✓.
10. Any $k-1=2$ points, say $(1,5),(2,10)$, are consistent with a quadratic passing through *any* chosen $p(0)=s'$: the third free coefficient can always be solved for, so the two points leak nothing about $s$.
:::

## Properties, Extensions, and Impossibility Results

<!-- section: properties-extensions -->

:::theorem[Share size lower bound]{#share-size-bound}
In a perfect $(k,n)$ scheme over a secret of entropy $H(s)$, each share has entropy at least that much:

$$H(\text{share}) \ge H(s).$$
:::

The reason is that otherwise the $k-1$ shares plus the missing one could not uniquely determine a high-entropy secret while the $k-1$ alone give zero information.

Linear secret sharing schemes (shares are linear functions of the secret and randomness) are equivalent to monotone span programs and have efficient realizations for many access structures.

General access structures (not just thresholds) can be realized by realizing the minimal authorized sets and using monotone formulas or by the Benaloh-Leichter construction.

Verifiable secret sharing (VSS) adds the ability for participants to check that their shares are consistent with some secret (protects against a cheating dealer). Proactive secret sharing periodically refreshes shares so that an adversary who compromises shares over time still cannot collect k at any epoch.

Multi-secret and ramp schemes trade off reconstruction threshold vs privacy for multiple secrets or partial leakage.

:::intuition
You cannot make shares smaller than the secret in the perfect information-theoretic setting; information has to 'flow' to the qualified sets.

For general who-can-reconstruct rules (e.g., 'any two managers plus the CEO, or any four managers') there are systematic ways to build the sharing from the simple threshold building blocks or from linear algebra over vector spaces.
:::

:::example
The lower bound proof: fix an unauthorized set $A$ of size $k-1$. By perfect privacy their joint shares are independent of $s$, so $H(s \mid \text{shares}_A)=H(s)$.
:::

:::solution
1. For a qualified set $B=A\cup\{p\}$, the shares of $B$ determine $s$, i.e. $H(s\mid\text{shares}_A,\text{share}_p)=0$.
2. Subtracting, the one extra share must remove all remaining uncertainty: $I(s;\text{share}_p\mid\text{shares}_A)=H(s)-0=H(s)$. Since conditional mutual information is at most the entropy of either argument, $H(\text{share}_p)\ge H(\text{share}_p\mid\text{shares}_A)\ge I(s;\text{share}_p\mid\text{shares}_A)=H(s)$.
3. **Sanity check with the XOR $(2,2)$ scheme:** a 128-bit secret has $H(s)=128$ bits, and each share is a uniform 128-bit string with $H(\text{share})=128$ bits — the bound $H(\text{share})\ge H(s)$ holds with equality, so XOR sharing is size-optimal.
:::

## The Secure MPC Problem

<!-- section: mpc-problem -->

:::definition[Secure multiparty computation]{#mpc}
Parties $P_1, \dots, P_n$ hold private inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and wish to compute

$$y = f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$$

such that **correctness** holds — all honest parties obtain the correct $y$ — and **privacy** holds: nothing is revealed about the $x_i$ beyond what $y$ itself reveals.
:::

:::definition[Simulation-based security]{#simulation-paradigm}
Security is defined via the simulation paradigm: for any adversary controlling a subset of parties, there exists a simulator that, given only the inputs of the corrupted parties and the output $y$, can produce a view indistinguishable from the real protocol execution.
:::

The function f can be any efficiently computable function (arithmetic circuit, Boolean circuit, RAM program, etc.).

:::intuition
Several hospitals want to compute the average salary of their doctors or the statistics of a rare disease across all their patient records, without any hospital seeing another hospital's raw data.

The protocol lets them obtain only the final statistic; the individual records stay private.
:::

:::example
Yao's "millionaires' problem": two parties want to know who is richer without revealing their wealth. Define $f(x,y)=1$ if $x>y$ else $0$.
:::

:::solution
**Concrete inputs.** Alice has $x=\$7\text{M}$, Bob has $y=\$5\text{M}$ (each private).
**Ideal functionality.** Imagine a trusted third party who takes $x$ and $y$, computes $f(x,y)$, and announces only the bit. Here $7>5$, so it announces $1$.
**What MPC guarantees.** A real MPC protocol (no trusted party) must reveal exactly what the ideal functionality would: both learn the bit $1$, and *nothing more*. In particular Bob does not learn that Alice has $7$M, nor even a range like "$>5$M" — only "Alice is richer."
**What the output does imply.** Privacy is only "beyond what $y$ reveals." From output $1$, Bob deduces $x>5$; that is unavoidable because it is implied by the function's own answer. **Sanity check:** the simulator for Bob, given Bob's input $y=5$ and the output $1$, can reproduce Bob's entire view — confirming no extra leakage. If instead $x=4$, the output would be $0$ and Alice would learn $y>4$; again only what the answer itself entails.
:::

## Yao's Garbled Circuits for Two Parties

<!-- section: yao-garbled -->

Yao's protocol (1986) solves the two-party case (semi-honest model) using garbled circuits.

:::recipe[Yao's garbled circuit protocol]{#yao-garbled}
1. The **garbler** takes a Boolean circuit for $f$ and replaces every wire with two random keys, one for $0$ and one for $1$.
2. For each gate it creates a *garbled table* of four ciphertexts that encrypt the correct output key under the two input keys, in random order.
3. The **evaluator** obtains the keys corresponding to its own input bits via oblivious transfer (OT).
4. The evaluator walks the circuit gate by gate, decrypting exactly one entry per gate with the keys it holds, and finally obtains the output keys and, with the mapping, the output bits.
:::

The garbler sends the garbled tables plus the keys for its own input; the evaluator learns nothing about the garbler's input except through the output.

:::intuition
The garbler creates an encrypted 'maze' that computes the function. Each wire is a pair of secret keys; each gate is a lookup table that, given the right two input keys, lets you decrypt exactly the right output key.

The evaluator walks the maze using keys for his inputs (obtained without the garbler learning which ones) and at the end holds only the output keys, which map to the answer.
:::

:::example
**Setup.** Garble one AND gate with input wires $A,B$ and output wire $C$. The garbler picks six random 128-bit keys: $K_A^0,K_A^1$ for wire $A$; $K_B^0,K_B^1$ for wire $B$; $K_C^0,K_C^1$ for wire $C$. The map key $\to$ bit is secret.
:::

:::solution
1. **Truth table of AND:** output is $1$ only when both inputs are $1$, i.e. $0\wedge0=0$, $0\wedge1=0$, $1\wedge0=0$, $1\wedge1=1$.
2. **Garbled table.** For each input combination, encrypt the *correct* output key under the two input keys. Writing $\mathrm{Enc}_{K,K'}(m)$ for double encryption under both keys:
3. $\mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^0,K_B^0}(K_C^0)$
4. $\mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^0,K_B^1}(K_C^0)$
5. $\mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^1,K_B^0}(K_C^0)$
6. $\mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^1,K_B^1}(K_C^1)$
7. The garbler randomly permutes these four rows (so row position leaks no bit) and sends them to the evaluator.
8. **Evaluation.** Say the evaluator holds $K_A^0$ (its own bit is $0$) and $K_B^1$ (obtained by OT, garbler doesn't know which). It tries to decrypt each row; the double-encryption authenticates so that only the row keyed by *exactly this pair* decrypts successfully — that is the second row, yielding $K_C^0$.
9. **What is learned.** The evaluator now holds $K_C^0$ but not $K_C^1$, so it cannot tell that $0$ corresponds to output-bit $0$ unless the garbler also reveals the output map — hence intermediate wires stay hidden and only the final output map is disclosed. **Sanity check:** the true gate value is $0\wedge1=0$, matching $K_C^0$ ✓. The evaluator never sees $K_A^1$ or $K_B^0$, so it learns nothing about the garbler's input beyond the gate output.
:::

## Information-Theoretic MPC with Secret Sharing (BGW)

<!-- section: bgw-ss -->

:::theorem[BGW theorem]{#bgw}
For $n$ parties with an honest majority, meaning $t < n/2$ corruptions, the BGW protocol (Ben-Or, Goldwasser, Wigderson 1988) achieves information-theoretic security using Shamir secret sharing.
:::

Each input is shared using a (t+1, n) Shamir scheme. Addition of shared values is local (just add shares). Multiplication of two shared values requires an interactive degree-reduction step (each party locally multiplies its shares, re-shares the product, then the parties jointly compute a linear combination that reduces the degree back to t).

With these two primitives any arithmetic circuit can be evaluated securely. For dishonest majority (t ≥ n/2) information-theoretic security is impossible for general functions without honest majority; computational assumptions or additional setup (e.g., OT) are required.

:::intuition
Everyone splits their private number into 'shares' that look random individually and gives one share to each other person.

Adding two shared numbers is easy: everyone just adds the shares they hold.

Multiplying is harder; it produces higher-degree shares that must be 'cleaned up' by an interactive protocol that uses fresh randomness so that the final shares still hide the product.
:::

:::example
Compute the sum of three private salaries $s_1+s_2+s_3$ with $t=1$ (any one party may be corrupt), so we use degree-1 (2-out-of-3) sharings over $F_{17}$. Parties evaluate at $x=1,2,3$.
:::

:::solution
**Inputs and polynomials.** Say $s_1=4,\ s_2=9,\ s_3=2$ (secrets, kept private). Each party picks its own random linear polynomial with the secret as constant term:
$P_1(x)=4+5x$ (party 1 chose slope $5$).
$P_2(x)=9+2x$.
$P_3(x)=2+11x$.
**Distribute shares.** Party $j$ receives $P_i(j)$ from each dealer $i$. Compute mod $17$:
Party 1 ($x=1$): $P_1(1)=9,\ P_2(1)=11,\ P_3(1)=13$.
Party 2 ($x=2$): $P_1(2)=14,\ P_2(2)=13,\ P_3(2)=24\equiv7$.
Party 3 ($x=3$): $P_1(3)=19\equiv2,\ P_2(3)=15,\ P_3(3)=35\equiv1$.
**Local addition.** Each party adds the three shares it holds (no communication):
Party 1: $9+11+13=33\equiv16 \pmod{17}$.
Party 2: $14+13+7=34\equiv0 \pmod{17}$.
Party 3: $2+15+1=18\equiv1 \pmod{17}$.
   These are shares of $Q(x)=P_1(x)+P_2(x)+P_3(x)=15+18x\equiv 15+x \pmod{17}$, whose constant term is $Q(0)=4+9+2=15$, the true sum.
**Reconstruct from any two.** Interpolate the summed shares $(1,16)$ and $(2,0)$ at $x=0$: weights $\ell_1(0)=\frac{0-2}{1-2}=2$, $\ell_2(0)=\frac{0-1}{2-1}=-1\equiv16$. Then $Q(0)=16\cdot2+0\cdot16=32\equiv15 \pmod{17}$. **Sanity check:** $s_1+s_2+s_3=4+9+2=15$ ✓.
**Privacy.** A single party sees three field elements (its own summed share $16$, say) that are independent of the true sum, because one point on a degree-1 polynomial is uniform; only $t+1=2$ shares reveal the sum. Addition needed zero interaction; multiplication would raise the degree to $2t=2$ and require the interactive degree-reduction step.
:::

## Security Models and Feasibility

<!-- section: security-models -->

- Semi-honest (honest-but-curious): parties follow the protocol but try to learn extra information from the messages they receive. Easier to achieve; many protocols are first designed in this model and then compiled to malicious security.
- Malicious (Byzantine): corrupted parties can deviate arbitrarily (send wrong messages, abort, etc.). Requires zero-knowledge proofs, commitments, or verifiable secret sharing to force correct behavior.
- Honest majority vs. dishonest majority: with t < n/2 information-theoretic security is possible for any function (BGW). With t ≥ n/2 computational assumptions are necessary for most functions (Yao + OT for 2 parties; GMW for many parties).
- With preprocessing (Beaver triples, OT extension, etc.) online phases can be extremely fast (information-theoretic or symmetric-crypto only).

Universally composable (UC) security gives security even when the MPC protocol is used as a subroutine inside larger protocols.

:::intuition
Semi-honest is like 'the other people follow the rules but are nosy and will try to infer your salary from the messages they legitimately receive.'

Malicious is 'the other people may lie, send garbage, or collude to try to learn or corrupt the result.'

With an honest majority you can use pure information-theoretic tricks (secret sharing + interaction). Without it you need cryptography (one-way functions, oblivious transfer) to create 'virtual honest majority' or to detect cheating.
:::

:::example[(GMW compiler):]
Turn a semi-honest protocol into a maliciously-secure one.
:::

:::solution
**Commit to inputs.** Every party first commits to its private input $x_i$ and to the randomness $r_i$ it will use, so it cannot later change them.
**Each message is a deterministic function.** In a fixed protocol, the next message a party should send is $m=\text{NextMsg}(x_i,r_i,\text{transcript})$ — a public function of the committed values and the messages seen so far.
**Prove correctness with zero-knowledge.** Alongside each message $m$, the party sends a zero-knowledge proof that "$m$ is the correct output of $\text{NextMsg}$ for the values I committed to, given this transcript" — without revealing $x_i$ or $r_i$.
**Effect.** A malicious party can no longer send a crafted wrong message: any deviation fails the proof and is caught, while honest behavior leaks nothing new (the proof is zero-knowledge). **Sanity check:** correctness is now enforced (matching the semi-honest guarantee) and privacy is preserved, so the compiled protocol is secure against Byzantine adversaries — at the cost of the extra ZK proofs, which is exactly the "cost of malicious security" noted in Common Mistakes.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that in Shamir's scheme the polynomial is evaluated at the participant identities only**: The x-coordinates are public identifiers; the y-values (or the pair) are the actual shares that must be kept secret.
- **Believing that k-1 shares give 'some' partial information about s**: In a perfect scheme they give zero information; the posterior on s is identical to the prior (uniform).
- **Using a small field for a large secret**: Shamir is usually applied to field elements; for a long secret one either uses a large field or shares the secret bit-by-bit / block-by-block with independent polynomials.
- **Forgetting that the dealer must be trusted to choose the polynomial correctly**: If the dealer is malicious he can give inconsistent shares. Verifiable secret sharing solves this.
- **Assuming computational secret sharing (e.g., using one-way functions) is the same as information-theoretic**: Computational schemes can have shorter shares but rely on hardness assumptions and may be broken by future algorithms or quantum computers.
- **Confusing secret sharing with threshold cryptography or multiparty computation**: Secret sharing is the distribution step; threshold crypto uses shares of a key to perform operations without reconstructing the key in the clear; MPC is the broader interactive protocol setting.

Secure multiparty computation (MPC, or SMPC) lets a set of parties, each holding a private input, compute a joint function of all inputs while revealing only the output (and whatever is implied by it). No party learns anything else about the other parties' inputs.

MPC is the broadest primitive in cryptography that generalizes secret sharing, zero-knowledge, and private set intersection. It can realize any 'mental poker' or 'millionaires' problem in a provably secure way.
- **Thinking MPC always requires a trusted third party**: The whole point is to remove the trusted party; the protocol itself emulates the ideal functionality.
- **Assuming that 'the output reveals nothing'**: By definition the output y is revealed; privacy only protects the inputs beyond what is implied by y (e.g., if f is the sum, the sum itself is learned but not the individual addends).
- **Believing information-theoretic security is always possible**: It requires an honest majority (or a private channel + honest majority). Dishonest majority needs computational assumptions.
- **Confusing 2-party Yao with multi-party secret-sharing MPC**: Yao is usually computational (via OT) and for two parties; BGW-style is information-theoretic for honest majority and uses Shamir sharing + interaction for multiplication.
- **Forgetting the cost of malicious security**: Semi-honest protocols can be very cheap (linear in circuit size); malicious security often adds heavy zero-knowledge or cut-and-choose overhead, although recent protocols (e.g., SPDZ, authenticated triples) have made malicious security practical.
- **Assuming MPC is only for 'millionaires' or toy functions**: Modern MPC frameworks (MP-SPDZ, SCALE-MAMBA, EMP-toolkit, etc.) routinely evaluate AES, neural-network inference, and complex database queries among dozens of parties.

Differential privacy (DP) is a rigorous, quantifiable definition of privacy for data analysis and machine learning. It asks that a randomized algorithm behave almost identically on any two datasets differing in at most one record, so that no output can betray whether a particular individual took part; the formal statement is given in the next section.

The parameter ε (the privacy budget) controls the strength of the guarantee: smaller ε gives stronger privacy. The definition makes no assumptions about the adversary's background knowledge or computational power.

DP has become the gold standard for private data release at companies (Apple, Google, Microsoft, Uber) and in the U.S. Census Bureau's 2020 release.
