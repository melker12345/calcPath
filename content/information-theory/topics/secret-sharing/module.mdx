# Secret Sharing and Secure Computation

Splitting a secret so that any t shares reveal it and any t−1 reveal nothing, and computing on data nobody is allowed to see.

This chapter is read in one direction. The first half builds a single object — a *linear* threshold sharing of a field element, with a proof that unqualified sets learn exactly nothing — and the second half spends it: the BGW protocol for secure multiparty computation is Shamir's scheme plus two operations on shares, and the famous threshold $t < n/2$ is nothing more than the arithmetic of polynomial degrees. Read the second half asking, at every step, *which property of the sharing is being used here*.

## Threshold Secret Sharing

<!-- section: threshold-ss -->

A secret sharing scheme splits a secret into pieces so that *authorised* coalitions can put it back together and *unauthorised* ones learn nothing at all. Everything hinges on making "nothing at all" a theorem rather than a hope, so we set the definitions up information-theoretically from the start.

:::definition[Access structure]{#access-structure}
Let $P = \{P_1,\dots,P_n\}$ be the participants. An **access structure** is a family $\Gamma \subseteq 2^{P}$ of subsets, called **authorised** (or *qualified*), which is **monotone**:

$$A \in \Gamma \ \text{ and } \ A \subseteq B \ \implies\ B \in \Gamma.$$

Subsets not in $\Gamma$ are **unauthorised**. The $(k,n)$-**threshold structure** is $\Gamma_{k,n} = \{A \subseteq P : |A| \ge k\}$.
:::

Monotonicity is not a technical convenience; it is forced. If $A$ can reconstruct the secret then so can any superset of $A$ — the extra participants can simply stay silent. A non-monotone rule ("these three may reconstruct, but not if the fourth is in the room") cannot be enforced by handing out data, because data cannot be taken back.

:::definition[Perfect secret sharing scheme]{#perfect-scheme}
A dealer holds a secret $s$ drawn from a distribution on a set $\mathcal{S}$ and, using private randomness $R$, computes shares $S_1,\dots,S_n$, giving $S_i$ to $P_i$. Write $S_A = (S_i)_{i \in A}$. The scheme **realises** $\Gamma$ **perfectly** if

- **(Correctness)** for every $A \in \Gamma$ there is a reconstruction function $\mathrm{Rec}_A$ with $\mathrm{Rec}_A(S_A) = s$ with probability $1$; equivalently $H(s \mid S_A) = 0$;
- **(Perfect privacy)** for every $A \notin \Gamma$,

$$I(s\,;\,S_A) = 0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad H(s \mid S_A) = H(s).$$
:::

The mutual-information form is the one to remember, but in constructions it is easier to verify the following equivalent statement, which says the shares an unauthorised coalition sees are *statistically unrelated* to what was shared.

:::proposition[Three faces of perfect privacy]{#privacy-equivalent}
For an unauthorised set $A$ the following are equivalent.

1. $I(s; S_A) = 0$.
2. $s$ and $S_A$ are independent random variables.
3. For all secrets $s_1, s_2 \in \mathcal{S}$ and all share vectors $y$, $\Pr[S_A = y \mid s = s_1] = \Pr[S_A = y \mid s = s_2]$.
:::

:::proof
(1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (2) is the standard fact that mutual information vanishes exactly for independent variables: $I(s;S_A) = \sum \Pr[s,y]\log\frac{\Pr[s,y]}{\Pr[s]\Pr[y]}$ is a relative entropy between the joint law and the product of the marginals, and a relative entropy is zero if and only if its two arguments coincide.

(2) $\Rightarrow$ (3): independence makes $\Pr[S_A = y \mid s = s_j] = \Pr[S_A = y]$, the same number for both $j$. (3) $\Rightarrow$ (2): if the conditional law of $S_A$ is a fixed distribution $Q$ for every secret, then $\Pr[S_A=y] = \sum_{s'}\Pr[s']Q(y) = Q(y)$, so $\Pr[s=s', S_A=y] = \Pr[s']Q(y) = \Pr[s']\Pr[S_A=y]$.
:::

Condition (3) is the working criterion: *the adversary's view has a distribution that does not mention the secret.* It is also, in miniature, the simulation paradigm of the second half of this chapter — a view you can sample without knowing the secret is a view that does not contain the secret.

:::intuition
Think of a safe whose combination should open only when three of five managers agree. Each manager is handed a slip of paper. Any three slips together name the combination exactly; any two slips are, as far as their holders can tell, two uniformly random strings that would have looked exactly the same no matter what the combination was. That last clause — *would have looked exactly the same* — is perfect privacy. It is stronger than "hard to invert"; there is nothing to invert.
:::

The simplest non-trivial scheme realises the $(n,n)$ structure, where every participant is needed.

:::theorem[XOR sharing realises $(n,n)$ perfectly]{#xor-scheme}
Let the secret be $s \in \{0,1\}^{\ell}$. Draw $r_1,\dots,r_{n-1}$ independently and uniformly from $\{0,1\}^{\ell}$ and set

$$S_i = r_i \ (1 \le i \le n-1), \qquad S_n = s \oplus r_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus r_{n-1}.$$

Then $S_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus S_n = s$, and any $n-1$ of the shares are uniformly distributed on $(\{0,1\}^{\ell})^{n-1}$ independently of $s$.
:::

:::proof
Correctness is immediate: XOR-ing all $n$ shares cancels every $r_i$ twice and leaves $s$.

For privacy fix an unauthorised set, i.e. any $A$ with $|A| = n-1$; let $j$ be the missing index. If $j = n$, then $S_A = (r_1,\dots,r_{n-1})$ is uniform and does not involve $s$, so criterion (3) of *Three faces of perfect privacy* holds trivially. If $j < n$, then $S_A$ consists of the $n-2$ pads $\{r_i\}_{i \neq j, i<n}$ together with $S_n = s \oplus r_j \oplus \bigoplus_{i\neq j, i<n} r_i$. Condition on the pads that appear: the remaining randomness is $r_j$, uniform and independent, and the map $r_j \mapsto s \oplus r_j \oplus c$ is a bijection of $\{0,1\}^{\ell}$ for every fixed $s$ and $c$. Hence $S_n$ is uniform on $\{0,1\}^{\ell}$ and independent of the other components, *for every value of $s$*. The conditional law of $S_A$ is therefore the uniform law regardless of $s$, which is criterion (3).
:::

:::example[A $(2,2)$ sharing by hand]
Share the $3$-bit secret $s = 101$ between two people, and check both properties.
:::

:::solution
The dealer draws $r = 011$ uniformly and sets $S_1 = 011$, $S_2 = 101 \oplus 011 = 110$.

Reconstruction: $011 \oplus 110 = 101 = s$. ✓

Privacy: suppose you hold only $S_2 = 110$. For *each* of the eight candidate secrets $s'$ there is exactly one pad $r' = s' \oplus 110$ producing what you see, and each pad had probability $1/8$. So $\Pr[S_2 = 110 \mid s = s'] = 1/8$ for all $s'$ — the same number eight times — and by criterion (3) your posterior on $s$ equals your prior. ✓ □
:::

:::pitfall
A frequently offered "$(2,3)$ scheme" sets $S_1 = r$, $S_2 = s \oplus r$, $S_3 = s$. Any two shares do reconstruct, so correctness holds — but $\{P_3\}$ is unauthorised and already knows everything, so privacy fails completely. Correctness is easy; a scheme is only as good as the proof of its privacy clause.
:::

Threshold structures are not the only ones we can realise. The following construction settles the general question, at a price we will quantify in the section *Share Size, Verifiability, and Extensions*.

:::theorem[Every monotone access structure is realisable]{#ito-nishizeki-saito}
Let $\Gamma$ be any monotone access structure on $n$ participants with minimal authorised sets $B_1,\dots,B_m$. Then $\Gamma$ is realised perfectly by the **replication scheme**: independently produce, for each $j$, an $(|B_j|,|B_j|)$ XOR sharing of $s$ and hand its pieces to the members of $B_j$. Participant $P_i$'s share is the list of all pieces it received.
:::

:::proof
*Correctness.* If $A \in \Gamma$ then $A \supseteq B_j$ for some $j$, so the members of $A$ jointly hold every piece of the $j$-th XOR sharing and can XOR them to get $s$.

*Privacy.* Let $A \notin \Gamma$. For each $j$ we have $B_j \not\subseteq A$, so $A$ misses at least one piece of the $j$-th sharing; by *XOR sharing realises $(n,n)$ perfectly* the pieces of that sharing held by $A$ are uniform and independent of $s$. The $m$ sharings use independent randomness, so the whole of $S_A$ is a uniform string of the appropriate length whose distribution does not depend on $s$. Criterion (3) applies.
:::

Applying this to $\Gamma_{2,3}$ gives shares consisting of two pieces each, and applying it to $\Gamma_{k,n}$ in general gives shares of size $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ secrets — exponential in $n$. That is intolerable, and it is the reason the next section exists: replacing combinatorics by algebra collapses the share down to a *single* field element.

## Shamir's Polynomial Scheme

<!-- section: shamir -->

Shamir's idea (1979) is to hide the secret as the value at $0$ of a random low-degree polynomial and to hand each participant one other point of it. Recovering a polynomial of degree at most $k-1$ takes $k$ points and no fewer, so the threshold property is inherited from a theorem of linear algebra rather than engineered by hand.

Work in a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q > n$. A field is essential: interpolation divides by differences of evaluation points, so those differences must be invertible, which fails in a ring like $\mathbb{Z}_{6}$. The condition $q > n$ guarantees $n$ distinct *nonzero* evaluation points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ — nonzero because $x=0$ is reserved for the secret itself.

:::theorem[Lagrange interpolation]{#lagrange}
Let $x_1,\dots,x_k$ be distinct elements of a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $y_1,\dots,y_k \in \mathbb{F}$ be arbitrary. Then there is exactly one polynomial $p$ of degree at most $k-1$ with $p(x_i) = y_i$ for all $i$, namely

$$p(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} y_i\,\ell_i(x), \qquad \ell_i(x) = \prod_{j \neq i} \frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j}.$$
:::

:::proof
*Existence.* Each $\ell_i$ has degree $k-1$, and $\ell_i(x_j) = \delta_{ij}$: the numerator vanishes at every $x_j$ with $j \neq i$, and at $x = x_i$ numerator and denominator agree. Hence $p(x_j) = \sum_i y_i \delta_{ij} = y_j$. The denominators are nonzero because the $x_j$ are distinct, which is where the field structure is used.

*Uniqueness.* If $p$ and $\tilde p$ both work, $d = p - \tilde p$ has degree at most $k-1$ and $k$ distinct roots. A nonzero polynomial of degree $\le k-1$ over a field has at most $k-1$ roots (factor out $(x-x_1)$ repeatedly), so $d \equiv 0$.
:::

Evaluating at $x=0$ gives the form we will use over and over: the secret is a *fixed linear combination* of any $k$ shares,

$$p(0) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i\,y_i, \qquad \lambda_i = \ell_i(0) = \prod_{j\neq i}\frac{-x_j}{x_i - x_j},$$

with coefficients $\lambda_i$ that depend only on *which* $k$ evaluation points were used — not on the secret, not on the randomness, not on the values. These **recombination coefficients** are public. Remember them; the whole of BGW is built on the fact that reconstruction is linear.

:::recipe[Shamir's dealing procedure]{#shamir-scheme}
1. Fix $\mathbb{F}_q$ with $q > n$ and public distinct nonzero points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ (usually $x_i = i$).
2. Draw $a_1,\dots,a_{k-1}$ independently and uniformly from $\mathbb{F}_q$ and set

$$p(x) = s + a_1x + a_2x^2 + \cdots + a_{k-1}x^{k-1}.$$

3. Give participant $P_i$ the share $S_i = p(x_i)$.
:::

:::theorem[Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly]{#shamir-threshold}
For every $k \le n < q$ the scheme above is a perfect $(k,n)$-threshold scheme over $\mathbb{F}_q$: any $k$ shares reconstruct $s$, and for any set $A$ with $|A| = k-1$ the vector $S_A$ is uniformly distributed on $\mathbb{F}_q^{\,k-1}$ **independently of $s$**.
:::

:::proof
*Correctness.* A set of $k$ participants holds $k$ pairs $(x_i, p(x_i))$ with distinct $x_i$, and $\deg p \le k-1$, so by *Lagrange interpolation* those pairs determine $p$ uniquely; then $s = p(0) = \sum_i \lambda_i p(x_i)$.

*Privacy.* Fix $A = \{i_1,\dots,i_{k-1}\}$ and fix a target vector $y = (y_1,\dots,y_{k-1}) \in \mathbb{F}_q^{\,k-1}$. Fix any candidate secret $s' \in \mathbb{F}_q$. We count the polynomials the dealer could have drawn that are consistent with both.

The dealer's polynomial ranges over
$$\mathcal{P}_{s'} = \{p : \deg p \le k-1,\ p(0) = s'\},$$
uniformly, because $a_1,\dots,a_{k-1}$ are uniform and independent; note $|\mathcal{P}_{s'}| = q^{\,k-1}$ for every $s'$.

Now the $k-1$ constraints $p(x_{i_j}) = y_j$ together with the constraint $p(0) = s'$ are $k$ interpolation conditions at $k$ *distinct* points — distinct because each $x_{i_j} \neq 0$ and the $x_i$ are distinct. By the uniqueness half of *Lagrange interpolation* there is **exactly one** polynomial of degree at most $k-1$ satisfying all $k$ of them. Hence

$$\Pr\bigl[S_A = y \ \big|\ s = s'\bigr] = \frac{\#\{p \in \mathcal{P}_{s'} : p(x_{i_j}) = y_j\ \forall j\}}{|\mathcal{P}_{s'}|} = \frac{1}{q^{\,k-1}}.$$

The right-hand side does not mention $s'$. So the conditional law of $S_A$ is uniform on $\mathbb{F}_q^{\,k-1}$ for every secret, and criterion (3) of *Three faces of perfect privacy* gives $I(s;S_A) = 0$.
:::

Read the proof again with an eye on where the hypotheses were spent. Uniqueness of interpolation gave privacy; existence gave correctness. The same theorem does both jobs, from opposite ends. And the count $1/q^{k-1}$ shows more than independence: the adversary's view is *uniform*, so a simulator can produce it by drawing $k-1$ random field elements — the cleanest possible form of the simulation paradigm.

:::example[A $(3,5)$ sharing over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$]
Share $s = 7$ with $k=3$, $n=5$, using $p(x) = 7 + 3x + 12x^2$ over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$, and reconstruct from participants $1, 2, 3$.
:::

:::solution
Shares, all arithmetic mod $17$:

$p(1) = 7+3+12 = 22 = 5$, $p(2) = 7+6+48 = 61 = 61-51 = 10$, $p(3) = 7+9+108 = 124 = 124-119 = 5$, $p(4) = 7+12+192 = 211 = 211-204 = 7$, $p(5) = 7+15+300 = 322 = 322-322 = 0$.

So the shares are $(1,5), (2,10), (3,5), (4,7), (5,0)$.

Recombination from $\{1,2,3\}$: the coefficients are
$$\lambda_1 = \tfrac{(0-2)(0-3)}{(1-2)(1-3)} = \tfrac{6}{2} = 3,\quad \lambda_2 = \tfrac{(0-1)(0-3)}{(2-1)(2-3)} = \tfrac{3}{-1} = -3 \equiv 14,\quad \lambda_3 = \tfrac{(0-1)(0-2)}{(3-1)(3-2)} = \tfrac{2}{2} = 1.$$

Then $s = 3\cdot 5 + 14 \cdot 10 + 1 \cdot 5 = 15 + 140 + 5 = 160 = 9\cdot 17 + 7 \equiv 7$. ✓

**Sanity check with a disjoint triple $\{2,4,5\}$.** Here $\lambda_2 = \frac{(-4)(-5)}{(-2)(-3)} = \frac{20}{6} \equiv \frac{3}{6}$, and $6^{-1} = 3$ because $6\cdot 3 = 18 \equiv 1$, so $\lambda_2 = 9$. Next $\lambda_4 = \frac{(-2)(-5)}{(2)(-1)} = \frac{10}{-2} = -5 \equiv 12$, and $\lambda_5 = \frac{(-2)(-4)}{(3)(1)} = \frac{8}{3} = 8\cdot 6 = 48 \equiv 14$. These sum to $9+12+14 = 35 \equiv 1$, as recombination coefficients must, since applied to the constant polynomial $1$ they must return $1$.

Reconstructing: $9\cdot 10 + 12\cdot 7 + 14\cdot 16 = 90 + 84 + 224 = 398 = 23\cdot 17 + 7 \equiv 7$ ✓ — the same secret from a completely different triple, as *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly* guarantees they must. □
:::

:::pitfall
The evaluation points $x_i$ are **public** labels; the shares are the values $p(x_i)$. Publishing which participant holds which index costs nothing, but publishing a value destroys one degree of protection. Likewise, never reuse the same polynomial to share a second secret, and never give two participants the same $x_i$ — two equal indices are one share, not two.
:::

The last property of the scheme is the one the MPC half consumes, and it is worth isolating.

:::lemma[Shamir sharing is linear]{#shamir-linear}
Suppose $p$ and $\tilde p$ are degree-$\le d$ polynomials sharing $a$ and $b$ at the same evaluation points, and let $c \in \mathbb{F}_q$. Then $p + \tilde p$ is a degree-$\le d$ sharing of $a+b$ and $c\,p$ is a degree-$\le d$ sharing of $ca$, and each participant computes its new share **locally**, as $S_i + \tilde S_i$ and $c\,S_i$.
:::

:::proof
Sums and scalar multiples of polynomials of degree at most $d$ have degree at most $d$, and evaluation is linear: $(p+\tilde p)(x_i) = p(x_i) + \tilde p(x_i)$ and $(cp)(x_i) = c\,p(x_i)$. The constant terms behave the same way: $(p+\tilde p)(0) = a + b$, $(cp)(0) = ca$.
:::

## Share Size, Verifiability, and Extensions

<!-- section: properties-extensions -->

Perfection has a price, and it is paid in share length. The bound below is information-theoretic — no cleverness escapes it — and it explains why every deployed information-theoretic scheme stores as many bits per participant as the secret itself.

:::theorem[Share size lower bound]{#share-size-bound}
In any perfect scheme for an access structure $\Gamma$, let $A \notin \Gamma$ and $P_j \notin A$ be such that $A \cup \{P_j\} \in \Gamma$. Then

$$H(S_j) \ \ge\ H(S_j \mid S_A) \ \ge\ H(s).$$

In particular, in a perfect $(k,n)$-threshold scheme every share has entropy at least $H(s)$: shares are never shorter than the secret.
:::

:::proof
Perfect privacy at $A$ gives $H(s \mid S_A) = H(s)$; correctness at $A \cup \{P_j\}$ gives $H(s \mid S_A, S_j) = 0$. Subtract:

$$I(s\,;\,S_j \mid S_A) = H(s \mid S_A) - H(s \mid S_A, S_j) = H(s) - 0 = H(s).$$

Conditional mutual information never exceeds the conditional entropy of either argument, so $H(S_j \mid S_A) \ge I(s;S_j \mid S_A) = H(s)$. Finally conditioning cannot increase entropy, so $H(S_j) \ge H(S_j\mid S_A)$.

For $\Gamma_{k,n}$, every participant $P_j$ lies outside some set $A$ of size $k-1$ whose union with $P_j$ has size $k$ and is therefore qualified, so the bound applies to every share.
:::

:::corollary[Shamir is size-optimal]{#shamir-optimal}
For a uniform secret in $\mathbb{F}_q$, Shamir's scheme meets the bound with equality: $H(S_i) = H(s) = \log_2 q$ bits.
:::

:::proof
By the privacy half of *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly* with $k-1 \ge 1$, each single share is uniform on $\mathbb{F}_q$ (take $A = \{i\}$ inside any unqualified set), so $H(S_i) = \log_2 q$, which is $H(s)$.
:::

:::intuition
Why can a share not be shorter than the secret? Stand in the shoes of a coalition one member short. They know nothing — every secret is still equally likely. The moment the last member arrives, they know everything. So that one arriving share had to carry the entire remaining uncertainty by itself, and a message cannot carry more information than its own length.
:::

:::remark
The bound is about *perfect* schemes. Two escapes exist. **Ramp schemes** weaken privacy: an $(\ell, k, n)$ ramp scheme reconstructs from $k$ shares, leaks nothing below $\ell$, and leaks partially in between, which lets shares be a factor $k - \ell$ smaller than the secret. **Computational** schemes encrypt the secret under a random key, share the key with Shamir, and distribute pieces of the ciphertext (Krawczyk); shares shrink to roughly $|s|/k$, at the cost of resting on an assumption. Neither contradicts the theorem; each gives up one of its hypotheses.
:::

A second cost is structural: the replication scheme of *Every monotone access structure is realisable* gives shares exponential in $n$ for threshold structures, while Shamir gives one field element. The general framework explaining why is linear algebra.

:::definition[Linear secret sharing scheme (LSSS)]{#lsss}
A scheme is **linear** over $\mathbb{F}_q$ if there is a matrix $M \in \mathbb{F}_q^{d \times e}$ and an assignment of the $d$ rows to participants such that, with $\rho = (s, r_2,\dots,r_e)$ for uniform $r_i$, the share of $P_i$ is the set of coordinates of $M\rho$ belonging to $P_i$'s rows. A set $A$ is qualified exactly when the target vector $(1,0,\dots,0)$ lies in the row span of $M$ restricted to $A$'s rows.
:::

Shamir is linear with $M$ the Vandermonde matrix of rows $(1, x_i, x_i^2, \dots, x_i^{k-1})$, and the recombination coefficients $\lambda_i$ are exactly the coefficients expressing $(1,0,\dots,0)$ in that span. Linear schemes are equivalent to *monotone span programs*; whether every monotone structure has a polynomial-size linear scheme is open, and the best known general upper bound is sub-exponential rather than polynomial.

So far the dealer has been honest. Remove that assumption and correctness collapses: a corrupt dealer can hand out points that lie on no single low-degree polynomial, so that different qualified sets reconstruct different "secrets". Verifiable secret sharing fixes this.

:::definition[Verifiable secret sharing (VSS)]{#vss}
A VSS scheme adds to the dealing phase a public verification procedure such that

- **(Binding/commitment)** after dealing, there is a well-defined value $s^{*}$ such that every qualified set of honest participants reconstructs $s^{*}$ — even if the dealer is corrupt;
- **(Validity)** if the dealer is honest, $s^{*} = s$;
- **(Privacy)** if the dealer is honest, unqualified sets still learn nothing about $s$.
:::

:::recipe[Feldman VSS]{#feldman}
Work in a group $G = \langle g\rangle$ of prime order $q$ in which discrete logarithms are hard.

1. The dealer chooses $p(x) = s + a_1x + \cdots + a_{k-1}x^{k-1}$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ and sends $S_i = p(x_i)$ privately to $P_i$.
2. The dealer **broadcasts** the commitments $C_0 = g^{s}$, $C_1 = g^{a_1}$, …, $C_{k-1} = g^{a_{k-1}}$.
3. $P_i$ accepts its share if and only if

$$g^{S_i} \ =\ \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} C_j^{\,x_i^{\,j}}.$$

4. If a participant's check fails it complains publicly; the dealer must reveal that share, and a dealer who cannot is disqualified.
:::

:::proposition[The Feldman check is exactly consistency with a degree-$(k-1)$ polynomial]{#feldman-check}
Given broadcast $C_0,\dots,C_{k-1}$, a value $S_i$ passes the check at $x_i$ if and only if $S_i = p(x_i)$ for the unique polynomial $p$ whose coefficients are the discrete logarithms of the $C_j$.
:::

:::proof
Write $C_j = g^{c_j}$ (possible since $g$ generates $G$) and $p(x) = \sum_j c_j x^j$. Then

$$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} C_j^{\,x_i^{\,j}} = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} g^{\,c_j x_i^{\,j}} = g^{\sum_j c_j x_i^{\,j}} = g^{\,p(x_i)}.$$

Since $g$ has prime order $q$, the map $u \mapsto g^{u}$ is injective on $\mathbb{F}_q$, so $g^{S_i} = g^{p(x_i)}$ holds if and only if $S_i = p(x_i)$.
:::

Binding here is *unconditional*: the commitments determine $p$ as an element of $\mathbb{F}_q[x]$ of degree $< k$ whatever the dealer does, so all accepted shares lie on one polynomial and every qualified set reconstructs its constant term. Privacy, however, is only *computational* — $C_0 = g^{s}$ leaks $s$ to anyone who can take discrete logarithms, and even without that it identifies $s$ uniquely. Pedersen's commitment trades the two around.

:::definition[Pedersen commitment]{#pedersen}
With two generators $g,h$ of $G$ whose relative discrete logarithm $\log_g h$ is unknown to the committer, the commitment to $m$ with randomness $\rho$ is

$$\mathrm{Com}(m;\rho) = g^{m}h^{\rho}.$$
:::

:::theorem[Pedersen is perfectly hiding and computationally binding]{#pedersen-security}
For fixed $m$, $\mathrm{Com}(m;\rho)$ with uniform $\rho$ is uniformly distributed on $G$, so a commitment reveals nothing whatsoever about $m$. Moreover, anyone who opens one commitment in two ways can compute $\log_g h$.
:::

:::proof
*Hiding.* $h$ generates $G$ (any non-identity element of a group of prime order does), so $\rho \mapsto h^{\rho}$ is a bijection $\mathbb{F}_q \to G$; multiplying by the fixed element $g^{m}$ is again a bijection. Hence the commitment is uniform on $G$ for **every** $m$, which is criterion (3) of *Three faces of perfect privacy* once more.

*Binding.* Suppose $g^{m}h^{\rho} = g^{m'}h^{\rho'}$ with $m \neq m'$. Then $g^{\,m-m'} = h^{\,\rho'-\rho}$. Here $\rho' \neq \rho$, since otherwise $g^{m-m'}=1$ would force $m=m'$ in a group of prime order. Therefore $\log_g h = (m-m')(\rho'-\rho)^{-1} \bmod q$, computable from the two openings.
:::

Pedersen VSS runs Feldman with $\mathrm{Com}(a_j;\rho_j)$ in place of $g^{a_j}$, the dealer additionally sharing the randomness polynomial; the verification identity is the same computation carried out in two exponents at once. The resulting scheme is *perfectly private* and binding only under the discrete-logarithm assumption — the mirror image of Feldman. Which you want depends on whether the secret must stay hidden forever (Pedersen) or the sharing must stay correct against an unbounded dealer (Feldman).

:::example[Running the Feldman check with small numbers]
Let $G$ be the subgroup of order $11$ inside $\mathbb{Z}_{23}^{*}$, generated by $g = 2$, so that exponents are read modulo $11$. The dealer uses $k=2$ and $p(x) = 4 + 3x$ over $\mathbb{F}_{11}$. Verify participant $x=2$'s share.
:::

:::solution
**Commitments.** $C_0 = g^{s} = 2^{4} = 16$ and $C_1 = g^{a_1} = 2^{3} = 8$, both broadcast; all group arithmetic is mod $23$.

**Share.** $p(2) = 4 + 3\cdot 2 = 10$, sent privately to participant $2$.

**Check.** The left side is $g^{S_2} = 2^{10} = 1024 = 44\cdot 23 + 12 \equiv 12$. The right side is
$$C_0^{\,2^{0}} C_1^{\,2^{1}} = 16 \cdot 8^{2} = 16\cdot 64 \equiv 16 \cdot 18 = 288 = 12\cdot 23 + 12 \equiv 12.$$
The two agree, so the share is accepted. ✓

**A corrupt dealer.** Suppose the dealer had sent $S_2 = 9$ instead. Then $g^{9} = 2^{9} = 512 = 22\cdot 23 + 6 \equiv 6 \neq 12$, and participant $2$ complains. **Sanity check:** the check passed exactly at the value predicted by *The Feldman check is exactly consistency with a degree-$(k-1)$ polynomial*, and $g = 2$ has order $11$ because $2^{11} = 2048 = 89\cdot 23 + 1 \equiv 1$, so exponent arithmetic mod $11$ is legitimate. □
:::

:::example[Refreshing shares without changing the secret]
Shares leak over time: an adversary who breaks into one machine per month eventually collects $k$ of them. Show how the participants can replace all shares by fresh ones, without ever reconstructing $s$ and without a dealer.
:::

:::solution
Each participant $P_i$ picks a random polynomial $z_i$ of degree $\le k-1$ with **zero constant term**, $z_i(0) = 0$, and sends $z_i(x_j)$ privately to each $P_j$.

Every participant updates its share to
$$S_j' = S_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i(x_j),$$
which by *Shamir sharing is linear* is the share of $P_j$ under the polynomial $p' = p + \sum_i z_i$. This $p'$ still has degree $\le k-1$ and $p'(0) = p(0) + 0 = s$, so the secret is unchanged and any $k$ new shares still recover it.

Why it helps: old and new shares are related by a sharing of $0$ that is uniform among degree-$\le k-1$ polynomials vanishing at $0$ (as long as at least one $z_i$ came from an honest participant), so $k-1$ *old* shares combined with $k-1$ *new* shares still leave $s$ uniform. The adversary must now collect $k$ shares **within a single epoch**. **Sanity check:** setting all $z_i \equiv 0$ returns the original sharing, and evaluating $p'$ at $0$ never involves the $z_i$ at all. □
:::

## The Secure Multiparty Computation Problem

<!-- section: mpc-problem -->

Secret sharing hides data at rest. Secure multiparty computation (MPC) hides it *while it is being used*: $n$ parties, each holding a private input, want the value of a joint function and nothing else.

:::definition[Secure multiparty computation]{#mpc}
Parties $P_1,\dots,P_n$ hold private inputs $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and wish to compute

$$y = f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$$

by exchanging messages, so that

- **(Correctness)** every honest party outputs the correct $y$, and
- **(Privacy)** the parties learn nothing about the inputs beyond what $y$ and their own inputs already imply.
:::

The second clause is useless until "learn nothing beyond" is made mathematical, and the naive attempts fail. "The adversary cannot compute any $x_j$" is too weak — leaking half of $x_j$'s bits satisfies it. "The transcript is independent of the inputs" is too strong — the transcript determines $y$, which depends on the inputs. The resolution, due to Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff and refined by Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson, is comparative: measure the real protocol against an idealisation that is private by construction.

:::definition[The ideal functionality]{#ideal-world}
In the **ideal world** an incorruptible trusted party $\mathcal{F}$ receives $x_1,\dots,x_n$ over private channels, computes $y = f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, returns $y$ to everyone, and forgets. An adversary corrupting a set $C$ of parties in the ideal world sees exactly $\{x_i\}_{i \in C}$ and $y$ — by construction, nothing else exists to see.
:::

:::definition[View and simulation-based security (semi-honest)]{#simulation-paradigm}
The **view** of party $P_i$ in a real execution is

$$\mathrm{view}_i = \bigl(x_i,\ r_i,\ m_{i,1}, m_{i,2}, \dots\bigr),$$

its input, its random tape and every message it received. A protocol $\pi$ computes $f$ with **perfect security against semi-honest adversaries corrupting at most $t$ parties** if it is correct and, for every $C$ with $|C| \le t$, there is a probabilistic polynomial-time **simulator** $\mathcal{S}$ such that for all inputs

$$\mathcal{S}\bigl(C, \{x_i\}_{i\in C},\, y\bigr) \ \equiv\ \bigl\{\mathrm{view}_i\bigr\}_{i \in C},$$

the two distributions being identical. Replacing $\equiv$ by "statistically close" gives **statistical** security, and by "computationally indistinguishable" gives **computational** security.
:::

The definition is exactly the right one because it is an *if-then* about information: anything the coalition could deduce from its real view, it could equally deduce from a transcript it manufactured itself out of its own inputs and the output. Since the manufactured transcript plainly contains no extra information about the honest inputs, neither does the real one. Notice the family resemblance to the privacy proof of Shamir's scheme, where the adversary's view was uniform and so could be sampled without the secret: that proof *was* a simulator.

:::intuition
Imagine the ideal world as a sealed room with a perfectly trustworthy clerk. You slide your number under the door, the clerk announces the answer, and the papers are burnt. A real protocol has no clerk; it is a conversation. The definition asks for a forger: someone who, knowing only what the clerk would have told you, can write a fake transcript of that conversation which you could not distinguish from the real one. If such a forger exists, the conversation told you nothing the clerk did not.
:::

:::pitfall
Privacy is "beyond what $y$ reveals", and $y$ can reveal a great deal. If $f$ is the average of three salaries and two parties collude, they subtract their own inputs and recover the third exactly. That is not a protocol failure — the ideal functionality leaks it too. Choosing $f$ so that its output is safe to publish is a separate problem, addressed by differential privacy rather than by MPC.
:::

:::example[The millionaires' problem]
Alice has $x = 7$ (millions) and Bob has $y = 5$. They compute $f(x,y) = 1$ if $x > y$ and $0$ otherwise. What exactly does the security definition promise Alice?
:::

:::solution
The output is $1$. In the ideal world, Bob's entire experience consists of his input $5$ and the bit $1$. The definition requires a simulator that, given only $(5, 1)$, produces a transcript distributed identically to what Bob really sees.

Consequently Bob learns $x > 5$ — this is implied by the bit itself and is unavoidable — but he does not learn $x = 7$, nor any bound better than $x > 5$: if he could, he could apply that same inference to the simulated transcript, which was generated without $x$ at all and therefore cannot distinguish $x = 7$ from $x = 6$ or $x = 10^{9}$.

Symmetrically, Alice learns only $y < 7$. **Sanity check:** run the function with $x = 4$ instead; the output becomes $0$, Bob learns $x \le 5$, and again nothing more. The leakage is a property of $f$, not of the protocol. □
:::

Not every function admits a perfectly secure protocol, and the obstruction appears already with two parties.

:::theorem[Two-party AND has no perfectly secure protocol]{#two-party-impossibility}
There is no protocol between two parties, secure against a single semi-honest corruption with unbounded computing power, that computes $f(x,y) = x \wedge y$ for input bits $x,y$.
:::

:::proof
This is a sketch; it rests on a combinatorial analysis of protocol transcripts due to Chor and Kushilevitz. Consider the matrix of $f$ over the four input pairs. Perfect security forces the set of transcripts occurring on inputs $(x,y)$ to depend only on $y$ from Alice's side and only on $x$ from Bob's, which makes the transcript distribution a product structure; one shows that the set of input pairs producing a fixed transcript must be a combinatorial rectangle on which $f$ is constant, and that these rectangles can be merged, forcing $f$ to be decomposable into functions of $x$ alone and $y$ alone. The AND matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ admits no such decomposition. Hence the two-party case must rest on computational assumptions — which is exactly what Yao's protocol supplies, via oblivious transfer.
:::

So the subject splits along a single fault line. With an honest majority, information-theoretic security is achievable for every function, and the tool is secret sharing (the section *Information-Theoretic MPC with Secret Sharing (BGW)*). Without one — in particular for two parties — cryptographic assumptions are necessary, and the tool is garbled circuits.

## Yao's Garbled Circuits for Two Parties

<!-- section: yao-garbled -->

Yao's construction (1986) computes any function of two inputs, in the semi-honest model, with a *constant* number of rounds — the garbler sends one big message, the evaluator computes in silence. The function is presented as a Boolean circuit, and each wire is replaced by a pair of random keys.

The protocol needs one cryptographic primitive besides encryption.

:::definition[1-out-of-2 oblivious transfer]{#ot}
$\mathrm{OT}$ is the two-party functionality in which the sender inputs a pair $(m_0,m_1)$, the receiver inputs a bit $b$, and the receiver learns $m_b$ while

- the sender learns nothing about $b$, and
- the receiver learns nothing about $m_{1-b}$.
:::

OT is precisely what is needed for the evaluator to obtain the key for its own input bit: the garbler must not learn which bit was requested, and the evaluator must not obtain both keys of a wire (holding both would let it evaluate the circuit on inputs it never had). By *Two-party AND has no perfectly secure protocol*, OT cannot be built unconditionally; it is constructed from trapdoor permutations or Diffie–Hellman.

:::definition[Garbling scheme]{#garbling-scheme}
A garbling scheme for circuits is a triple of algorithms

- $\mathrm{Gb}(C) \to (\tilde C,\ \{K_w^{0},K_w^{1}\}_{w},\ \mathrm{dec})$, producing a garbled circuit, a pair of keys per wire, and an output decoding map;
- $\mathrm{Ev}(\tilde C, \{K_w^{b_w}\}_{w \in \text{inputs}}) \to \{K_w^{\,\cdot}\}_{w\in\text{outputs}}$;
- $\mathrm{De}(\mathrm{dec},\cdot)$, turning output keys into bits,

satisfying **correctness** — $\mathrm{De}(\mathrm{dec},\mathrm{Ev}(\tilde C, \text{keys for } x)) = C(x)$ — and **privacy**: there is a simulator that, given only $C$ and the output $C(x)$, produces $(\tilde C, \text{input keys}, \mathrm{dec})$ computationally indistinguishable from a real garbling.
:::

:::recipe[Yao's protocol]{#yao-garbled}
1. **Garble.** For each wire $w$ the garbler draws independent random keys $K_w^{0},K_w^{1}$. For each gate $g$ with input wires $a,b$ and output wire $c$ it builds the four ciphertexts
$$\mathrm{Enc}_{K_a^{\alpha},\,K_b^{\beta}}\bigl(K_c^{\,g(\alpha,\beta)}\bigr), \qquad \alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\},$$
and permutes them randomly.
2. **Send.** The garbler sends all garbled tables, the keys $K_w^{\,x_w}$ for its own input wires, and the output decoding map (key $\mapsto$ bit) for the output wires only.
3. **Transfer.** For each of the evaluator's input wires the parties run one OT: sender input $(K_w^{0},K_w^{1})$, receiver input its bit.
4. **Evaluate.** Holding one key per input wire, the evaluator processes gates in topological order, decrypting the one row of each table that its two keys open, obtaining one key per internal wire.
5. **Decode.** The evaluator maps the output keys to bits and sends the result to the garbler.
:::

:::intuition
Picture an encrypted maze. Every wire is a pair of keys and every gate is a padlocked box with four compartments, only one of which your two keys will open. You walk the maze holding exactly one key per wire, opening one compartment per gate, never learning whether the key in your hand means $0$ or $1$ — the two look alike. Only at the exit does the garbler hand you a legend for the final wires, and only then does anything become readable.
:::

:::theorem[Security of Yao's protocol]{#yao-security}
If the encryption scheme is semantically secure (and has the mild property that a wrong-key decryption is detectably invalid) and the OT is secure, then Yao's protocol computes $f$ with computational security against a semi-honest adversary corrupting either party.
:::

:::proof
A sketch; it rests on the semantic security of the underlying encryption and on the security of OT, neither proved here.

*Corrupt evaluator.* By induction over the circuit in topological order, the evaluator holds exactly one key per wire and cannot tell which bit it represents, because the two keys of a wire are identically distributed random strings and the decoding map is released only for output wires. The simulator, given the output bits, garbles a **fake** circuit in which all four rows of every table encrypt the *same* output key, and rigs the output decoding map so that the resulting keys decode to the true output. A distinguisher between real and fake garbling yields a distinguisher against the encryption of the three rows the evaluator never opens, one hybrid gate at a time. The evaluator's OT messages are simulated by OT's receiver-privacy.

*Corrupt garbler.* The garbler receives no messages except the OT responses and the final output, so its view is simulated from its own input and $y$ using OT's sender-privacy.
:::

:::example[Garbling a single AND gate]
Write out the garbled table for one AND gate with input wires $A,B$ and output wire $C$, and evaluate it on $a=0$, $b=1$.
:::

:::solution
The garbler draws six random $128$-bit keys $K_A^{0},K_A^{1},K_B^{0},K_B^{1},K_C^{0},K_C^{1}$ and forms, from the truth table of AND,

$$\mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^{0},K_B^{0}}(K_C^{0}),\quad \mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^{0},K_B^{1}}(K_C^{0}),\quad \mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^{1},K_B^{0}}(K_C^{0}),\quad \mathrm{Enc}_{K_A^{1},K_B^{1}}(K_C^{1}),$$

then shuffles the four rows so that position carries no information.

Evaluation with $K_A^{0}$ (the evaluator's own wire, via OT) and $K_B^{1}$ (sent by the garbler, whose bit is $1$): trying all four rows, exactly the second decrypts validly, yielding $K_C^{0}$.

What has been learned: the evaluator holds $K_C^{0}$, a random string, and never sees $K_C^{1}$, so on an internal wire it cannot tell the value is $0$. **Sanity check:** $0 \wedge 1 = 0$ and the recovered key is $K_C^{0}$ ✓; also, had the evaluator been given both $K_B^{0}$ and $K_B^{1}$ it could have opened two rows and learned the garbler's input — which is exactly why step 3 uses OT and why the garbler sends only one key per input wire of its own. □
:::

:::remark
Costs and the standard optimisations. A naive garbling sends $4$ ciphertexts per gate. **Point-and-permute** appends a random *select bit* to each key and uses the two select bits to index the row directly, removing the trial decryptions. **Garbled row reduction** fixes one ciphertext to zero and sends $3$. **Free-XOR** chooses $K_w^{1} = K_w^{0}\oplus \Delta$ for a single global $\Delta$, after which an XOR gate needs no table and no communication at all — so only AND gates cost anything, and **half-gates** bring those to $2$ ciphertexts. An AES-128 circuit has about $6{,}800$ AND gates, so a garbled AES is a few hundred kilobytes, sent in one round.
:::

:::pitfall
A garbled circuit is **single-use**. Evaluating the same $\tilde C$ on two different inputs would give the evaluator two keys on some wire, which breaks the induction in the security proof and typically reveals the garbler's input. Each execution requires fresh garbling — and fresh OTs.
:::

## Information-Theoretic MPC with Secret Sharing (BGW)

<!-- section: bgw-ss -->

Now the two halves meet. BGW (Ben-Or, Goldwasser, Wigderson 1988) evaluates an arithmetic circuit over $\mathbb{F}_q$ gate by gate while every intermediate value stays Shamir-shared, and it needs no computational assumption whatsoever.

:::notation
The secret-sharing half wrote $(k,n)$ with reconstruction threshold $k$ and polynomial degree $k-1$. MPC counts corruptions instead. Throughout this section $t$ is the number of corrupted parties, sharings use polynomials of degree at most $t$, and reconstruction therefore needs $t+1$ shares. In the earlier notation: $k = t+1$.
:::

The choice of degree is forced from both sides. Degree $t$ is high enough that any $t$ colluding parties see $t$ points of a degree-$t$ polynomial and, by *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly*, learn nothing. It must not be higher, because reconstruction must still succeed from the $n - t$ honest parties.

:::recipe[The BGW protocol, semi-honest version]{#bgw-protocol}
1. **Input sharing.** Each party Shamir-shares its input with a random polynomial of degree $\le t$ and sends one share to each party.
2. **Addition gate.** Each party adds its two shares locally. No communication.
3. **Multiply-by-constant gate.** Each party scales its share locally. No communication.
4. **Multiplication gate.** Each party multiplies its two shares locally, then the parties run the degree-reduction subprotocol below.
5. **Output.** The parties broadcast their shares of the output wire and interpolate.
:::

Steps 2 and 3 are correct and private immediately by *Shamir sharing is linear*: sums and scalar multiples of degree-$\le t$ sharings are degree-$\le t$ sharings of the corresponding values, and a party that computes only on the shares it already holds sends nothing and hence leaks nothing.

Multiplication is where the work is. Let $f$ and $g$ be the degree-$\le t$ polynomials sharing $a$ and $b$. Party $i$ can form $h(x_i) = f(x_i)g(x_i)$ locally, and the polynomial $h = fg$ does satisfy $h(0) = ab$. But $h$ is wrong as a sharing in two independent ways:

1. **Its degree is $2t$**, so $t+1$ shares no longer determine it; reconstruction would need $2t+1$ shares, and after a second multiplication the degree would be $4t$.
2. **It is not uniformly random** among polynomials with the right constant term: $h$ is a *product*, hence reducible, and its coefficients are correlated. A coalition holding $t$ points of $h$ can extract information that $t$ points of a random polynomial would not reveal.

:::intuition
The whole protocol is degree bookkeeping. A sharing is a polynomial and its degree is a budget: degree $t$ hides from $t$ people and needs $t+1$ to open. Addition spends nothing — adding two lines gives a line. Multiplication spends double, turning two lines into a parabola, and the budget must be paid back before the next gate. Degree reduction is the repayment, and it can only be afforded when there are at least $2t+1$ people in the room.
:::

The fix uses the one structural fact established in the first half: *reconstruction is a fixed linear map*. Spell it out.

:::lemma[Reconstruction of a degree-$2t$ sharing is linear]{#deg2t-linear}
Suppose $n \ge 2t+1$ and let $h$ have degree at most $2t$. With $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{2t+1}$ the recombination coefficients of *Lagrange interpolation* at $x=0$ for the points $x_1,\dots,x_{2t+1}$,

$$h(0) = \sum_{i=1}^{2t+1} \lambda_i\, h(x_i),$$

and the $\lambda_i$ are public constants, independent of $h$.
:::

:::proof
$h$ has degree at most $2t$ and $2t+1$ points determine such a polynomial uniquely, so $h(x) = \sum_i h(x_i)\ell_i(x)$ by *Lagrange interpolation*. Evaluate at $x = 0$ and put $\lambda_i = \ell_i(0)$, which depend only on the evaluation points.
:::

:::recipe[Degree reduction (GRR re-sharing)]{#degree-reduction}
Each party $i \le 2t+1$ holds $h_i = f(x_i)g(x_i)$, a share of $ab$ under a degree-$2t$ polynomial.

1. **Re-share.** Party $i$ picks a fresh random polynomial $H_i$ of degree $\le t$ with $H_i(0) = h_i$ and sends $H_i(x_j)$ to every party $j$.
2. **Recombine locally.** Party $j$ computes
$$c_j \ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{2t+1} \lambda_i\,H_i(x_j).$$
:::

:::theorem[Degree reduction is correct and private]{#degree-reduction-thm}
Let $C(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{2t+1}\lambda_i H_i(x)$. Then $\deg C \le t$ and $C(0) = ab$, so $(c_1,\dots,c_n)$ is a degree-$\le t$ Shamir sharing of $ab$. Moreover, if at most $t$ parties are corrupted, their shares $\{c_j\}$ are distributed exactly as $t$ shares of a *uniformly random* degree-$\le t$ sharing of $ab$, so they reveal nothing about $a$, $b$ or $ab$.
:::

:::proof
*Degree.* Each $H_i$ has degree at most $t$ and $C$ is a fixed linear combination of them, so $\deg C \le t$ by *Shamir sharing is linear*.

*Value.* $C(0) = \sum_i \lambda_i H_i(0) = \sum_i \lambda_i h_i = \sum_i \lambda_i h(x_i) = h(0) = ab$, the middle equality being *Reconstruction of a degree-$2t$ sharing is linear* and the last $h = fg$ with $f(0)=a$, $g(0)=b$.

*Privacy.* At least one index $i^{*} \le 2t+1$ belongs to an honest party, because there are at most $t < 2t+1$ corrupted ones. Its polynomial $H_{i^{*}}$ is uniform among degree-$\le t$ polynomials with the prescribed constant term, independent of everything else, and $\lambda_{i^{*}} \neq 0$ (a Lagrange coefficient is a product of nonzero field elements). Write $C = \lambda_{i^{*}}H_{i^{*}} + D$ with $D$ the rest. Conditioned on all the adversary's other information, the non-constant coefficients of $\lambda_{i^{*}}H_{i^{*}}$ are uniform and independent, hence so are those of $C$. Thus $C$ is a uniform degree-$\le t$ sharing of $ab$, and by the privacy half of *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly* its $t$ shares held by corrupted parties are uniform and independent of $ab$. The messages $H_i(x_j)$ received from honest $i$ are, by the same theorem, uniform field elements.
:::

Everything in that proof needed $2t+1$ distinct honest-or-not evaluation points to exist, i.e. $n \ge 2t+1$. This is the whole content of the famous threshold.

:::corollary[Honest majority: $t < n/2$]{#honest-majority}
BGW evaluates any arithmetic circuit over $\mathbb{F}_q$ with perfect security against a semi-honest adversary corrupting $t$ parties, provided $n \ge 2t+1$, i.e. $t < n/2$.
:::

:::proof
Input sharing, addition and scalar multiplication are perfectly private and correct by *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly* and *Shamir sharing is linear*; multiplication is by *Degree reduction is correct and private*, which requires $2t+1 \le n$. Every wire of the circuit therefore carries a uniform degree-$\le t$ sharing of its true value, and the adversary's view at every step consists of uniform field elements, which a simulator can sample without any input. Composing gate by gate, the whole view is simulatable from the corrupted inputs and the output.
:::

:::example[Multiplying two shared values over $\mathbb{F}_{17}$]
Take $n=3$, $t=1$, $x_i = i$. Let $a = 4$ be shared by $f(x) = 4+5x$ and $b = 9$ by $g(x) = 9+2x$. Carry out one multiplication gate in full.
:::

:::solution
**Shares held.** $f(1)=9$, $f(2)=14$, $f(3)=19\equiv 2$; $g(1)=11$, $g(2)=13$, $g(3)=15$.

**Local products.** $h_1 = 9\cdot 11 = 99 \equiv 14$, $h_2 = 14\cdot 13 = 182 \equiv 12$, $h_3 = 2\cdot 15 = 30 \equiv 13$. These lie on $h = fg = 36 + 53x + 10x^{2} \equiv 2 + 2x + 10x^{2}$, a degree-$2$ polynomial with $h(0) = 2 \equiv 36 = 4\cdot 9$ ✓.

**Recombination coefficients** for points $1,2,3$ (computed in the Shamir section): $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 14$, $\lambda_3 = 1$. Check: $3\cdot 14 + 14\cdot 12 + 1\cdot 13 = 42+168+13 = 223 = 13\cdot 17 + 2 \equiv 2$ ✓ — the degree-$2$ sharing does reconstruct $ab$, but it took all three shares.

**Re-sharing.** Each party picks a random degree-$1$ polynomial with its $h_i$ as constant term:
$H_1(x) = 14 + 3x$, $H_2(x) = 12 + 7x$, $H_3(x) = 13 + x$, and sends the evaluations
$$H_1: (0,\,3,\,6), \qquad H_2: (2,\,9,\,16), \qquad H_3: (14,\,15,\,16)$$
at $x=1,2,3$ respectively (all mod $17$; e.g. $H_1(1)=17\equiv 0$, $H_2(3)=33\equiv 16$).

**Local recombination.** $c_j = 3H_1(j) + 14H_2(j) + H_3(j)$:

$c_1 = 3\cdot 0 + 14\cdot 2 + 14 = 42 \equiv 8$,
$c_2 = 3\cdot 3 + 14\cdot 9 + 15 = 9+126+15 = 150 \equiv 14$,
$c_3 = 3\cdot 6 + 14\cdot 16 + 16 = 18+224+16 = 258 \equiv 3$.

**Verification.** Interpolate $c_1=8$, $c_2=14$ at $0$: coefficients $\ell_1(0)=\frac{-2}{-1}=2$ and $\ell_2(0)=\frac{-1}{1}=-1\equiv 16$, giving $2\cdot 8 + 16\cdot 14 = 16+224 = 240 = 14\cdot 17 + 2 \equiv 2 = ab$ ✓. **Sanity check:** the new sharing is degree $1$, since $C(x) = 2 + 6x$ fits all three: $C(1)=8$, $C(2)=14$, $C(3)=20\equiv 3$ ✓ — so any **two** parties now suffice, and a further multiplication can be performed. □
:::

Against a *malicious* adversary the threshold tightens, for a reason that is pure coding theory.

:::proposition[Malicious BGW needs $t < n/3$ without broadcast]{#third-threshold}
The shares $(p(x_1),\dots,p(x_n))$ of a degree-$\le t$ polynomial form a Reed–Solomon code of length $n$ and dimension $t+1$, whose minimum distance is $n-t$. Correcting $t$ arbitrary corruptions requires $n - t \ge 2t+1$, i.e. $n \ge 3t+1$.
:::

:::proof
Two distinct degree-$\le t$ polynomials agree in at most $t$ places (their difference is a nonzero polynomial of degree $\le t$), so two distinct codewords differ in at least $n - t$ positions; and the bound is met by a polynomial with $t$ roots among the $x_i$. A code corrects $e$ errors exactly when its minimum distance exceeds $2e$, since otherwise two codewords have a common word within distance $e$ and decoding is ambiguous. With $e = t$ this reads $n - t \ge 2t+1$.
:::

So with $n \ge 3t+1$ the honest parties can *decode through* the lies: a set of shares from which $t$ are arbitrarily wrong still determines the right polynomial, by Berlekamp–Welch. This is what makes the malicious version of BGW work with perfect security and guaranteed output delivery. If a broadcast channel is assumed instead, verifiable secret sharing plus zero-knowledge checks push the bound back to $t < n/2$ at the cost of a negligible error probability (Rabin–Ben-Or); it is the *combination* of no broadcast and no error that costs the factor three.

## Security Models and Feasibility

<!-- section: security-models -->

The results above hold in specific adversary models, and comparing protocols means fixing the model first. Four axes matter.

:::definition[Adversary models]{#adversary-models}
- **Behaviour.** A **semi-honest** (honest-but-curious, passive) adversary follows the protocol exactly and tries to infer extra information from its view. A **malicious** (Byzantine, active) adversary deviates arbitrarily: wrong messages, wrong inputs, early aborts.
- **Corruption schedule.** A **static** adversary fixes the corrupted set before the protocol starts; an **adaptive** one may corrupt further parties during the execution, based on what it has seen.
- **Power.** **Computationally bounded** (polynomial-time) or **unbounded**. Unbounded adversaries force information-theoretic protocols.
- **Threshold.** The maximum number $t$ of simultaneously corrupted parties, or more generally an *adversary structure* of tolerable corruption sets.
:::

For malicious adversaries the semi-honest definition needs amendment, because a corrupt party may simply lie about its input, and no definition can prevent that — the ideal functionality accepts whatever input it is given. Security therefore compares the two worlds as *whole executions*.

:::definition[Real/ideal security against malicious adversaries]{#real-ideal}
Let $\mathrm{REAL}_{\pi,\mathcal{A}}(\vec{x})$ be the joint distribution of the adversary's output and the honest parties' outputs in an execution of $\pi$, and let $\mathrm{IDEAL}_{f,\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x})$ be the same for a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ interacting with the ideal functionality (which $\mathcal{S}$ may feed substituted inputs for the corrupted parties). The protocol $\pi$ **securely computes** $f$ if for every efficient adversary $\mathcal{A}$ there is a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ with

$$\mathrm{IDEAL}_{f,\mathcal{S}}(\vec{x}) \ \approx\ \mathrm{REAL}_{\pi,\mathcal{A}}(\vec{x}) \qquad \text{for all } \vec{x},$$

where $\approx$ is equality, statistical closeness, or computational indistinguishability according to the model.
:::

Three properties are bundled into that single line, and it is worth naming them. *Privacy*: the simulator works from the ideal view only. *Correctness*: the honest parties' outputs in the ideal world are by definition $f$ of the substituted inputs, so the real protocol cannot make them output anything else. *Input independence*: the simulator must choose the substituted inputs before seeing the output, which rules out a corrupt party making its input depend on an honest one.

:::remark
**Security with abort.** With a dishonest majority one weakens the ideal world: the adversary receives $y$ first and may then tell the functionality whether to deliver $y$ to the honest parties or the symbol $\bot$. This is not a blemish to be polished away — the next theorem says it is unavoidable.
:::

:::intuition
Why is fairness the first casualty of a dishonest majority? Whoever speaks last in a protocol sees the answer a moment before everyone else, and nothing but an honest majority can force them to pass it on. With a majority of honest parties the others can finish the computation without the deserter; without one, the deserter holds the only remaining copy. That single asymmetry is what Cleve's theorem turns into a quantitative bound.
:::

:::theorem[Cleve: fair coin-tossing is impossible with a dishonest majority]{#cleve}
There is no two-party protocol for tossing an unbiased coin that is secure against a malicious party who may abort: for every $r$-round protocol some party has a strategy biasing the honest party's output by $\Omega(1/r)$.
:::

:::proof
A sketch of Cleve's argument. Let $b_i$ be the value the honest party would output if the other aborted after round $i$; $b_0$ is a fixed default and $b_r$ is the true output, so the expectations run from a constant to $1/2$. Along the sequence $\mathbb{E}[b_0],\dots,\mathbb{E}[b_r]$ some consecutive pair differs by at least $1/(2r)$ in absolute value, and the party who moves at that round can, by choosing between aborting and continuing, force the output towards whichever side it prefers. Hence perfect fairness is unattainable, and with it guaranteed output delivery.
:::

:::corollary[The feasibility map]{#feasibility-map}
- $n = 2$, semi-honest or malicious, dishonest majority: possible **with computational assumptions** (Yao + OT; GMW), **with abort**. Not possible information-theoretically, by *Two-party AND has no perfectly secure protocol*.
- $t < n/2$, semi-honest, unbounded adversary: **perfect** security for every function, by *Honest majority: $t < n/2$*.
- $t < n/3$, malicious, unbounded, no broadcast: **perfect** security with guaranteed output delivery (BGW), by *Malicious BGW needs $t < n/3$ without broadcast*.
- $t < n/2$, malicious, unbounded, with broadcast: **statistical** security with guaranteed output delivery (Rabin–Ben-Or), using VSS.
- $t \ge n/2$, malicious: computational assumptions and security with abort (GMW, SPDZ); fairness impossible by *Cleve: fair coin-tossing is impossible with a dishonest majority*.
:::

The route from the weakest model to the strongest is a compiler rather than a new protocol.

:::example[The GMW compiler]
How does one turn a protocol secure against semi-honest adversaries into one secure against malicious adversaries?
:::

:::solution
**Commit.** Each party commits to its input $x_i$ and to its random tape $r_i$ at the outset, so neither can be changed later. The random tape is generated jointly (a coin-tossing-into-the-well subprotocol) so that a corrupt party cannot choose biased randomness.

**Observe that messages are deterministic.** In any fixed protocol the next message is a public function $m = \mathrm{NextMsg}(x_i, r_i, \text{transcript so far})$ of committed data and public data.

**Prove each message.** Along with $m$, the party sends a zero-knowledge proof of the statement "there exist $x_i,r_i$ opening my commitments for which $m = \mathrm{NextMsg}(x_i,r_i,\text{transcript})$". The proof reveals nothing beyond the truth of that statement.

**Effect.** Any deviation produces a false statement and is rejected, so a malicious party is restricted to semi-honest behaviour with a possibly substituted input — precisely what the ideal world already permits. **Sanity check:** privacy is preserved because zero-knowledge proofs are simulatable, and the compiled protocol's simulator runs the semi-honest simulator plus the ZK simulators. The cost is the price of malicious security: a zero-knowledge proof per message, which is why practical protocols instead use cut-and-choose or information-theoretic MACs (SPDZ). □
:::

:::remark
**Preprocessing.** Modern protocols split into a slow, input-independent offline phase and a fast online one. Beaver triples are the canonical example: shared random $(\langle u\rangle,\langle v\rangle,\langle w\rangle)$ with $w = uv$, prepared in advance. To multiply shared $a$ and $b$ online, the parties open $d = a - u$ and $e = b - v$ — safe, because $u,v$ are uniform and independent one-time pads — and compute

$$\langle ab\rangle = \langle w\rangle + d\langle v\rangle + e\langle u\rangle + de,$$

using only the linear operations of *Shamir sharing is linear*. The online phase is then two openings per multiplication, with no cryptography at all.
:::

:::remark
**Composition.** A protocol proved secure standalone need not stay secure when many copies run concurrently inside a larger system. The **universal composability** framework quantifies over an *environment* that interacts with the protocol and the adversary throughout, and its composition theorem states that a UC-secure subprotocol may be substituted for its ideal functionality anywhere without loss. UC security is strictly stronger and, for most functionalities, unachievable without setup assumptions such as a common reference string.
:::

:::pitfall
Two thresholds are easy to confuse. The **secret-sharing** threshold $k$ says how many shares reconstruct; the **corruption** threshold $t$ says how many parties the adversary controls. BGW ties them by $k = t+1$, so a statement like "$3$-out-of-$5$ sharing tolerates $3$ corruptions" is doubly wrong: it tolerates $2$, and $2 < 5/2$ is exactly why the protocol works.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading the evaluation points as the secret data.** In Shamir's scheme the $x_i$ are public identifiers; the secrets are the values $p(x_i)$. Publishing an index is harmless, publishing a value is not.
- **Believing $k-1$ shares give "partial" information.** In a perfect scheme they give exactly zero: by *Shamir's scheme realises $\Gamma_{k,n}$ perfectly* the posterior on $s$ equals the prior, however much computing power you have.
- **Working over $\mathbb{Z}_m$ for composite $m$.** Interpolation divides by $x_i - x_j$, which need not be invertible outside a field. $\mathbb{Z}_6$ is not a field, and the scheme built over it is not a threshold scheme.
- **Sharing a long secret in a small field.** Shamir shares one field element. A long secret must be split into blocks shared with *independent* polynomials, or a larger field used; reusing one polynomial across blocks correlates them.
- **Trusting the dealer.** A corrupt dealer can distribute inconsistent points so that different qualified sets reconstruct different values. That is what verifiable secret sharing exists to prevent, and Feldman's check is the cheapest fix.
- **Equating computational and information-theoretic sharing.** Computational schemes achieve shares shorter than the secret, which *Share size lower bound* forbids in the perfect setting; they do so by assuming an adversary that cannot break an encryption.
- **Assuming MPC needs a trusted party.** The trusted party is a *definitional device*: the ideal world exists to say what security means. The protocol's entire purpose is to emulate it with no such party present.
- **Believing the output leaks nothing.** MPC protects inputs only beyond what $y$ implies. If $y$ is the sum, the sum becomes public — and $n-1$ colluding parties recover the last input by subtraction.
- **Expecting information-theoretic security with a dishonest majority.** It is impossible already for the AND of two bits. Dishonest-majority MPC is necessarily computational and necessarily allows abort.
- **Confusing Yao with BGW.** Yao is two-party, constant-round, computational, and rests on OT; BGW is $n$-party, round complexity proportional to circuit *depth*, information-theoretic, and rests on an honest majority.
- **Forgetting the degree blow-up.** Multiplying shares locally is correct but doubles the degree and destroys uniformity. Without the re-sharing step the second multiplication of a circuit is unreconstructable.
- **Underestimating the cost of malicious security.** Semi-honest protocols are typically linear in circuit size; malicious security adds zero-knowledge proofs, cut-and-choose, or authenticated triples — practical today, but never free.
