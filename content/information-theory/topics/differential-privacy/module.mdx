# Differential Privacy

A guarantee stated as a divergence bound, the mechanisms that meet it, and what composition costs across repeated queries.

## The Definition of Differential Privacy

<!-- section: dp-definition -->

:::definition[Neighboring datasets]{#neighboring-datasets}
Two datasets are neighboring if they differ by the addition, removal, or change of a single individual's record.
:::

:::definition[ε-differential privacy]{#differential-privacy}
A randomized algorithm $M$ is $\varepsilon$-differentially private if, for any two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$ and any measurable set $S$ of possible outputs,

$$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[M(D') \in S].$$
:::

The definition is closed under post-processing: if $M$ is DP then any (possibly randomized) function of $M$'s output is also DP with the same $\varepsilon$.

:::definition[Approximate differential privacy]{#approximate-dp}
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP relaxes pure DP to allow a small probability $\delta$ of 'catastrophic' failure: the bound above may be violated with probability at most $\delta$.
:::

This relaxation is necessary for many mechanisms — Gaussian noise, in particular — and for composition over many queries.

Group privacy follows from the definition by iterating it: if $M$ is $\varepsilon$-DP then changing $k$ records changes probabilities by at most $e^{k\varepsilon}$, or $(k\varepsilon,\delta')$ in the approximate case.

:::intuition
Whatever the output distribution looks like when your data is in the dataset, it cannot look too different (multiplicatively e^ε) from the distribution when your data is removed or changed.

The guarantee holds even if the adversary knows everything else about the world and sees the exact output; your participation still cannot be reliably detected.
:::

:::example
A database of medical records; a DP release of "how many patients have disease X."
:::

:::solution
**Neighboring datasets.** $D$ has $1000$ patients, of whom $200$ have disease X. Remove one patient who has X to get neighbor $D'$: now the true count is $199$. Neighboring means they differ by exactly one record.
**The requirement.** For the release $M$ to be $\varepsilon$-DP, for *every* output set $S$, $\Pr[M(D)\in S]\le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D')\in S]$ and vice-versa. So no observed output can be much more likely under "patient included" than under "patient removed."
**Numbers with $\varepsilon=\ln 3\approx1.099$.** Then $e^{\varepsilon}=3$. Whatever the chance the mechanism outputs some value, say $205$, it may be at most $3\times$ as likely when the patient is present as when absent. An adversary who sees $205$ therefore cannot update its belief about that patient's participation by more than a factor of $3$.
**Why noise is needed.** The true counts differ by only $1$, but a deterministic release would output $200$ vs $199$ — perfectly distinguishing $D$ from $D'$ (ratio $\infty$). The mechanism must add randomness to smear the outputs together. **Sanity check:** as $\varepsilon\to0$, $e^{\varepsilon}\to1$, forcing the two output distributions to coincide — maximum privacy, and by post-processing, any function of the release inherits the same $\varepsilon$.
:::

## The Laplace and Gaussian Mechanisms

<!-- section: mechanisms -->

:::definition[Sensitivity]{#sensitivity}
The $L_1$ sensitivity of a numeric query $f$ is

$$\Delta = \max_{D, D' \text{ neighbors}} |f(D)-f(D')|,$$

the most that one record can move the answer.
:::

:::theorem[Laplace mechanism]{#laplace-mechanism}
The mechanism that outputs

$$f(D) + \mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon),$$

adding noise from the Laplace distribution with scale $\Delta/\varepsilon$, is $\varepsilon$-DP.
:::

:::theorem[Gaussian mechanism]{#gaussian-mechanism}
For $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP, adding $N(0, \sigma^2)$ noise with

$$\sigma \ge \frac{\Delta \sqrt{2 \ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}$$

(or similar calibrated constants) suffices.
:::

Gaussian noise is often preferred in practice because of its tail behavior and compatibility with composition.

For non-numeric outputs (histograms, synthetic data, ML models) one uses the exponential mechanism (sample from a distribution weighted by a utility score) or more sophisticated constructions (private SGD, PATE, etc.).

:::intuition
Compute the statistic you care about on the real data. Because one person can affect the statistic by at most Δ, add random noise whose 'width' is proportional to Δ/ε. The noise hides whether any single person was present.

Laplace noise is 'double-exponential' and gives pure ε-DP; Gaussian is bell-shaped and gives the slightly weaker (ε,δ) guarantee but is easier to analyze with many queries.
:::

:::example[(Laplace):]
Counting query with sensitivity $\Delta=1$ (adding/removing one person changes the count by at most $1$), target $\varepsilon=0.5$.
:::

:::solution
**Calibrate the noise.** Laplace scale $b=\Delta/\varepsilon=1/0.5=2$. The mechanism releases $M(D)=\text{count}(D)+\text{Lap}(2)$, whose density at output $z$ is $\propto \exp(-|z-\text{count}(D)|/b)=\exp(-|z-\text{count}(D)|/2)$.
**Check the DP ratio.** Take neighbors with counts $c$ and $c'$ where $|c-c'|\le\Delta=1$. For any output $z$ the density ratio is
$$\frac{\exp(-|z-c|/2)}{\exp(-|z-c'|/2)}=\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{|z-c'|-|z-c|}{2}\Big)\le \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{|c-c'|}{2}\Big)\le \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big)=e^{0.5}=e^{\varepsilon}.$$
   The middle step uses the reverse triangle inequality $|z-c'|-|z-c|\le|c-c'|$.
**Sanity check.** The bound is exactly $e^{\varepsilon}$ and it holds for *every* $z$, so the mechanism is $\varepsilon$-DP with $\varepsilon=0.5$ ✓. Smaller $\varepsilon$ would force a larger scale $b=1/\varepsilon$, i.e. more noise.
:::

:::example[(Gaussian):]
Suppose the query is a vector-valued statistic with $L_2$ sensitivity $\Delta=4$, and we want $(\varepsilon=1,\delta=10^{-5})$-DP via the Gaussian mechanism using the rule of thumb $\sigma\ge \Delta\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon$.
:::

:::solution
$1.25/\delta=1.25/10^{-5}=1.25\times10^{5}$, and $\ln(1.25\times10^{5})\approx11.74$.
$\sqrt{2\cdot11.74}=\sqrt{23.48}\approx4.85$.
$\sigma\ge 4\cdot4.85/1\approx19.4$. So adding $N(0,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma\approx19.4$ per coordinate suffices. **Sanity check:** halving the budget to $\varepsilon=0.5$ would double $\sigma$ to $\approx38.8$ — tighter privacy costs proportionally more noise.
:::

## Composition, Post-Processing, and Advanced Properties

<!-- section: composition-properties -->

:::theorem[Basic composition]{#basic-composition}
$k$ mechanisms, each $\varepsilon$-DP, are together $k\varepsilon$-DP — and $(k\varepsilon, k\delta)$-DP in the approximate case.
:::

:::theorem[Advanced composition]{#advanced-composition}
For $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanisms, $k$-fold adaptive composition is roughly $(\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')$-DP with

$$\varepsilon' \approx \sqrt{2k \ln(1/\delta')}\;\varepsilon + k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon}-1),$$

or tighter under modern bounds.
:::

Advanced composition is the source of the 'privacy budget' accounting used in practice: the $\sqrt{k}$ in place of $k$ is what makes hundreds of queries affordable.

:::proposition[Post-processing immunity]{#post-processing}
Any function, even a randomized one, of a DP output remains DP with the same parameters.
:::

This lets you post-process noisy answers arbitrarily — take medians, train models on synthetic data — without spending more privacy.

Other properties: parallel composition (queries on disjoint subsets cost only the max ε), privacy amplification by sampling, and privacy odometers / filters for fully adaptive composition.

:::intuition
Every time you release a DP statistic you 'spend' some of your privacy budget ε. After many releases the total spent budget is tracked by composition theorems; you stop when the budget is exhausted.

Once something is DP, you can compute anything you like from it later (graphs, ML models, press releases) and the privacy guarantee still holds; you do not pay extra.
:::

:::example[(basic composition):]
Release $k=3$ statistics, each made $\varepsilon_0=0.2$-DP.
:::

:::solution
By basic composition the combined release is $k\varepsilon_0=3\cdot0.2=0.6$-DP.
**Why:** for neighbors $D,D'$ and outputs $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$, the joint density ratio factors, and each factor is bounded by $e^{\varepsilon_0}$, so the product is at most $e^{3\varepsilon_0}=e^{0.6}$. **Sanity check:** $3\cdot0.2=0.6\le$ the analyst's budget if the budget is, say, $1$.
:::

:::example[(advanced composition):]
An analyst wants $k=100$ counting queries (each sensitivity $1$) within total budget $(\varepsilon=1,\delta=10^{-6})$; pick $\delta'=10^{-6}$ for the composition slack.
:::

:::solution
**Basic composition is too lossy:** it would force each query to be $\varepsilon/k=1/100=0.01$-DP — very noisy per query.
**Advanced composition** gives roughly $\varepsilon\approx\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon_0 + k\varepsilon_0(e^{\varepsilon_0}-1)$. For small $\varepsilon_0$ the first term dominates, so set $\varepsilon_0\approx \dfrac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}}$.
**Plug in:** $\ln(1/\delta')=\ln(10^{6})\approx13.8$, so $\sqrt{2\cdot100\cdot13.8}=\sqrt{2760}\approx52.5$. Then $\varepsilon_0\approx 1/52.5\approx0.019$.
**Sanity check:** this per-query $\varepsilon_0\approx0.019$ is nearly $2\times$ larger than the $0.01$ that basic composition allowed, so each query can use about half the noise — the $\sqrt{k}$ savings of advanced composition. The exact calibration is done by a privacy accountant (e.g. Rényi-DP), which is tighter still.
:::

## Applications and Limitations

<!-- section: applications-limitations -->

DP is used for:
- Releasing aggregate statistics, contingency tables, synthetic data.
- Training ML models (DP-SGD adds noise to gradients; the privacy cost depends on the noise multiplier, batch size, and number of epochs).
- Apple's 'differential privacy' deployment for emoji and Safari usage (local DP, each device adds noise before sending).
- The 2020 U.S. Census (Disclosure Avoidance System based on the TopDown algorithm, a form of DP).

Limitations: the utility/privacy tradeoff can be severe for small datasets or many queries; choosing ε is a policy question (typical deployed values 0.1–10); composition can exhaust the budget quickly; DP does not protect against all inference (it protects participation, not the fact that someone is in a certain group if group membership is already known).

:::intuition
DP gives a dial you can turn: more noise → stronger privacy but less accurate answers. For big datasets and few queries the dial can be set so that the noise is tiny relative to the signal.

In machine learning, DP-SGD makes models slightly less accurate but gives a mathematical guarantee that the model weights do not memorize any single training example too strongly.
:::

:::example[(DP-SGD):]
Train a neural net privately. Concrete setup: dataset size $N=50{,}000$, batch size $B=500$, noise multiplier $\sigma=1.1$, clipping norm $C=1.0$, and $E=30$ epochs.
:::

:::solution
**Per-step mechanism.** Compute each example's gradient $g_i$, clip it to $L_2$ norm $C$ via $\bar g_i=g_i/\max(1,\lVert g_i\rVert_2/C)$ (bounding sensitivity to $C$), sum over the batch, add Gaussian noise $N(0,(\sigma C)^2 I)=N(0,1.21\,I)$, then divide by $B$ and take the SGD step.
**Sampling rate.** $q=B/N=500/50{,}000=0.01$. Poisson subsampling amplifies privacy: each step is much more private than $\sigma$ alone would suggest.
**Number of steps.** Steps per epoch $=N/B=50{,}000/500=100$, so total $T=E\cdot(N/B)=30\cdot100=3000$ steps.
**Accounting.** A Rényi-DP / moments accountant composes these $T=3000$ subsampled Gaussian steps and outputs a single $(\varepsilon,\delta)$. For these parameters one typically lands near $\varepsilon\approx2$–$3$ at $\delta=10^{-5}$. **Sanity check:** raising $\sigma$ or lowering $q$ or $T$ all *decrease* $\varepsilon$ (stronger privacy) at the cost of accuracy — the levers behave monotonically, as expected. Because the trained weights are a post-processing of the noisy gradients, the final model inherits the same $(\varepsilon,\delta)$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking that in Shamir's scheme the polynomial is evaluated at the participant identities only**: The x-coordinates are public identifiers; the y-values (or the pair) are the actual shares that must be kept secret.
- **Believing that k-1 shares give 'some' partial information about s**: In a perfect scheme they give zero information; the posterior on s is identical to the prior (uniform).
- **Using a small field for a large secret**: Shamir is usually applied to field elements; for a long secret one either uses a large field or shares the secret bit-by-bit / block-by-block with independent polynomials.
- **Forgetting that the dealer must be trusted to choose the polynomial correctly**: If the dealer is malicious he can give inconsistent shares. Verifiable secret sharing solves this.
- **Assuming computational secret sharing (e.g., using one-way functions) is the same as information-theoretic**: Computational schemes can have shorter shares but rely on hardness assumptions and may be broken by future algorithms or quantum computers.
- **Confusing secret sharing with threshold cryptography or multiparty computation**: Secret sharing is the distribution step; threshold crypto uses shares of a key to perform operations without reconstructing the key in the clear; MPC is the broader interactive protocol setting.

Secure multiparty computation (MPC, or SMPC) lets a set of parties, each holding a private input, compute a joint function of all inputs while revealing only the output (and whatever is implied by it). No party learns anything else about the other parties' inputs.

MPC is the broadest primitive in cryptography that generalizes secret sharing, zero-knowledge, and private set intersection. It can realize any 'mental poker' or 'millionaires' problem in a provably secure way.

- **Thinking MPC always requires a trusted third party**: The whole point is to remove the trusted party; the protocol itself emulates the ideal functionality.
- **Assuming that 'the output reveals nothing'**: By definition the output y is revealed; privacy only protects the inputs beyond what is implied by y (e.g., if f is the sum, the sum itself is learned but not the individual addends).
- **Believing information-theoretic security is always possible**: It requires an honest majority (or a private channel + honest majority). Dishonest majority needs computational assumptions.
- **Confusing 2-party Yao with multi-party secret-sharing MPC**: Yao is usually computational (via OT) and for two parties; BGW-style is information-theoretic for honest majority and uses Shamir sharing + interaction for multiplication.
- **Forgetting the cost of malicious security**: Semi-honest protocols can be very cheap (linear in circuit size); malicious security often adds heavy zero-knowledge or cut-and-choose overhead, although recent protocols (e.g., SPDZ, authenticated triples) have made malicious security practical.
- **Assuming MPC is only for 'millionaires' or toy functions**: Modern MPC frameworks (MP-SPDZ, SCALE-MAMBA, EMP-toolkit, etc.) routinely evaluate AES, neural-network inference, and complex database queries among dozens of parties.

Differential privacy (DP) is a rigorous, quantifiable definition of privacy for data analysis and machine learning. It asks that a randomized algorithm behave almost identically on any two datasets differing in at most one record, so that no output can betray whether a particular individual took part; the formal statement is given in the next section.

The parameter ε (the privacy budget) controls the strength of the guarantee: smaller ε gives stronger privacy. The definition makes no assumptions about the adversary's background knowledge or computational power.

DP has become the gold standard for private data release at companies (Apple, Google, Microsoft, Uber) and in the U.S. Census Bureau's 2020 release.

- **Confusing differential privacy with k-anonymity or other syntactic notions**: k-anonymity can be broken by background knowledge or homogeneity attacks; DP gives a worst-case multiplicative guarantee independent of the adversary's knowledge.
- **Thinking that 'adding noise' is automatically DP**: The noise distribution and its scale must be calibrated exactly to the sensitivity and the desired ε (or (ε,δ)); arbitrary noise may give no guarantee.
- **Forgetting that post-processing does not cost privacy but composition does**: You can analyze the released DP object as much as you want for free; each new release that depends on the original data costs more budget.
- **Using a fixed ε across all datasets and applications**: ε is a policy choice that should depend on the sensitivity of the data, the number of expected queries, and the risk tolerance;  ε=1 is not 'always safe.'
- **Believing local DP (each user perturbs before sending) is the same as central DP**: Local DP requires much more noise (roughly √n worse) because each report must be private by itself; central DP (a trusted curator adds noise to the aggregate) is far more accurate.
- **Ignoring that DP protects participation, not the value of an attribute**: If everyone in a group has the same sensitive attribute, learning that the group statistic is high may still reveal the attribute for a member even if participation is hidden.
