# Quantum States and Von Neumann Entropy

Qubits, density matrices and partial traces; what a measurement is; and the von Neumann entropy, whose properties agree with Shannon's in every respect but one.

## Qubits, Superposition and No-Cloning

<!-- section: qubits-states -->

Classical information theory starts from a random variable on a finite alphabet. Quantum information theory starts from a vector in a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space, and almost every difference between the two theories can be traced to that one substitution.

:::notation
A vector is written $|\psi\rangle$ (a *ket*); its conjugate transpose is the row vector $\langle\psi|$ (a *bra*), so that $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ is the inner product — conjugate-linear in the first slot — and $|\psi\rangle\langle\phi|$ is an operator of rank one. Throughout this chapter every Hilbert space is finite-dimensional, so "operator" means "matrix" and every Hermitian operator has an orthonormal eigenbasis. Logarithms are base $2$ unless stated otherwise, and $I$ is the identity.
:::

:::definition[Qubit]{#qubit}
A **qubit** is a unit vector in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space with a fixed orthonormal *computational basis* $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$:

$$|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle, \qquad \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1.$$

Two unit vectors differing by a **global phase**, $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$, describe the same physical state.
:::

The amplitudes $\alpha,\beta$ are not probabilities. They are complex numbers whose *squared moduli* are probabilities, and the phase they carry is what makes quantum information behave unlike a biased coin.

:::definition[Born rule, computational basis]{#born-rule}
Measuring $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ in the basis $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ returns the outcome $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and $1$ with probability $|\beta|^2$, and leaves the system in the corresponding basis vector. The general form of this rule is the subject of the section *Measurement, POVMs and What Can Be Distinguished*.
:::

Global phase is invisible because $|e^{i\gamma}\alpha|^2 = |\alpha|^2$ in every basis. *Relative* phase is not: the states

$$|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\bigr), \qquad |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\bigr)$$

give identical statistics in the computational basis — one bit of noise each — yet they are orthogonal, $\langle +|-\rangle = 0$, and so are told apart perfectly by a measurement in the basis $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$. A classical bit has no analogue of this: there is no pair of coins that look identical under one reading and are perfectly distinguishable under another.

:::intuition
Think of the two amplitudes as an arrow in a plane rather than a pair of odds. Rotating the arrow can leave both shadows — the measurement probabilities in the computational basis — unchanged while pointing somewhere completely different. Information is stored in the direction of the arrow; a choice of basis decides which shadow you get to see.
:::

Discarding the invisible global phase leaves exactly two real parameters, and they have a standard geometric name.

:::definition[Bloch representation of a pure qubit]{#bloch-pure}
Every pure qubit state can be written, up to global phase, as

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad \theta \in [0,\pi],\ \varphi \in [0,2\pi),$$

so that pure states correspond to the points $(\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$ on the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$ — the **Bloch sphere**.
:::

The poles are $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$; the equator carries the equal-weight superpositions, with $|\pm\rangle$ at $\varphi = 0,\pi$. Orthogonal states sit at *antipodal* points, not at right angles: the Hilbert-space angle is doubled by the map. That factor of two is worth remembering, because it says that a qubit's two perfectly distinguishable states are as far apart as the geometry allows.

:::notation
The **Pauli matrices**

$$X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$$

together with $I$ form a basis of the real vector space of $2\times2$ Hermitian matrices. Each is Hermitian and unitary, squares to $I$, is traceless, and anticommutes with the other two.
:::

:::example[Reading a state off the sphere]
Where does $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\,|1\rangle$ sit on the Bloch sphere, and what is the probability of the outcome $1$?
:::

:::solution
Match the standard form: $\cos(\theta/2) = 1/2$ gives $\theta/2 = \pi/3$, so $\theta = 2\pi/3$. The phase of the second amplitude is $i = e^{i\pi/2}$, so $\varphi = \pi/2$. The Bloch vector is therefore

$$\Bigl(\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\Bigr) = \Bigl(0,\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ -\tfrac12\Bigr),$$

a point in the lower half of the sphere on the $y$-axis side. By the Born rule $\Pr(1) = |\sqrt3\,i/2|^2 = 3/4$. □
:::

Now the first genuinely quantum limitation, and the one that reshapes every coding theorem in the two chapters that follow: quantum information cannot be duplicated.

:::theorem[No-cloning]{#no-cloning}
Let $\dim\mathcal{H}\ge 2$. There is no unitary $U$ on $\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}$ and no fixed state $|b\rangle$ with

$$U\bigl(|\psi\rangle \otimes |b\rangle\bigr) = |\psi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle \quad\text{for every unit vector } |\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}.$$
:::

:::proof
Suppose such a $U$ existed and let $|\psi\rangle, |\phi\rangle$ be any two unit vectors. Unitaries preserve inner products, so pairing $U(|\psi\rangle|b\rangle)$ with $U(|\phi\rangle|b\rangle)$ gives

$$\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\,\langle b|b\rangle = \bigl(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\bigr)^2, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad z = z^2 \ \text{ with } z = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle.$$

Hence $z \in \{0,1\}$: any two states in the domain are either orthogonal or equal. In dimension at least two we may take $|\psi\rangle = |0\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle = |+\rangle$, for which $z = 1/\sqrt2$, a contradiction.
:::

The proof says exactly where the obstruction lies. A cloner for a *known* set of mutually orthogonal states is easy — measure, then re-prepare — and a cloner for one known state is trivial. What is impossible is a device that works on an unknown state drawn from a set containing two non-orthogonal members.

:::corollary[No perfect deterministic discrimination]{#no-discrimination}
Two states with $0 < |\langle\psi|\phi\rangle| < 1$ cannot be distinguished with certainty by any measurement.
:::

:::proof
If some measurement identified them with certainty, a single run would produce the classical label, from which arbitrarily many copies could be prepared, contradicting Theorem *No-cloning*. A quantitative version, giving the optimal error probability, appears below as Theorem *Helstrom bound, two pure states*.
:::

:::pitfall
No-cloning does not say that quantum information cannot be *moved*, *encoded* or *protected*. Teleportation moves an unknown qubit perfectly, and quantum error-correcting codes spread one qubit across five without copying it. What is forbidden is producing two independent systems each carrying the original state — which is exactly what a classical repeater does, and exactly why quantum repeaters are hard.
:::

## Density Matrices and Mixed States

<!-- section: density-matrices -->

A unit vector describes a system about which we have maximal information. That is rarely the situation: a source may emit $|\psi_1\rangle$ with probability $p_1$ and $|\psi_2\rangle$ with probability $p_2$, or the system we hold may be half of a larger one. Both cases are handled by one object.

:::definition[Density operator]{#density-operator}
A **density operator** on $\mathcal{H}$ is an operator $\rho$ that is

1. Hermitian, $\rho = \rho^{\dagger}$;
2. positive semidefinite, $\langle\phi|\rho|\phi\rangle \ge 0$ for all $|\phi\rangle$; and
3. of unit trace, $\operatorname{Tr}\rho = 1$.

It is **pure** if $\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ for some unit vector, and **mixed** otherwise. The density operators on a fixed $\mathcal{H}$ form a convex set whose extreme points are exactly the pure states.
:::

An ensemble $\{p_i, |\psi_i\rangle\}$ gives rise to the density operator $\rho = \sum_i p_i |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$, and the Born rule extends to it by linearity: the probability of an outcome with projector $P$ is $\operatorname{Tr}(P\rho)$. Conditions 1–3 are precisely what is needed for that formula to return a probability distribution for every measurement.

:::theorem[Purity criterion]{#purity}
For a density operator $\rho$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2) \le 1$, with equality if and only if $\rho$ is pure.
:::

:::proof
Diagonalise $\rho = \sum_i \lambda_i |i\rangle\langle i|$; positivity and unit trace make the spectrum a probability distribution, $\lambda_i\ge 0$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$. Then $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2) = \sum_i \lambda_i^2 \le \sum_i \lambda_i = 1$, because $\lambda_i^2\le\lambda_i$ on $[0,1]$. Equality needs $\lambda_i^2 = \lambda_i$ for every $i$, so every eigenvalue is $0$ or $1$; with unit trace exactly one equals $1$ and $\rho = |i\rangle\langle i|$ is a rank-one projector.
:::

The number $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)$ is the **purity**. On a $d$-dimensional system it runs from $1/d$, attained only by the **maximally mixed state** $I/d$, up to $1$ for pure states.

:::theorem[Bloch ball]{#bloch-ball}
Every qubit density matrix can be written uniquely as

$$\rho = \tfrac12\bigl(I + r_x X + r_y Y + r_z Z\bigr) = \tfrac12\bigl(I + \vec{r}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\bigr), \qquad \vec{r}\in\mathbb{R}^3,$$

and this is a density operator exactly when $\|\vec{r}\| \le 1$. Its eigenvalues are $\tfrac{1 \pm \|\vec r\|}{2}$, its purity is $\tfrac{1+\|\vec r\|^2}{2}$, and it is pure exactly when $\|\vec{r}\| = 1$.
:::

:::proof
$\{I,X,Y,Z\}$ is a basis of the Hermitian $2\times 2$ matrices, orthogonal under $\langle A,B\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)$ with each $\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_\mu^2) = 2$, so $\rho = \tfrac12\sum_\mu c_\mu\sigma_\mu$ with $c_\mu = \operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_\mu\rho)$ real and unique. Unit trace forces $c_0 = 1$, since the Paulis are traceless. For the spectrum, the anticommutation relations give $(\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = \|\vec r\|^2 I$, so $\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma$ has eigenvalues $\pm\|\vec r\|$ and $\rho$ has eigenvalues $(1\pm\|\vec r\|)/2$. These are non-negative exactly when $\|\vec r\|\le1$, and then

$$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2) = \Bigl(\tfrac{1+\|\vec r\|}{2}\Bigr)^{2} + \Bigl(\tfrac{1-\|\vec r\|}{2}\Bigr)^{2} = \tfrac{1+\|\vec r\|^2}{2}.$$
:::

A mixed state is *not* a state in which the system "really is" one of the $|\psi_i\rangle$ while we merely fail to know which. The reason is that $\rho$ does not remember the ensemble that produced it.

:::theorem[Ensembles with the same density matrix]{#ensemble-ambiguity}
The ensembles $\bigl\{\tfrac12, |0\rangle;\ \tfrac12, |1\rangle\bigr\}$ and $\bigl\{\tfrac12, |+\rangle;\ \tfrac12, |-\rangle\bigr\}$ have the same density operator $I/2$, and are therefore indistinguishable by any measurement whatsoever.
:::

:::proof
Directly, $\tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1| = \tfrac12 I$. Since $|\pm\rangle\langle\pm| = \tfrac12(I \pm X)$, the second ensemble gives $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(I+X) + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(I-X) = \tfrac12 I$ as well. Every measurement statistic has the form $\operatorname{Tr}(E\rho)$, so it depends on the ensemble only through $\rho$.
:::

:::intuition
Two different factories — one flipping a fair coin between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, the other between $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ — ship output that no experiment can tell apart. The density matrix is the complete inventory of what an experimenter can ever learn about a system. That is why every quantum information measure is a function of $\rho$ and never of the ensemble behind it.
:::

:::example[Purity of a partially mixed qubit]
Compute the Bloch vector, eigenvalues and purity of $\rho = 0.75\,|0\rangle\langle0| + 0.25\,|1\rangle\langle1|$.
:::

:::solution
In matrix form $\rho = \begin{pmatrix}0.75 & 0\\ 0& 0.25\end{pmatrix}$, already diagonal, so the eigenvalues are $0.75$ and $0.25$. Reading off the Bloch decomposition, $r_z = \operatorname{Tr}(Z\rho) = 0.75-0.25 = 0.5$ and $r_x = r_y = 0$, so $\vec r = (0,0,0.5)$ with $\|\vec r\| = 0.5$ — halfway out of the ball, exactly as Theorem *Bloch ball* predicts from the eigenvalues $(1\pm0.5)/2$. The purity is $0.75^2 + 0.25^2 = 0.625$, matching $(1+0.25)/2 = 0.625$. □
:::

:::pitfall
A superposition is not a mixture. The pure state $|+\rangle$ and the mixture $\tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|$ both give $50/50$ statistics in the computational basis, but $|+\rangle$ has purity $1$ and is *certain* in the $X$ basis, while the mixture has purity $\tfrac12$ and is $50/50$ in every basis. The off-diagonal entries of $\rho$ — the *coherences* — are what separate them.
:::

## Composite Systems and the Partial Trace

<!-- section: composite-partial-trace -->

Two classical variables are described by a joint distribution on the product alphabet; two quantum systems are described by a vector in the *tensor product* of their spaces. Dimension grows multiplicatively in both theories, but only in the quantum one does the joint state fail to be determined by the parts.

:::definition[Composite system, product and separable states]{#composite}
The state space of a system made of parts $A$ and $B$ is $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$, of dimension $d_Ad_B$. A state is a **product state** if it factorises as $|\psi\rangle_A\otimes|\phi\rangle_B$ (or $\rho_A\otimes\rho_B$), **separable** if it is a mixture $\sum_i p_i\,\sigma^A_i\otimes\tau^B_i$ of product states, and **entangled** otherwise.
:::

:::definition[Partial trace]{#partial-trace}
The **partial trace over $B$** is the linear map $\operatorname{Tr}_B$ from operators on $\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_B$ to operators on $\mathcal{H}_A$ determined by

$$\operatorname{Tr}_B\bigl(M \otimes N\bigr) = M\,\operatorname{Tr}(N).$$

In components, for any orthonormal basis $\{|j\rangle_B\}$,

$$\rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B(\rho_{AB}) = \sum_j \bigl(I_A \otimes \langle j|_B\bigr)\,\rho_{AB}\,\bigl(I_A\otimes|j\rangle_B\bigr).$$

$\rho_A$ is the **reduced state**, or **marginal**, of $A$.
:::

The partial trace earns its place by being the *only* map with the right operational meaning: it is the state reproducing all statistics of measurements performed on $A$ alone.

:::theorem[The partial trace is the right marginal]{#partial-trace-justified}
For every operator $M$ on $\mathcal{H}_A$,

$$\operatorname{Tr}\bigl[(M\otimes I_B)\,\rho_{AB}\bigr] = \operatorname{Tr}\bigl[M\,\operatorname{Tr}_B(\rho_{AB})\bigr],$$

and $\operatorname{Tr}_B$ is the unique linear map with this property.
:::

:::proof
Both sides are linear in $\rho_{AB}$, so it is enough to check them on $\rho_{AB} = P\otimes Q$: the left side is $\operatorname{Tr}(MP)\operatorname{Tr}(Q)$ and the right side is $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(MP\operatorname{Tr}(Q)\bigr)$, the same number. For uniqueness, suppose a linear $\Lambda$ had the property too. Then $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl[M\bigl(\Lambda(\rho)-\operatorname{Tr}_B\rho\bigr)\bigr] = 0$ for every $M$; letting $M$ run over a basis of operators on $\mathcal{H}_A$ forces $\Lambda(\rho) = \operatorname{Tr}_B(\rho)$, because the trace form $\langle M,N\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(M^{\dagger}N)$ is non-degenerate.
:::

The classical analogue is summation, $p_A(a) = \sum_b p(a,b)$, and it behaves the same way — except in what it does to *uncertainty*. Classically the marginal of a point mass is a point mass. Quantum mechanically the marginal of a pure state need not be pure at all, and that single fact is entanglement.

:::theorem[Schmidt decomposition]{#schmidt}
Let $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ be a unit vector. Then there are orthonormal families $\{|a_i\rangle\}$ in $\mathcal{H}_A$ and $\{|b_i\rangle\}$ in $\mathcal{H}_B$ and reals $\lambda_i > 0$ with $\sum_i\lambda_i = 1$ such that

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sqrt{\lambda_i}\,|a_i\rangle\otimes|b_i\rangle .$$

The number $k \le \min(d_A,d_B)$ is the **Schmidt rank** and the $\lambda_i$ the **Schmidt coefficients**. Moreover $\rho_A = \sum_i\lambda_i|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$ and $\rho_B = \sum_i \lambda_i |b_i\rangle\langle b_i|$: the two marginals share their non-zero spectrum.
:::

:::proof
Write $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m,n} C_{mn}|m\rangle|n\rangle$ in fixed bases, so $C$ is a $d_A\times d_B$ matrix with $\operatorname{Tr}(CC^{\dagger}) = 1$. The singular value decomposition gives $C = UDV$ with $U,V$ unitary and $D$ diagonal with entries $s_i\ge0$. Substituting and setting $|a_i\rangle = \sum_m U_{mi}|m\rangle$ and $|b_i\rangle = \sum_n V_{in}|n\rangle$ — orthonormal because $U$ and $V$ are unitary — gives $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i s_i|a_i\rangle|b_i\rangle$. Normalisation forces $\sum_i s_i^2 = 1$, so put $\lambda_i = s_i^2$. Finally

$$\rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \sum_{i,j}s_is_j\,|a_i\rangle\langle a_j|\,\langle b_j|b_i\rangle = \sum_i s_i^2\,|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|,$$

and symmetrically for $\rho_B$.
:::

:::corollary[Product states are exactly the Schmidt-rank-one states]{#schmidt-rank-one}
A pure $|\psi\rangle_{AB}$ is a product state if and only if its Schmidt rank is $1$, if and only if $\rho_A$ is pure.
:::

:::proof
Schmidt rank $1$ means $|\psi\rangle = |a_1\rangle|b_1\rangle$, a product; conversely a product state has rank $1$. By Theorem *Schmidt decomposition* the spectrum of $\rho_A$ is $(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k)$, which is a point mass exactly when $k = 1$, i.e. exactly when $\rho_A$ is pure.
:::

:::example[The Bell state and its marginals]
Compute $\rho_A$ for $|\Phi^{+}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\bigr)$ and give its Schmidt rank.
:::

:::solution
The state is already in Schmidt form with $|a_i\rangle = |b_i\rangle = |i\rangle$ and $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \tfrac12$, so the Schmidt rank is $2$ and

$$\rho_A = \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1| = \tfrac{I}{2}.$$

By hand it comes out the same: $\rho_{AB} = \tfrac12\bigl(|00\rangle\langle00| + |00\rangle\langle11| + |11\rangle\langle00| + |11\rangle\langle11|\bigr)$, and tracing out $B$ kills the two cross terms because $\langle0|1\rangle = 0$. The joint state is pure and maximally informative; each half is maximally *un*informative. □
:::

The converse of that example is a construction we shall use repeatedly.

:::theorem[Purification]{#purification}
For every density operator $\rho_A$ there is a space $\mathcal{H}_R$ of dimension $\operatorname{rank}\rho_A$ and a pure state $|\psi\rangle_{AR}$ with $\operatorname{Tr}_R|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \rho_A$. Any two purifications of $\rho_A$ differ by a unitary acting on $R$ alone.
:::

:::proof
Diagonalise $\rho_A = \sum_i\lambda_i|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$ over the non-zero eigenvalues, take $\mathcal{H}_R$ with an orthonormal basis $\{|r_i\rangle\}$ of the same size, and set $|\psi\rangle_{AR} = \sum_i\sqrt{\lambda_i}|a_i\rangle|r_i\rangle$. The computation in the proof of Theorem *Schmidt decomposition* gives $\operatorname{Tr}_R|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \rho_A$. For uniqueness, any purification is in Schmidt form with the same coefficients $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ and the same $|a_i\rangle$ — these are fixed as the spectral data of $\rho_A$ — so two purifications differ only in the orthonormal family used on $R$, and a unitary carries one orthonormal family to another.
:::

:::intuition
Every mixed state is a pure state with part of it hidden. Randomness in quantum theory can always be recast as entanglement with something you are not looking at: the environment, the other half of a pair, a machine that measured and will not tell you. Purification turns that slogan into a theorem, and it converts many statements about mixed states into statements about pure states on a larger space, where the Schmidt decomposition applies.
:::

:::pitfall
The partial trace is irreversible, and must not be read as "the state of $A$ given whatever $B$ turned out to be". There is no conditional state until a measurement on $B$ actually happens — and no measurement on $B$ changes $\rho_A$ at all, which is exactly what forbids signalling with entanglement.
:::

## Measurement, POVMs and What Can Be Distinguished

<!-- section: measurement -->

Everything an experimenter learns arrives through a measurement, so the limits on measurement are the limits on quantum information. The general rule has two layers: what the outcome probabilities are, and what state is left behind.

:::definition[Projective measurement]{#projective-measurement}
A **projective measurement** is a family of orthogonal projectors $\{P_m\}$ with $P_mP_{m'} = \delta_{mm'}P_m$ and $\sum_m P_m = I$. Applied to $\rho$ it returns outcome $m$ with probability

$$\Pr(m) = \operatorname{Tr}(P_m\rho),$$

leaving the post-measurement state $P_m\rho P_m/\Pr(m)$. Equivalently one measures an **observable** $M = \sum_m m\,P_m$, a Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are the outcomes.
:::

For $\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ and $P_m = |m\rangle\langle m|$ this is the Born rule of the first section, $\Pr(m) = |\langle m|\psi\rangle|^2$. But projective measurements are not the most general possibility: one may couple the system to an ancilla and measure *that*, and the resulting statistics need not come from any projective measurement on the system alone.

:::definition[POVM]{#povm}
A **positive operator-valued measure** is a family $\{E_m\}$ of positive semidefinite operators with $\sum_m E_m = I$. Outcome $m$ occurs with probability $\Pr(m) = \operatorname{Tr}(E_m\rho)$.
:::

Positivity makes the probabilities non-negative and completeness makes them sum to $1$, so a POVM is exactly what is needed for $\rho\mapsto(\Pr(m))_m$ to be a probability distribution for every $\rho$. Projective measurements are the special case $E_m = P_m$ with $E_m^2 = E_m$; in general a POVM element is not a projector, and the number of outcomes may exceed the dimension.

:::note
Naimark's dilation theorem says every POVM on $\mathcal{H}$ arises from a projective measurement on a larger space $\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{anc}}$, so nothing is lost by taking POVMs as the primitive. We quote it: its proof is a spectral-theory argument outside this course, which assumes linear algebra but not operator theory. Note also that a POVM fixes the outcome *probabilities* but not the post-measurement state — several distinct physical processes share one POVM.
:::

:::example[A POVM that no projective measurement can imitate]
Alice sends either $|0\rangle$ or $|+\rangle$, each with probability $\tfrac12$. Bob wants a measurement that *never lies*: it may output "don't know", but when it names a state it must be right. Show that

$$E_0 = c\,|-\rangle\langle-|, \qquad E_{+} = c\,|1\rangle\langle1|, \qquad E_{?} = I - E_0 - E_{+}$$

does this, find the largest admissible $c$, and say how often Bob gets an answer.
:::

:::solution
Because $\langle-|+\rangle = 0$, the outcome $E_0$ has probability zero when the state is $|+\rangle$; so if Bob sees it, the state was $|0\rangle$ with certainty. Symmetrically $\langle1|0\rangle = 0$, so $E_+$ can occur only for $|+\rangle$. The scheme is error-free for every $c>0$, and $c$ is taken as large as $E_{?}\ge 0$ allows.

Now $E_0 + E_+ = c\bigl(|-\rangle\langle-| + |1\rangle\langle1|\bigr)$, which in the computational basis is

$$c\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & -1/2\\ -1/2 & 3/2\end{pmatrix},$$

a matrix of trace $2c$ and determinant $c^2(3/4 - 1/4) = c^2/2$, so its eigenvalues satisfy $\mu^2 - 2c\mu + c^2/2 = 0$ and are $\mu = c\bigl(1\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigr)$. We need the larger one to be at most $1$, giving $c \le \bigl(1+1/\sqrt2\bigr)^{-1} = 2-\sqrt2\approx 0.5858$.

At that value the conclusive probability for input $|0\rangle$ is $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(E_0|0\rangle\langle0|\bigr) = c\,|\langle-|0\rangle|^2 = c/2 = 1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\approx 0.293$, and the same for $|+\rangle$ by symmetry. So Bob learns the state with certainty about $29.3\%$ of the time and learns nothing at all otherwise — the trade Corollary *No perfect deterministic discrimination* forces on him. □
:::

If Bob must guess every time instead, the optimal trade-off is a clean formula.

:::theorem[Helstrom bound, two pure states]{#helstrom}
Let $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ be equally likely, with overlap $c = |\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|$. The minimum probability of a wrong guess, over all POVMs, is

$$p_{\mathrm{err}} = \tfrac12\bigl(1 - \sqrt{1-c^2}\bigr).$$
:::

:::proof
Work in the span of the two states and set $\Delta = \tfrac12|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| - \tfrac12|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$, a Hermitian traceless operator, so its two eigenvalues are $\pm\mu$. For a two-outcome POVM $\{E, I-E\}$ the success probability is

$$\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(E|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\bigr) + \tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((I-E)|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\bigr) = \tfrac12 + \operatorname{Tr}(E\Delta).$$

Since $0\le E\le I$, the quantity $\operatorname{Tr}(E\Delta)$ is largest when $E$ is the projector onto the positive eigenspace of $\Delta$, which gives $\mu$; hence $p_{\mathrm{err}} = \tfrac12-\mu$. To find $\mu$, use $\operatorname{Tr}\Delta = 0$ and

$$\operatorname{Tr}(\Delta^2) = \tfrac14\bigl(1 + 1 - 2c^2\bigr) = \tfrac{1-c^2}{2},$$

so that $2\mu^2 = \tfrac{1-c^2}{2}$ and $\mu = \tfrac12\sqrt{1-c^2}$.
:::

Both endpoints are what intuition demands: orthogonal states ($c=0$) give $p_{\mathrm{err}} = 0$, identical states ($c=1$) give $p_{\mathrm{err}} = \tfrac12$, the coin flip. Everything in between is a genuine information-theoretic cost, and it is why the Holevo bound of the next chapter falls strictly below $\log d$ for non-orthogonal ensembles.

:::pitfall
"The state collapses" is a statement about the *projective* rule, and only for a measurement performed and recorded. If a measurement is made and the outcome discarded, the correct description is the average $\sum_m P_m\rho P_m$ — a *decohered* state, generally mixed, and not any one of the $P_m\rho P_m/\Pr(m)$.
:::

## Von Neumann Entropy

<!-- section: von-neumann -->

We can now define the quantity this chapter is named for. It is Shannon's entropy applied to the one distribution a quantum state canonically carries: its spectrum.

:::definition[Von Neumann entropy]{#von-neumann-entropy}
The **von Neumann entropy** of a density operator $\rho$ is

$$S(\rho) = -\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho\log\rho\bigr),$$

where $\log\rho$ is defined through the spectral decomposition. Equivalently, if $\rho$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d$ then

$$S(\rho) = -\sum_i\lambda_i\log\lambda_i = H(\lambda),$$

the Shannon entropy of the spectrum, with the convention $0\log 0 = 0$. Units are bits when the logarithm is base $2$.
:::

Never take a matrix logarithm literally: diagonalise, then evaluate $-\sum_i\lambda_i\log\lambda_i$. And because $S$ depends only on the spectrum it is invariant under every unitary, $S(U\rho U^{\dagger}) = S(\rho)$ — the counterpart of the fact that Shannon entropy does not care how the alphabet is labelled.

:::theorem[Basic properties]{#vn-basic}
For $\rho$ on a $d$-dimensional space:

1. $S(\rho)\ge 0$, with equality if and only if $\rho$ is pure;
2. $S(\rho)\le\log d$, with equality if and only if $\rho = I/d$;
3. $S(U\rho U^{\dagger}) = S(\rho)$ for every unitary $U$;
4. if $\rho$ is diagonal in some basis with entries $p_i$, then $S(\rho) = H(p)$.
:::

:::proof
All four reduce to $S(\rho) = H(\lambda)$ and the corresponding classical facts. (1) $H\ge0$ with equality iff the distribution is a point mass, and by Theorem *Purity criterion* a point-mass spectrum means a pure state. (2) $H(\lambda)\le\log d$ with equality iff $\lambda$ is uniform, which says $\rho = I/d$. (3) Conjugating by a unitary changes the eigenvectors and not the eigenvalues. (4) Immediate: a diagonal matrix's entries are its eigenvalues.
:::

:::notation
For a bipartite state $\rho_{AB}$ write $S(A) = S(\rho_A)$ and $S(AB) = S(\rho_{AB})$, and define

$$S(A\mid B) = S(AB) - S(B), \qquad I(A;B) = S(A) + S(B) - S(AB),$$

so that $I(A;B) = S(A) - S(A\mid B)$, exactly as in the classical chain rules. There is no underlying joint distribution these are computed from; the formulas *are* the definitions.
:::

:::example[Entropy from the Bloch radius]
Give $S(\rho)$ for a qubit whose Bloch vector has length $r$, and evaluate it at $\rho = 0.75|0\rangle\langle0| + 0.25|1\rangle\langle1|$.
:::

:::solution
By Theorem *Bloch ball* the eigenvalues are $(1\pm r)/2$, so

$$S(\rho) = h\!\left(\tfrac{1+r}{2}\right), \qquad h(q) = -q\log q - (1-q)\log(1-q).$$

For the given state $r = 0.5$, so $S = h(0.75) = -0.75\log_2 0.75 - 0.25\log_2 0.25 \approx 0.311 + 0.5 = 0.811$ bits. The function falls from $1$ bit at the centre of the ball to $0$ on its surface: entropy measures how far inside the Bloch ball a qubit sits. □
:::

Relative entropy transfers as well, and it is the engine behind almost every inequality in the next section.

:::definition[Quantum relative entropy]{#quantum-relative-entropy}
For density operators $\rho,\sigma$,

$$D(\rho\|\sigma) = \operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho\log\rho\bigr) - \operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\rho\log\sigma\bigr),$$

taken to be $+\infty$ when the support of $\rho$ is not contained in the support of $\sigma$.
:::

:::theorem[Klein's inequality]{#klein}
$D(\rho\|\sigma)\ge 0$, with equality if and only if $\rho = \sigma$.
:::

:::proof
Let $\rho = \sum_i\lambda_i|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$ and $\sigma = \sum_j\mu_j|b_j\rangle\langle b_j|$ be spectral decompositions and put $P_{ij} = |\langle a_i|b_j\rangle|^2$, a doubly stochastic matrix because both families are orthonormal bases. Then $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\sigma) = \sum_{i,j}\lambda_iP_{ij}\log\mu_j$, and applying Jensen's inequality to the concave $\log$ with the weights $P_{ij}$, which sum to $1$ over $j$,

$$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\sigma) \le \sum_i\lambda_i\log\Bigl(\sum_j P_{ij}\mu_j\Bigr) = \sum_i\lambda_i\log\nu_i,\qquad \nu_i := \sum_jP_{ij}\mu_j.$$

Hence $D(\rho\|\sigma)\ge\sum_i\lambda_i\log(\lambda_i/\nu_i) = D(\lambda\|\nu)\ge0$ by the classical Gibbs inequality; note $\sum_i\nu_i = 1$ because $P$ is doubly stochastic. Equality in Jensen forces the $\mu_j$ to agree wherever $P_{ij}>0$, and equality in Gibbs forces $\lambda = \nu$; together these give $\rho = \sigma$.
:::

Two consequences get used constantly. Taking $\sigma = I/d$ gives $D(\rho\|I/d) = \log d - S(\rho)\ge 0$, recovering the maximum-entropy bound; and for any $\sigma$,

$$S(\rho)\le-\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\log\sigma),$$

the quantum form of "cross-entropy is at least entropy" — describing a state with the wrong model costs you bits.

:::intuition
Von Neumann entropy is Shannon entropy asked in the state's own preferred basis. Measure $\rho$ in its eigenbasis and you get a classical variable of entropy exactly $S(\rho)$; measure it in any other basis and the classical entropy is strictly larger, because the doubly stochastic smearing in the proof of Klein's inequality can only spread a distribution out. So $S(\rho)$ is the *minimum* classical randomness extractable from the state — read the other way, the irreducible uncertainty in it.
:::

## Properties of Von Neumann Entropy

<!-- section: entropy-properties -->

Almost every classical entropy inequality has an exact quantum counterpart. The exception is important enough to organise this whole section: classically $H(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is never smaller than the entropy of any subcollection, and quantum mechanically that fails.

:::theorem[Subadditivity]{#subadditivity}
For any bipartite state, $S(AB)\le S(A)+S(B)$, with equality if and only if $\rho_{AB} = \rho_A\otimes\rho_B$.
:::

:::proof
Compute the relative entropy of $\rho_{AB}$ to the product of its own marginals. Since $\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = \log\rho_A\otimes I + I\otimes\log\rho_B$,

$$D\bigl(\rho_{AB}\,\|\,\rho_A\otimes\rho_B\bigr) = -S(AB) - \operatorname{Tr}\bigl[\rho_{AB}(\log\rho_A\otimes I)\bigr] - \operatorname{Tr}\bigl[\rho_{AB}(I\otimes\log\rho_B)\bigr].$$

By Theorem *The partial trace is the right marginal* the second trace is $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_A\log\rho_A) = -S(A)$ and the third is $-S(B)$, so

$$D\bigl(\rho_{AB}\,\|\,\rho_A\otimes\rho_B\bigr) = S(A)+S(B)-S(AB) = I(A;B).$$

Klein's inequality gives $I(A;B)\ge0$, which is the claim, with equality exactly when $\rho_{AB} = \rho_A\otimes\rho_B$.
:::

That identity — quantum mutual information *is* a relative entropy — is worth keeping. It gives $I(A;B)\ge0$ at once, and it is the form in which the Holevo bound will be proved in the next chapter.

:::theorem[Araki–Lieb triangle inequality]{#araki-lieb}
For any bipartite state, $S(AB)\ge\bigl|S(A)-S(B)\bigr|$.
:::

:::proof
Purify: by Theorem *Purification* there is a system $R$ and a pure $|\psi\rangle_{ABR}$ whose marginal on $AB$ is $\rho_{AB}$. Because the global state is pure, Theorem *Schmidt decomposition* across the cut $AB\,|\,R$ gives $S(AB) = S(R)$, and across the cut $B\,|\,AR$ gives $S(B) = S(AR)$. Applying subadditivity to the pair $(A,R)$,

$$S(B) = S(AR)\le S(A)+S(R) = S(A)+S(AB),$$

so $S(AB)\ge S(B)-S(A)$. Exchanging $A$ and $B$ gives $S(AB)\ge S(A)-S(B)$, and the two together give the absolute value.
:::

:::corollary[Conditional entropy is bounded below]{#cond-entropy-bound}
$S(A\mid B)\ge -S(A)$, and in particular $S(A\mid B)\ge-\log d_A$.
:::

:::proof
$S(A\mid B) = S(AB)-S(B)\ge -S(A)$ is Araki–Lieb rearranged, and $S(A)\le\log d_A$ by Theorem *Basic properties*.
:::

Here is the break with the classical theory. Classically $H(X\mid Y)\ge0$: knowing $Y$ cannot leave you with *negative* uncertainty about $X$, because $H(X,Y)\ge H(Y)$. The quantum statement is simply false.

:::theorem[Failure of monotonicity]{#non-monotonicity}
There is a bipartite state with $S(AB) < S(A)$; equivalently $S(A\mid B)$ can be negative. For the Bell state $|\Phi^{+}\rangle$ one has $S(AB) = 0$, $S(A) = S(B) = 1$ and $S(A\mid B) = -1$ bit, the most negative value Corollary *Conditional entropy is bounded below* permits for a qubit.
:::

:::proof
$|\Phi^+\rangle$ is pure, so $S(AB) = 0$. Its Schmidt coefficients are $\tfrac12,\tfrac12$, so $\rho_A = \rho_B = I/2$ and $S(A) = S(B) = 1$ by Theorem *Basic properties*. Hence $S(A\mid B) = 0-1 = -1$, while $-S(A) = -1$ shows the bound is attained.
:::

:::intuition
Classically a joint state is a list of values, and the whole cannot be less uncertain than a part because the part is read off the whole. An entangled pair is not a list of values. The pair is in a perfectly definite state — "the two outcomes will agree" — while neither member has any definite state of its own. The negative bit in $S(A\mid B)$ is not a bookkeeping accident: in the quantum state-merging protocol it counts Bell pairs *returned* to the parties, one ebit per negative bit.
:::

The deeper inequality, from which most of quantum Shannon theory is derived, cannot be proved with the tools this course develops.

:::theorem[Strong subadditivity, quoted]{#ssa}
For any tripartite state $\rho_{ABC}$,

$$S(ABC) + S(B)\ \le\ S(AB) + S(BC),$$

equivalently $S(A\mid BC)\le S(A\mid B)$ — discarding a system cannot decrease conditional entropy — equivalently again $I(A;BC)\ge I(A;B)$.
:::

:::proof
This is a sketch, and the gap is named. Unlike subadditivity, the result does not follow from Klein's inequality: the natural argument would need $\operatorname{Tr}\exp$ to behave multiplicatively on non-commuting operators, and it does not. Lieb and Ruskai (1973) derive it from **Lieb's concavity theorem**, that $(A,B)\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(K^{\dagger}A^{t}KB^{1-t}\bigr)$ is jointly concave in positive $A,B$ for $t\in[0,1]$ — a matrix-analysis result about non-commuting operators requiring operator-convexity theory this course does not develop. An alternative route derives it from the monotonicity of relative entropy under quantum channels, stated in the next chapter; each of the two statements implies the other, so quoting one is quoting both.
:::

:::corollary[Consequences of strong subadditivity]{#ssa-corollaries}
Strong subadditivity yields: $I(A;B)\le I(A;BC)$; concavity of $S(A\mid B)$ as a function of $\rho_{AB}$; and $S(A\mid B)\ge0$ for every *separable* $\rho_{AB}$ — so a negative conditional entropy certifies entanglement.
:::

:::proof
The first is a restatement. Granting the second, take a separable $\rho_{AB} = \sum_i p_i\,\sigma^A_i\otimes\tau^B_i$; concavity gives

$$S(A\mid B)_{\rho}\ \ge\ \sum_i p_i\,S(A\mid B)_{\sigma_i\otimes\tau_i} = \sum_i p_i\,S(\sigma_i)\ \ge\ 0,$$

using that a product state has $S(AB) = S(A)+S(B)$ and hence $S(A\mid B) = S(A)$. The concavity step is one of the standard equivalent forms of strong subadditivity and is quoted with it.
:::

Finally, mixing. Classically, averaging distributions increases entropy, by at most the entropy of the mixing variable; both halves survive the passage to quantum states.

:::theorem[Mixing bounds]{#mixing}
Let $\rho = \sum_i p_i\rho_i$ be a mixture of density operators. Then

$$\sum_i p_iS(\rho_i)\ \le\ S(\rho)\ \le\ \sum_i p_iS(\rho_i) + H(p),$$

and the right-hand inequality is an equality if and only if the $\rho_i$ have pairwise orthogonal supports.
:::

:::proof
For the lower bound — concavity of $S$ — expand the average relative entropy to $\rho$:

$$\sum_i p_i D(\rho_i\|\rho) = -\sum_i p_iS(\rho_i) - \operatorname{Tr}\Bigl(\bigl(\textstyle\sum_i p_i\rho_i\bigr)\log\rho\Bigr) = S(\rho) - \sum_i p_iS(\rho_i),$$

and every $D(\rho_i\|\rho)\ge0$ by Klein's inequality. For the upper bound, adjoin a classical register: on an orthonormal family $\{|i\rangle\}$ put $\rho_{XA} = \sum_i p_i\,|i\rangle\langle i|\otimes\rho_i$. This is block diagonal, so its spectrum is the union of the spectra of the blocks $p_i\rho_i$, giving

$$S(XA) = -\sum_i\sum_k p_i\lambda^{(i)}_k\log\bigl(p_i\lambda^{(i)}_k\bigr) = H(p) + \sum_i p_iS(\rho_i),$$

where $\lambda^{(i)}$ is the spectrum of $\rho_i$. Also $S(X) = H(p)$ and $S(A) = S(\rho)$. The claim is exactly $S(A)\le S(XA)$, that is $S(X\mid A)\ge0$, which holds because $\rho_{XA}$ is separable — Corollary *Consequences of strong subadditivity*. Equality means $I(X;A) = H(p)$, i.e. $X$ is perfectly recoverable from $A$, which happens exactly when the $\rho_i$ occupy orthogonal subspaces and so are told apart by one projective measurement.
:::

:::example[The mixing gap is the Holevo quantity]
Compute both bounds for the ensemble $\bigl\{\tfrac12,|0\rangle;\ \tfrac12,|+\rangle\bigr\}$ and interpret the gap $S(\rho)-\sum_i p_iS(\rho_i)$.
:::

:::solution
Both members are pure, so $\sum_i p_iS(\rho_i) = 0$ and the lower bound is $0$, while $H(p) = 1$ bit gives the upper bound. The average state is

$$\rho = \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|+\rangle\langle+| = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3/4 & 1/4\\ 1/4 & 1/4\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its trace is $1$ and its determinant is $3/16-1/16 = 1/8$, so the eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2-\lambda+1/8 = 0$ and are $\lambda = \tfrac12\bigl(1\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigr)\approx0.8536,\ 0.1464$. Hence $S(\rho) = h(0.8536)\approx0.601$ bits, strictly between the two bounds.

The gap is $0.601$ bits, and it is exactly the Holevo quantity $\chi$ of this ensemble. By the theorem of the next chapter no measurement can extract more than $0.601$ bits about which state was sent — less than the $1$ bit that was encoded, because $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$ are not orthogonal. □
:::

:::pitfall
Do not import $H(X\mid Y)\ge0$, $H(X)\le H(X,Y)$, or "a subsystem is less uncertain than the whole" into the quantum setting. The first two are false. The third, in the correct form $S(A\mid BC)\le S(A\mid B)$, is true but is strong subadditivity and is not cheap. When in doubt, test a claim on the Bell state: it is the counterexample to almost every false quantum entropy inequality.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a qubit as a biased coin.** A coin has one parameter, a qubit has two, and the extra one — relative phase — is what makes $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ perfectly distinguishable despite identical computational-basis statistics.
- **Confusing a superposition with a mixture.** $|+\rangle$ is pure, with purity $1$; $\tfrac12(|0\rangle\langle0|+|1\rangle\langle1|)$ is mixed, with purity $\tfrac12$. They agree in one basis and differ in every other.
- **Thinking the ensemble can be recovered from $\rho$.** It cannot: $\{\tfrac12,|0\rangle;\tfrac12,|1\rangle\}$ and $\{\tfrac12,|+\rangle;\tfrac12,|-\rangle\}$ both give $I/2$, and no experiment separates them.
- **Reading the partial trace as conditioning.** $\rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B\rho_{AB}$ is the unconditional marginal. There is no "state of $A$ given $B$" until $B$ is measured, and measuring $B$ never changes $\rho_A$.
- **Assuming $S(A\mid B)\ge0$.** It is $-1$ bit for a Bell pair. Any argument that silently uses a classical conditioning inequality on quantum states is wrong.
- **Using $S(AB)\ge S(A)$.** False, and the same Bell pair kills it. The correct lower bound is Araki–Lieb, $S(AB)\ge|S(A)-S(B)|$.
- **Taking the logarithm of $\rho$ entrywise.** $\log\rho$ is defined through the spectral decomposition; for a non-diagonal $\rho$ the entrywise logarithm is not even the right kind of object.
- **Expecting strong subadditivity to be a corollary of Klein's inequality.** It is not. It rests on Lieb's concavity theorem, and every proof of it that works proves something of that depth along the way.
