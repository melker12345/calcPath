# Network Information Theory

Multiple-access and broadcast channels, network coding, common randomness, and the Blahut–Arimoto algorithm.

A multiple-access channel (MAC) has several independent transmitters sending to a single receiver over a shared medium. The receiver observes a superposition (or other combination) of the transmitted signals plus noise. The fundamental question is the set of rate pairs (R1, R2, …) at which the senders can reliably communicate their independent messages.

The MAC is the simplest non-trivial network in information theory and introduces the concept of a capacity region rather than a single capacity number.

## The Multiple-Access Channel Model

<!-- section: mac-model -->

:::definition[Multiple-access channel]{#mac}
Two (or more) encoders observe independent messages $W_1, W_2$ and produce channel inputs $X_1^n, X_2^n$ according to their codebooks. The channel is a conditional law $p(y \mid x_1, x_2, \dots)$. The single decoder, upon receiving $Y^n$, must recover both messages with high probability.
:::

The classic example is the Gaussian MAC

$$Y = X_1 + X_2 + Z, \qquad Z \sim N(0,N),$$

with power constraints $\mathbb{E}[X_i^2] \le P_i$.

:::intuition
Two people shouting messages at the same time to one listener over a noisy room. The listener hears the sum of voices plus background noise and must disentangle who said what.

The 'channel' is the acoustics that mix the voices; the listener's job is joint decoding.
:::

:::example
Consider the *binary adder MAC* with integer sum: $X_1, X_2 \in \{0,1\}$ and $Y = X_1 + X_2$ (noiseless, so $Y \in \{0,1,2\}$).
:::

:::solution
Enumerate the outputs.** The map is deterministic: $(0,0)\to 0$, $(0,1)\to 1$, $(1,0)\to 1$, $(1,1)\to 2$.
**Step 2 — Which inputs does $Y$ reveal?** If $Y=0$ the decoder knows $(X_1,X_2)=(0,0)$; if $Y=2$ it knows $(1,1)$. Only $Y=1$ is ambiguous — it could be $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$.
**Step 3 — Why joint decoding matters.** Neither user's bit is individually recoverable from $Y$ alone when $Y=1$, yet the *pair* is confusable only along one axis. Take $X_1, X_2$ i.i.d. uniform. Then $Y$ takes values $0,1,2$ with probabilities $\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14$, so $H(Y) = -\tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 - \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1.5$ bits.
**Step 4 — Sum-rate.** Because the channel is deterministic, $I(X_1,X_2;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X_1,X_2) = 1.5 - 0 = 1.5$ bits per use. So the two users *together* can push $1.5$ bits/use through this channel — strictly more than the $1$ bit a single binary input could carry, and strictly less than the $2$ bits they would get on clean separate links.
**Sanity check:** $1 \le 1.5 \le 2$, matching the intuition that sharing the channel costs something (you cannot get the full $2$ bits) but joint use still beats giving the whole channel to one user.
:::

## Capacity Region for the Two-User MAC

<!-- section: capacity-region -->

:::theorem[Capacity region of the two-user MAC]{#mac-capacity-region}
The capacity region of the two-user MAC is the convex closure of all rate pairs $(R_1,R_2)$ satisfying

$$R_1 \le I(X_1; Y \mid X_2), \qquad R_2 \le I(X_2; Y \mid X_1), \qquad R_1 + R_2 \le I(X_1,X_2; Y)$$

for some product distribution $p(x_1)p(x_2)$, i.e. independent codebooks.
:::

Time-sharing convexifies the region.

For the Gaussian MAC the region is a pentagon whose corner points are achieved by treating one user as noise or by successive interference cancellation (SIC) ordering.

:::intuition
Each user has a 'private' rate limited by what the other user's signal looks like (as side information at the decoder).

Together they cannot exceed the mutual information when both inputs are considered as a single super-input.

The sum-rate bound is the total 'pipe' capacity when the two users cooperate in choosing their signals.
:::

:::example
Gaussian MAC with equal powers $P_1 = P_2 = P = 1$ and noise variance $N = 1$. All logs are base 2, giving rates in bits per channel use.
:::

:::solution
Individual bounds (other user known).** With $X_2$ available at the decoder as side information it can be subtracted, so $R_1 \le I(X_1;Y\mid X_2) = \tfrac12\log(1 + P/N) = \tfrac12\log 2 = 0.5$. By symmetry $R_2 \le 0.5$ as well.
**Step 2 — Sum bound.** $R_1 + R_2 \le I(X_1,X_2;Y) = \tfrac12\log(1 + (P_1+P_2)/N) = \tfrac12\log(1 + 2) = \tfrac12\log 3 \approx 0.792$.
**Step 3 — The pentagon corners.** The two individual caps at $0.5$ and the sum cap at $0.792$ carve a pentagon; its dominant face runs between the two corner points. Consider the SIC corner where user 1 is decoded *first* while user 2 is treated as noise. Decoding user 1 against noise power $P + N$ gives $R_1 = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{P}{P+N}\big) = \tfrac12\log 1.5 \approx 0.292$ bits.
**Step 3b — Second user after cancellation.** Subtract user 1; user 2 then sees noise power $N$ only, giving $R_2 = \tfrac12\log(1+P/N) = \tfrac12\log 2 = 0.5$ bits.
**Step 4 — Verify the corner lies on the sum face.** $R_1 + R_2 = 0.292 + 0.5 = 0.792 = \tfrac12\log 3$. ✓ The corner sits exactly on the sum-rate boundary, and the symmetric corner $(0.5,\,0.292)$ is reached by reversing the decoding order.
**Sanity check:** each user's rate stays below its individual cap ($0.292 < 0.5$ and $0.5 = 0.5$), and the two corners are connected by a line of slope $-1$ (constant sum $0.792$) that time-sharing traces out.
:::

## Coding Strategies: Joint Decoding and SIC

<!-- section: coding-strategies -->

With independent codebooks, the decoder can use joint typicality decoding: look for the unique pair (codeword1, codeword2) jointly typical with Y^n.

:::recipe[Successive interference cancellation]{#sic}
1. Decode the stronger user first, treating the other user's signal as noise.
2. Subtract that user's contribution from the received signal.
3. Decode the weaker user on the resulting clean channel.
:::

SIC achieves the corner points of the capacity region; rate-splitting or time-sharing fills the rest. For more than two users, more sophisticated strategies (compute-forward, etc.) are studied.

:::intuition
Joint decoding: the listener considers all possible pairs of messages and picks the pair whose combined voices best match what was heard.

SIC: 'First understand the loud person, write it down, subtract that voice from the recording, then understand the quiet person on the cleaned-up tape.'
:::

:::example
Gaussian MAC with a strong and a weak user, $P_1 = 10$, $P_2 = 1$, noise $N = 1$; decode the strong user 1 first. Logs are base 2.
:::

:::solution
Decode user 1 treating user 2 as noise.** User 1's signal competes against $X_2$ plus thermal noise, an effective noise power of $P_2 + N = 2$. So $R_1 = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{P_1}{P_2+N}\big) = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{10}{2}\big) = \tfrac12\log 6 \approx 1.292$.
**Step 2 — Cancel and decode user 2.** Once $X_1^n$ is correctly decoded it is subtracted from $Y^n$, leaving user 2 alone against thermal noise only: $R_2 = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{P_2}{N}\big) = \tfrac12\log 2 = 0.5$.
**Step 3 — Check against the sum bound.** $R_1 + R_2 = 1.292 + 0.5 = 1.792$, and $I(X_1,X_2;Y) = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{P_1+P_2}{N}\big) = \tfrac12\log(1+11) = \tfrac12\log 12 \approx 1.792$. ✓ The two match, confirming SIC lands exactly on the sum-rate corner.
**Note on ordering:** decoding user 1 *first* is what forces user 1 to swallow user 2 as noise (hence the $P_2+N$ denominator), while the last-decoded user always enjoys the clean $\tfrac12\log(1+P_i/N)$ channel. This is why the corner reached depends on the order.
**Sanity check:** neither individual rate exceeds its genie-aided cap $\tfrac12\log(1+P_i/N)$ — user 1's cap is $\tfrac12\log 11 \approx 1.729$, and $1.292 < 1.729$; user 2 is at its cap $0.5$ because it was decoded last. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the MAC capacity region is always a rectangle**: Only when the users do not interfere (orthogonal channels) is it a rectangle; otherwise there is a sum-rate tradeoff.
- **Using the same code as for a single-user channel without accounting for interference**: Treating the other user purely as noise is suboptimal except at the corner points.
- **Confusing the MAC with the broadcast channel**: In MAC multiple senders → one receiver; in broadcast one sender → multiple receivers with possibly different channels.
- **Assuming independent codebooks are always optimal**: For some MACs (deterministic) correlation can help, but for the standard memoryless MAC independent inputs suffice for the capacity region.
- **Forgetting that the capacity region is for asymptotically long blocks**: For finite n there is a penalty and practical systems use HARQ, power control, and scheduling on top of the information-theoretic region.
- **Believing SIC always requires the stronger user to be decoded first in every realization**: In information theory we consider average power and typical sets; practical SIC adapts ordering or uses joint decoding for robustness.

A broadcast channel (BC) has a single transmitter sending one or more messages to several receivers. Each receiver observes its own (possibly different) noisy version of the transmitted signal. The goal is to characterize the set of rates at which the transmitter can send private messages to each receiver (and possibly a common message to all).

The broadcast channel is the dual of the multiple-access channel and introduces superposition and binning ideas central to network information theory.

## Broadcast Channel Model

<!-- section: bc-model -->

:::definition[Broadcast channel]{#bc}
One encoder maps messages ($W_0$ common, $W_1$ private to receiver 1, $W_2$ to receiver 2, and so on) into a single channel input $X^n$. Receiver $i$ observes $Y_i^n \sim p(y_i \mid x)$, where the marginal channels may differ, and must recover its intended messages. The capacity region is the set of achievable rate tuples $(R_0, R_1, R_2, \dots)$.
:::

The most important special case is the degraded broadcast channel in which the receivers can be ordered so that one is a stochastically degraded version of the other.

:::intuition
One radio tower broadcasting a signal; some listeners have good antennas (strong channel), others have poor reception (weak channel). The tower must send content that the good listeners can decode extra private parts while the weak listeners at least get the basic common program.

The physics of the channels to different listeners are different and usually nested (degraded).
:::

:::example
A scalar Gaussian broadcast channel: $Y_1 = X + Z_1$ and $Y_2 = X + Z_2$ with noise variances $N_1 = \mathrm{Var}(Z_1) = 1$ and $N_2 = \mathrm{Var}(Z_2) = 4$, so receiver 1 has the better channel. Total power constraint $\mathbb{E}[X^2] \le P = 10$. Logs are base 2.
:::

:::solution
Single-user ceilings.** If the transmitter dedicated *all* power to one receiver, receiver 1 could get up to $\tfrac12\log(1 + P/N_1) = \tfrac12\log 11 \approx 1.730$ bits, and receiver 2 up to $\tfrac12\log(1 + P/N_2) = \tfrac12\log(1 + 2.5) = \tfrac12\log 3.5 \approx 0.904$ bits.
**Step 2 — Read off the ordering.** Because $N_1 < N_2$, this channel is *degraded*: $Y_2$ is a noisier version of $Y_1$ (formally $Y_2 = Y_1 + Z'$ with $Z' \sim \mathcal N(0, N_2 - N_1)$ independent of $Y_1$). The strong receiver can always simulate whatever the weak one sees.
**Step 3 — Consequence.** Receiver 1 supports strictly higher rates ($1.730 > 0.904$), so any layered scheme should hand the *coarse* layer to the weak receiver 2 and the *fine* enhancement to the strong receiver 1 — exactly the superposition split developed in the next sections.
**Sanity check:** halving the noise from $N_2=4$ to $N_1=1$ roughly *doubles* the achievable rate ($0.904 \to 1.730$), consistent with the $\log(1+P/N)$ dependence on SNR. ✓
:::

## Degraded Broadcast Channel and Capacity Region

<!-- section: degraded-bc -->

:::definition[Degraded broadcast channel]{#degraded-bc}
A BC is (stochastically) *degraded* when the receivers can be ordered so that the Markov chain $X - Y_1 - Y_2$ holds: receiver 2's output is a further-corrupted version of receiver 1's, so anything $Y_2$ reveals, $Y_1$ reveals at least as well.
:::

Under this ordering the capacity region, with a common message and two private messages, is known and achieved by superposition. The construction introduces an auxiliary random variable $U$ — the 'cloud center' carrying the weak user's message — satisfying the Markov chain $U - X - (Y_1, Y_2)$. It is a bookkeeping variable, not transmitted directly, and its alphabet may be taken bounded ($|\mathcal U| \le |\mathcal X| + 2$) without loss.

:::theorem[Capacity region of the degraded broadcast channel]{#degraded-bc-region}
Taking $Y_2$ as the weaker receiver, the achievable rates are those satisfying

$$R_2 \le I(U; Y_2), \qquad R_1 \le I(X; Y_1 \mid U), \qquad R_0 + R_2 \le I(U; Y_2),$$

maximized over the joint law $p(u)\,p(x\mid u)$, equivalently $p(u,x)$.
:::

The common message shares the weak user's budget: that is what the third inequality records.

:::intuition
The weaker receiver can only 'see' the coarse cloud centers U; the stronger receiver can resolve the fine 'satellite' points X inside each cloud.

This gives the weaker user its message 'for free' from the cloud index while the strong user gets both its private message and the weak user's as side information.
:::

:::example
Continue the Gaussian BC with $N_1 = 1$ (strong receiver 1), $N_2 = 4$ (weak receiver 2), and total power $P = 10$. Split the power into $\alpha P$ for the weak/cloud layer and $(1-\alpha)P$ for the strong/satellite layer, choosing $\alpha = 0.5$. Logs are base 2.
:::

:::solution
Weak user's rate.** Receiver 2 decodes only the cloud, so the satellite power $(1-\alpha)P = 5$ acts as extra noise on top of $N_2 = 4$. Its cloud SNR is $\dfrac{\alpha P}{(1-\alpha)P + N_2} = \dfrac{5}{5 + 4} = \dfrac{5}{9}$, giving $R_2 \le \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac59\big) = \tfrac12\log\tfrac{14}{9} \approx 0.319$ bits.
**Step 2 — Strong user's rate.** Receiver 1 first decodes the cloud (it can, since its channel is better than receiver 2's), *subtracts* it, and then reads the satellite over noise $N_1 = 1$ only: $R_1 \le \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{(1-\alpha)P}{N_1}\big) = \tfrac12\log(1 + 5) = \tfrac12\log 6 \approx 1.292$ bits.
**Step 3 — Compare to naive splitting.** Time-sharing / orthogonal splitting would force the two rates to trade along a straight line between the single-user points; superposition instead lets receiver 1 keep a large rate ($1.292$) while receiver 2 still gets $0.319$ — the whole power is "reused" because the strong user cancels the weak layer rather than avoiding it.
**Step 4 — Sweep $\alpha$.** As $\alpha \to 1$, all power goes to the cloud: $R_2 \to \tfrac12\log(1 + 10/4) = \tfrac12\log 3.5 \approx 0.904$ (its single-user ceiling) while $R_1 \to 0$. As $\alpha \to 0$: $R_1 \to \tfrac12\log 11 \approx 1.730$, $R_2 \to 0$. The union over $\alpha \in [0,1]$ traces the boundary of the capacity region.
**Sanity check:** the endpoints of the sweep reproduce exactly the single-user ceilings computed in the BC model example ($1.730$ and $0.904$). ✓
:::

## Superposition Coding

<!-- section: superposition-coding -->

:::recipe[Superposition coding]{#superposition-coding}
1. Generate a coarse codebook for the weak user's message; its codewords are the cloud centers.
2. For each cloud center, generate a fine codebook of satellites for the strong user's message. The transmitted $X$ is the satellite point.
3. The weak receiver decodes only the cloud center, treating the satellites as noise.
4. The strong receiver first decodes the cloud center, using its better channel, subtracts it, and then decodes its own satellite.
:::

This is dual to SIC on the MAC and achieves every point in the capacity region of the degraded BC.

:::intuition
Imagine sending a 'base layer' that the poor receiver can just barely understand, plus 'enhancement layers' that only the good receiver can peel off on top of the base.

The enhancement is hidden inside the 'noise' that the weak receiver already has to tolerate.
:::

:::example
Same Gaussian BC as above ($N_1 = 1$, $N_2 = 4$, $P = 10$), now viewed from the *encoder's* construction with $\alpha = 0.5$. Write $X = U + V$ where $U$ (the base/cloud) has power $\alpha P = 5$ and $V$ (the enhancement/satellite) has power $(1-\alpha)P = 5$, chosen independently.
:::

:::solution
Build the codebooks.** Draw $2^{nR_2}$ cloud centers $U^n$ i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0, \alpha P)$. Around each cloud center draw $2^{nR_1}$ satellites by adding an independent $V^n \sim \mathcal N(0, (1-\alpha)P)$. The transmitted symbol is $X = U + V$, with total power $\alpha P + (1-\alpha)P = 10 = P$. ✓ (power constraint met with equality).
**Step 2 — Weak receiver decodes the base.** Receiver 2 sees $Y_2 = U + V + Z_2$. It treats $V + Z_2$ (variance $5 + 4 = 9$) as noise and decodes $U$, so it can support $R_2 = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{5}{9}\big) \approx 0.319$ bits — identical to the previous section.
**Step 3 — Strong receiver peels the base, then reads the enhancement.** Receiver 1 sees $Y_1 = U + V + Z_1$ with $Z_1$ of variance $1$. Its channel is cleaner than receiver 2's, so it can decode $U$ *at least* as reliably, subtract $\hat U$, and be left with $V + Z_1$ (variance $5 + 1 = 6$ before cancellation, but the useful signal is $V$ over noise $N_1 = 1$). Its enhancement rate is $R_1 = \tfrac12\log\!\big(1 + \tfrac{5}{1}\big) = \tfrac12\log 6 \approx 1.292$ bits.
**Step 4 — Why the strong user can always decode the base.** Degradedness guarantees receiver 1's SNR for the cloud, $\tfrac{\alpha P}{(1-\alpha)P + N_1} = \tfrac{5}{6}$, exceeds receiver 2's cloud SNR $\tfrac{5}{9}$. Since $\tfrac56 > \tfrac59$, whatever rate the weak user can decode, the strong user decodes too. This is the crucial condition that makes cancellation valid.
**Sanity check:** the achieved pair $(R_1, R_2) = (1.292,\, 0.319)$ matches the degraded-BC computation exactly, confirming superposition and the region description agree. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming the broadcast capacity region is always the intersection of the individual point-to-point capacities**: That would be too pessimistic; superposition lets the strong user get extra rate 'on top' without hurting the weak user as much as orthogonal allocation would.
- **Confusing superposition with time-sharing**: Time-sharing is a simple inner bound; superposition strictly enlarges the region for non-degraded or even some degraded channels.
- **Thinking the same codebook is sent to everyone**: The encoding uses auxiliary variables; the actual X carries a combination of messages in a structured way.
- **Forgetting the common message**: Many treatments focus on private messages only; adding a common message R0 changes the bounds and is important for video multicast etc.
- **Believing all broadcast channels have known capacity regions**: Only degraded (and a few other classes such as more capable, less noisy) BCs have single-letter capacity regions; the general BC capacity is still open.
- **Ignoring that practical broadcast systems use layered video + unequal error protection rather than pure information-theoretic superposition**: The principle is the same, but implementation uses scalable codecs and different modulation/coding per layer.

In traditional packet networks, intermediate routers only store and forward packets. Network coding allows (or requires) routers to compute linear combinations (or other functions) of the packets they receive and forward the combinations. For multicast traffic this can achieve higher throughput than routing alone; the classic gain is demonstrated by the butterfly network.

Network coding sits at the intersection of information theory, graph theory, and algebra; random linear network coding makes it practical in large networks.

## Store-and-Forward vs Network Coding

<!-- section: store-forward-vs-nc -->

Under pure routing, each edge carries a copy of some source packet (or a duplicate). The max-flow min-cut theorem gives the maximum rate from one source to one sink, but for multiple sinks the routing rate may be strictly less than the cut value.

:::definition[Network coding]{#network-coding}
Under network coding, an edge may carry a function — typically a linear combination over a finite field — of several packets, rather than a copy of one of them.
:::

This 'mixing' lets information flow more efficiently to multiple destinations that need different subsets of the data.

:::intuition
Routing is like delivering whole letters or whole boxes; each truck on a road carries one intact box.

Network coding is like allowing the post office to open the boxes, mix the contents (say take the XOR of two letters), and send the mixture onward. The recipients, having side information, can still recover everything.
:::

:::example
Take the setup where two packets $a$ and $b$ must each reach two sinks $T_1$ and $T_2$, and every route from a source to the "far" sink is forced through a single shared bottleneck edge of capacity $1$ packet per use.
:::

:::solution
What routing can do.** The bottleneck can carry only one packet per use. To route both $a$ and $b$ across it takes *two* uses, so in a single use routing delivers at most $1$ packet of "cross" traffic. One sink ends up missing its far packet, capping the achievable rate.
**Step 2 — What coding can do.** Send the single combined packet $a \oplus b$ (bitwise XOR over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$) across the bottleneck. It still occupies exactly $1$ unit of capacity — no more bits than a plain packet.
**Step 3 — Recovery at each sink.** $T_1$ already holds $a$ from a direct path; it computes $b = a \oplus (a \oplus b)$. $T_2$ already holds $b$; it computes $a = b \oplus (a \oplus b)$. Both sinks now have both packets.
**Step 4 — Count the gain.** Routing delivered $1$ useful packet-worth of cross traffic per bottleneck use; coding delivered $2$ (one to each sink) for the same $1$ unit of capacity — a factor-of-2 throughput gain on the bottleneck.
**Sanity check:** $a \oplus (a \oplus b) = (a \oplus a) \oplus b = 0 \oplus b = b$. ✓ The XOR is its own inverse, which is exactly why one coded transmission serves both recipients.
:::

## The Butterfly Network Example

<!-- section: butterfly -->

The canonical butterfly: sources S1 and S2 each produce one packet (say bits a and b). Two edge-disjoint paths from S1 to T1 and from S2 to T2, plus a cross path through a central node C that has only unit capacity to both T1 and T2.

With routing, C can forward either a or b but not both in one use; at least one sink gets only rate 1/2 or one message is lost.

With network coding, C forwards a+b (over GF(2)). T1 receives a (direct) and a+b, recovers b = a + (a+b). T2 symmetrically recovers a. Both sinks get both packets at rate 1 each; the bottleneck carried 'one packet worth' of information that served both.

:::intuition
The middle link sends a 'mixture' a+b. The guy who already has a can subtract to get b; the guy who has b can subtract to get a.

One transmission on the scarce middle link satisfies the missing piece for two different people.
:::

:::example
Packets $a$ from $S_1$, $b$ from $S_2$; the middle node $C$ computes and sends $a \oplus b$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$.
:::

:::solution
Direct paths.** $T_1$ receives $a$ over its dedicated direct edge; $T_2$ receives $b$ over its direct edge.
**Step 2 — Coded path.** $C$ receives both $a$ and $b$, forms $a \oplus b$, and forwards this single packet down the shared branch that reaches *both* sinks.
**Step 3 — Decode.** $T_1$: $a \oplus (a \oplus b) = b$. $T_2$: $b \oplus (a \oplus b) = a$. Each sink now has $\{a,b\}$, i.e. rate $1$ for $a$ and rate $1$ for $b$.
**Step 4 — Edge accounting.** Number the unit-capacity edges: $S_1\!\to\!C$, $S_2\!\to\!C$, the coded edge $C\!\to\!(T_1,T_2)$, and the two direct edges $S_1\!\to\!T_1$, $S_2\!\to\!T_2$ — five edge-uses total, each carrying one packet. The min-cut to each sink is $2$, and coding achieves rate $2$ (both packets) at *every* sink simultaneously, which pure routing cannot on this topology.
**Sanity check:** count what each sink knows — $T_1$ holds $\{a, a\oplus b\}$, a full-rank pair over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, so it inverts to $\{a,b\}$; symmetrically for $T_2$. ✓
:::

## Linear Network Coding and Throughput

<!-- section: linear-nc -->

:::theorem[Multicast capacity via linear network coding]{#multicast-capacity}
For multicast — one source and many sinks *all wanting the same data* — the achievable rate is exactly the minimum over sinks of that sink's individual max-flow from the source. Linear network coding over a sufficiently large field achieves this common min-cut rate to every sink simultaneously, something routing cannot always do.
:::

The clean "min of max-flows" characterization is special to single-source multicast; general multi-source or multi-sink demands need not have a simple cut-based capacity.

Each edge carries a linear combination of the source packets; the coefficients can be chosen centrally (algebraic NC) or randomly (random linear NC, with high probability invertible at the sinks when field is large).

Decoding at a sink reduces to solving a linear system whose matrix is the product of the local coding matrices along the paths; the sink needs the matrix to be full rank.

:::intuition
Each packet on the wire is a 'recipe' — a vector of coefficients telling how much of each original source packet is mixed in.

At the destination you collect enough independent recipes; linear algebra (Gaussian elimination) recovers the original ingredients.
:::

:::example[(small, over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$):]
Source has $k = 2$ packets $a, b$. A sink collects two coded packets whose global encoding vectors are $v_1 = (1,1)$ (payload $a \oplus b$) and $v_2 = (0,1)$ (payload $b$).
:::

:::solution
Form the decoding matrix.** Stack the vectors as rows: $G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and the received payloads as $\begin{pmatrix} a\oplus b \\ b \end{pmatrix} = G \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}$.
**Step 2 — Check invertibility.** $\det G = (1)(1) - (1)(0) = 1 \ne 0$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, so $G$ is full rank and invertible.
**Step 3 — Invert.** Over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, $G^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (self-inverse here). Then $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix} = G^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} a\oplus b \\ b \end{pmatrix}$, i.e. $b = b$ and $a = (a\oplus b)\oplus b = a$. ✓
**Step 4 — Sanity check.** $G G^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ (recall $1+1 = 0$), confirming the inverse.
:::

:::example[(why a large field helps):]
Now a generation of $k = 8$ source packets over $\mathrm{GF}(256)$ with random coefficients. The sink can decode iff the $8$ collected global vectors are linearly independent in $\mathbb{F}_q^k$ with $q = 256$.
:::

:::solution
Count independent choices.** The first vector must be nonzero: probability $\dfrac{q^k - 1}{q^k}$. The $i$-th vector must avoid the span of the previous $i-1$ (a subspace of size $q^{i-1}$): probability $\dfrac{q^k - q^{i-1}}{q^k}$.
**Step 2 — Multiply.** $\Pr[\text{full rank}] = \displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(1 - q^{\,i-k}\Big) = \prod_{i=0}^{7}\big(1 - 256^{\,i-8}\big)$.
**Step 3 — Evaluate.** The dominant loss term is the last factor $1 - 256^{-1} \approx 0.99609$; the product over all eight factors is $\approx 0.99608$, so failure probability $\approx 0.0039$, well under $1\%$.
**Sanity check / scaling:** the failure probability is dominated by $q^{-(q-k+1)} \approx q^{-1} = 1/256$; pushing to $\mathrm{GF}(2^{16})$ would drop it to $\approx 1/65536$. This is the precise sense in which "a large enough field" makes random linear network coding succeed with probability $\to 1$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking network coding always doubles throughput**: The gain is topology-dependent; in some networks (trees) coding gives no gain over routing; the butterfly is the smallest example with gain 2.
- **Confusing network coding with source or channel coding**: Network coding operates on packets at intermediate nodes; it is orthogonal and can be composed with end-to-end source/channel coding.
- **Assuming the field size can be arbitrarily small**: For linear NC to achieve capacity, the field must be large enough relative to the number of sinks or edges; GF(2) sometimes works but not always.
- **Forgetting header overhead for coefficients**: In random linear NC the coding vector (coefficients) must be sent with the packet or known by other means; this overhead vanishes asymptotically but matters for small generations.
- **Believing coding is only for multicast**: While the big theorem is for multicast, network coding ideas (including index coding, functional compression, compute-forward) apply to many other traffic patterns and have practical uses in wireless (COPE, MORE) and distributed storage.
- **Ignoring that practical deployments use generation-based or sliding-window NC rather than the block model of the theorems**: Real systems chunk data into generations to keep matrices small and decoding delay low.

Common randomness refers to the problem of generating or extracting identical random bits that are shared among two or more parties, often when the parties observe correlated but noisy versions of some source or communicate over noisy channels. The extracted bits should be nearly uniform and independent of any eavesdropper's view (when privacy is required).

This area sits at the intersection of information theory, cryptography, and communication complexity. It underlies secret-key agreement, privacy amplification, randomness extraction, and certain physical-layer security schemes.

## The Common Randomness Generation Problem

<!-- section: cr-problem -->

Two parties, Alice and Bob, observe correlated random variables X and Y (e.g., repeated independent draws from a joint distribution p(x,y), or the inputs and outputs of a noisy channel). They may also have access to a public (authenticated but eavesdropped) communication channel.

:::definition[Common randomness generation]{#common-randomness}
The goal is to output identical strings $K_A$ and $K_B$ that are (almost) uniformly random and (almost) independent of any side information $Z$ that an eavesdropper may possess.
:::

:::theorem[Common-randomness rate with free communication]{#cr-rate}
When communication is free and unlimited, the maximum rate of common randomness that can be generated is the mutual information

$$I(X;Y),$$

or more precisely the supremum of $I(U;V)$ over auxiliaries with the right Markov chains.
:::

With rate-limited communication the problem becomes a rate region instead of a single number.

:::intuition
Alice and Bob each see a noisy version of the same underlying 'coin flips' or 'fading coefficients.' By talking over a public line they can reconcile their views and distill a shorter string of bits that both hold exactly and that an eavesdropper who saw the conversation and maybe one of the noisy observations still cannot predict.

The distilled bits can be used as a shared secret key or as a source of shared randomness for coordination.
:::

:::example
Alice holds $X$, uniform on $\{0,1\}$, and Bob holds $Y = X \oplus N$ where $N \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ with $p = 0.1$ — i.e. their observations pass through a BSC with crossover $0.1$. All logs base 2, and $H_2(p) = -p\log p - (1-p)\log(1-p)$.
:::

:::solution
How much correlation is there?** The common-randomness rate with free public discussion is $I(X;Y)$. Since $X$ is uniform, $Y$ is uniform too, so $H(Y) = 1$ bit, and $H(Y\mid X) = H_2(p) = H_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits (given $X$, the only uncertainty in $Y$ is the noise bit).
**Step 2 — Mutual information.** $I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits per observation.
**Step 3 — Achieving it.** Alice sends Bob just enough about $X$ to let him correct the $\approx 10\%$ of positions where $X$ and $Y$ differ. That reconciliation costs $H(X\mid Y) = H_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits per symbol (a Slepian–Wolf/syndrome description), after which both hold $X$. The *net* common randomness is $H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 = I(X;Y)$.
**Sanity check:** as $p \to 0$ the channels agree, $H_2(p)\to 0$, and the rate $\to 1$ bit — they share a whole fair coin per symbol; as $p \to 0.5$ the observations become independent, $H_2(p)\to 1$, and the rate $\to 0$ — nothing to share. ✓
:::

## Secret Key Agreement from Common Randomness

<!-- section: key-agreement -->

When an eavesdropper Eve observes Z correlated with (X,Y), the goal is often secret key agreement: produce K that is uniform, identical at Alice and Bob, and I(K; communication, Z) ≈ 0 (Eve learns essentially nothing about K).

The secret-key rate is bounded by the mutual information quantities

$$I(X;Y) - I(X;Z),$$

or by more refined expressions involving auxiliary variables (Csiszár–Körner).

:::theorem[Secrecy capacity of the wiretap channel]{#secrecy-capacity}
For the wiretap channel, with no public discussion, the secrecy capacity is

$$\max_{U - X - (Y,Z)} \bigl[\, I(U;Y) - I(U;Z) \,\bigr].$$
:::

:::recipe[Distilling a secret key]{#key-distillation}
1. **Reconciliation**: error-correct so that Alice's and Bob's strings become identical.
2. **Privacy amplification**: hash the common string down to remove Eve's information.
:::

:::intuition
Alice and Bob start with some 'raw' shared uncertainty (I(X;Y)) that Eve also partially sees (I(X;Z)). They first correct the differences in their views (reconciliation), then hash the corrected string down to a shorter key whose bits are unpredictable to Eve (privacy amplification).

The net rate of secure key they can distill is limited by how much 'advantage' they have over Eve: the mutual information gap.
:::

:::example
Binary source: Alice sees $X$ uniform; Bob sees $Y = X \oplus N_1$ with $N_1 \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.1)$; Eve sees $Z = X \oplus N_2$ with $N_2 \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.25)$, all noises independent. Logs base 2, $H_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$, $H_2(0.25) \approx 0.811$.
:::

:::solution
Alice–Bob correlation.** $I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(0.1) \approx 0.531$ bits, as in the previous example.
**Step 2 — Alice–Eve leakage.** $I(X;Z) = 1 - H_2(0.25) = 1 - 0.811 \approx 0.189$ bits — Eve's noisier channel means she learns less about $X$.
**Step 3 — Secret-key rate.** The one-way lower bound (advantage of the legitimate pair over Eve) is $I(X;Y) - I(X;Z) \approx 0.531 - 0.189 = 0.342$ secret bits per symbol.
**Step 4 — Two-phase protocol.** *Reconciliation:* Alice sends a syndrome of rate $H(X\mid Y) \approx 0.469$ so Bob recovers $X$; they now share $W = X$. *Privacy amplification:* they hash $W$ down, discarding roughly the $I(X;Z) \approx 0.189$ bits Eve could infer plus the reconciliation leakage, keeping $\approx 0.342$ bits per symbol that are $\varepsilon$-close to uniform and independent of $(Z, \text{transcript})$.
**Sanity check:** the rate is positive precisely because Bob's channel is *less* noisy than Eve's ($0.1 < 0.25$). If Eve's channel matched Bob's ($p_2 = 0.1$), the gap would be $0$ and no secret key could be distilled by this one-way scheme. ✓
:::

## Randomness Extraction and Privacy Amplification

<!-- section: extraction-amplification -->

:::definition[Randomness extractor]{#extractor}
A randomness extractor is a function $\mathrm{Ext}(X, S)$, with $S$ a short random seed, such that when $X$ has sufficient min-entropy, $\mathrm{Ext}(X,S)$ is close to uniform even conditioned on $S$.
:::

Strong extractors (output independent of the seed) are used for privacy amplification: after reconciliation Alice and Bob apply the same extractor (or a randomness condenser followed by hashing) to their common string using a short public random seed; the output is uniform and independent of Eve's entire view (including the seed).

:::theorem[Leftover hash lemma]{#leftover-hash}
Suppose $W$ has min-entropy $k$, that is

$$H_\infty(W) = -\log_2 \max_w \Pr[W=w] \ge k,$$

and a universal hash is applied to produce an $m$-bit output with

$$m \le k - 2\log(1/\varepsilon).$$

Then the output is $\varepsilon$-close to uniform even given the seed and Eve's side information; equivalently the statistical distance obeys $\varepsilon \le 2^{-(k-m)/2}$.
:::

Note that the hypothesis is on the *worst-case* uncertainty from Eve's viewpoint, which is what matters here — not the average-case Shannon entropy.

:::intuition
After Alice and Bob have made their strings identical (but Eve still has some partial information), they both run the same public hash function on the string. If the string had enough uncertainty left, the hash output looks like fresh random bits to everyone, including Eve.

The seed for the hash can be sent in the clear; strong extractors guarantee that the output is uniform even given the seed.
:::

:::example
$W$ is a 256-bit string with min-entropy at least $k = 200$ bits from Eve's perspective (she has partial side information but cannot pin $W$ down better than $2^{-200}$). Alice and Bob apply a universal hash with a public seed to produce an $m$-bit key.
:::

:::solution
Choose the output length.** The leftover-hash lemma requires $m \le k - 2\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$. Target $\varepsilon = 2^{-20}$, so the "entropy tax" is $2\log_2(1/\varepsilon) = 2 \times 20 = 40$ bits.
**Step 2 — Solve for $m$.** $m \le 200 - 40 = 160$ bits. Outputting a $128$-bit key ($m = 128$) is comfortably within budget.
**Step 3 — Bound the leakage.** With $m = 128$ and $k = 200$, the achieved distance is $\varepsilon \le 2^{-(k-m)/2} = 2^{-(200-128)/2} = 2^{-36}$ — far better than the $2^{-20}$ target, because we left $160 - 128 = 32$ bits of headroom.
**Step 4 — Publicity of the seed.** Because a *strong* extractor / universal hash is used, the output stays uniform even when the seed $S$ is revealed, so $S$ can be sent in the clear over the public channel.
**Sanity check:** with the maximal $m = 160$ the distance is exactly $2^{-(200-160)/2} = 2^{-20}$, hitting the target with zero margin — confirming $160$ is the largest safe key length and $128$ is conservative. ✓
:::

## Applications: Key Distribution, Correlation, and Communication

<!-- section: applications-cr -->

Common-randomness primitives are used for:
- Information-theoretic secret-key agreement over noisy channels or quantum channels (quantum key distribution can be viewed as a form of common-randomness distillation).
- Coordinated actions without communication: two parties that share common randomness can choose the same random codebook, the same time slot, the same hash function, etc.
- Wireless physical-layer security, localization, and authentication (shared randomness from channel reciprocity).
- Distributed simulation and coordination complexity: the amount of common randomness needed to simulate a joint distribution with limited communication.
- Explicit constructions of extractors, condensers, and Ramsey graphs from common-randomness ideas.

In the asymptotic regime the rates are characterized by mutual information quantities; for finite block length one uses smooth min-entropy, one-shot bounds, and second-order analysis.

:::intuition
Two wireless devices that see the same fading channel can turn the reciprocity into shared secret bits (after reconciliation and privacy amplification) that they use to encrypt their traffic — without ever sending the key over the air.

Once they have shared randomness they can 'flip a shared coin' to decide who transmits first, which frequency to hop to, or which code to use, all without further messages.
:::

:::example
A wireless pairing protocol. Alice and Bob each send a probe; by *reciprocity* both measure (nearly) the same channel coefficient $h$, plus independent local noise. Eve, at a different location more than half a wavelength away, sees an essentially decorrelated coefficient.
:::

:::solution
Harvest.** Over one coherence block each device quantizes its noisy $h$ estimate into, say, $3$ bits. Reciprocity makes Alice's and Bob's $3$-bit words agree with high probability; Eve's are near-independent, so her min-entropy about the joint word is close to the full $3$ bits.
**Step 2 — Accumulate.** Repeat over $N = 500$ independent coherence blocks. Suppose after reconciliation the shared string $W$ retains a conservative min-entropy of $k \approx 0.4$ bits per block $\times\ 500 = 200$ bits from Eve's viewpoint.
**Step 3 — Distill the key.** Apply privacy amplification (leftover-hash lemma) with target $\varepsilon = 2^{-20}$: allowed output $m \le k - 2\log_2(1/\varepsilon) = 200 - 40 = 160$ bits. Output a $128$-bit key — safely under the $160$-bit ceiling, with $2^{-36}$ leakage.
**Step 4 — Security claim.** The resulting AES-128 key is *information-theoretically* secured against Eve's noisy channel observations: no computational assumption is used, only her lack of correlation with $h$ and the min-entropy budget.
**Sanity check:** the number of usable key bits scales linearly with the number of independent probes ($200$ bits of entropy needed $\Rightarrow \sim 500$ blocks at $0.4$ bit/block), so a longer probing phase buys a longer key — the expected behaviour of an entropy-limited resource. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Equating common randomness with secret-key agreement**: Common randomness can be generated even when there is no secrecy requirement (just identical uniform bits); secrecy adds the privacy-amplification step and changes the achievable rates.
- **Forgetting reconciliation**: Correlated observations X and Y are almost never identical; an error-correction / reconciliation phase (often using Slepian-Wolf or LDPC codes on the virtual correlation channel) is required before extraction.
- **Thinking that sending the raw observations over the public channel always works**: It does give common randomness at rate H(X|Y), but it may leak everything to an eavesdropper; privacy amplification is still needed if secrecy is required.
- **Confusing min-entropy with Shannon entropy in one-shot extraction**: Extractors are analyzed with min-entropy (worst-case); Shannon entropy gives only asymptotic typical-set statements.
- **Assuming that common randomness always requires interaction**: In some models (e.g., channel reciprocity with no active adversary) the correlation itself may be sufficient with one-way or even zero communication after the initial observations.
- **Overlooking that common randomness is a resource that can be consumed**: Once Alice and Bob use up their shared bits for a key or for coordination, they may need to distill more from a fresh batch of correlated observations.

The Blahut–Arimoto (BA) algorithm is a practical iterative method for numerically computing the rate-distortion function R(D) for a given source and distortion measure, and a closely related algorithm computes the channel capacity C of a DMC. It was developed independently by Blahut and Arimoto in the early 1970s and remains the standard tool when no closed form is available.

Because R(D) and C are defined by convex optimizations, alternating optimization between the variables yields a convergent procedure whose fixed point satisfies the necessary KKT conditions for optimality.

## The Blahut-Arimoto Algorithm for R(D)

<!-- section: ba-for-rd -->

To compute $R(D)$ we alternate between two steps, for a fixed Lagrange multiplier $s < 0$ or directly for a target $D$.

:::recipe[Blahut–Arimoto iteration for R(D)]{#ba-rd}
1. Given the current output distribution $p(\hat x)$, update the test channel to $p(\hat x \mid x) \propto p(\hat x)\, \exp(s\, d(x,\hat x))$, normalized over $\hat x$ for each $x$.
2. Given the test channel, update the output marginal to the one it induces, $p(\hat x) = \sum_x p(x)\, p(\hat x \mid x)$.
3. Repeat until the distributions stabilize, then read off $D$ and $R$.
:::

Iterating these two steps monotonically decreases the Lagrangian and converges to the minimizing test channel for that $s$.

:::intuition
Start with a guess at what the reproductions 'look like' on average (p(\hat x)).

Update the 'best way to map originals to reproductions' given that guess, then update the guess to match what the mapping actually produces. Repeat.

The math is just re-normalizing exponentials of the (negative) distortion; it is a soft version of 'assign to the closest reproduction'.
:::

:::example
Binary source with $\Pr[X=1] = p = 0.1$ and Hamming distortion $d(x,\hat x) = \mathbb 1[x \ne \hat x]$. We target $D = 0.05$ and check BA against the known closed form $R(D) = H_2(p) - H_2(D)$ (valid for $0 \le D \le p \le \tfrac12$). Logs base 2.
:::

:::solution
Closed-form target.** $H_2(0.1) = -0.1\log 0.1 - 0.9\log 0.9 \approx 0.469$ and $H_2(0.05) \approx 0.286$, so $R(0.05) = 0.469 - 0.286 \approx 0.183$ bits.
**Step 2 — What BA converges to.** For this source the optimal test channel is a BSC with crossover $D = 0.05$: $p(\hat x \mid x)$ flips the symbol with probability $0.05$. The BA iteration, updating $p(\hat x \mid x) \propto p(\hat x)\exp(s\,d(x,\hat x))$ then re-inducing $p(\hat x)$, drives the effective crossover to $0.05$ for the $s$ that yields $\mathbb E[d] = 0.05$.
**Step 3 — Read off the rate.** At the fixed point $R = I(X;\hat X)$ evaluated at that test channel equals $H_2(0.1) - H_2(0.05) \approx 0.183$ bits, matching Step 1.
**Step 4 — Check the slope.** The Lagrange multiplier at convergence should equal $s = \dfrac{dR}{dD}$. Numerically $\dfrac{d}{dD}\big[H_2(p) - H_2(D)\big] = -\dfrac{d}{dD}H_2(D) = -\log_2\dfrac{1-D}{D} = -\log_2\dfrac{0.95}{0.05} = -\log_2 19 \approx -4.25$, so BA reports $s \approx -4.25$ at $D = 0.05$ (negative, as required for a rate–distortion tradeoff).
**Sanity check:** at $D = p = 0.1$ the formula gives $R = H_2(0.1) - H_2(0.1) = 0$ — you can achieve zero rate by always outputting $\hat x = 0$, incurring distortion exactly $p = 0.1$. ✓
:::

## The Algorithm for Channel Capacity

<!-- section: ba-for-capacity -->

For capacity of a DMC with fixed forward channel $p(y\mid x)$, the Arimoto–Blahut iteration alternates two closed-form steps. Given the current input distribution $p(x)$:

- **Compute the induced output** $p(y) = \sum_x p(x)\,p(y\mid x)$.
- **Update the input** by reweighting each symbol by how "informative" it is, measured by the KL divergence between its output row and the average output:
$$p'(x) = \frac{p(x)\,\exp\!\big(D\big(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)\big)\big)}{\sum_{x'} p(x')\,\exp\!\big(D\big(p(y\mid x')\,\|\,p(y)\big)\big)},$$
where $D(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)) = \sum_y p(y\mid x)\log\frac{p(y\mid x)}{p(y)}$ is exactly $I(x;Y)$, the mutual information contributed by input symbol $x$.

Symbols whose output row stands out from the average (large divergence) get boosted; symbols that look like the crowd get suppressed. The normalization keeps $p'(x)$ a valid pmf.

:::proposition[Fixed point of the capacity iteration]{#ba-kkt}
At the fixed point the KKT conditions hold: every symbol with $p(x) > 0$ has the *same* value $I(x;Y) = C$, and every unused symbol has $I(x;Y) \le C$.
:::

Inputs with lower $I(x;Y)$ are therefore driven to probability zero — the iteration prunes the alphabet as it goes.

:::intuition
Guess a distribution on what you send.

Compute what comes out and how much information each possible input 'explains' the output.

Reweight your guess toward the inputs that carried more information. Repeat until the distribution stabilizes on the best inputs.
:::

:::example
The **Z-channel**: input $0$ is always received as $0$; input $1$ is flipped to $0$ with probability $e = 0.5$ and received correctly with probability $1-e = 0.5$. So $p(0\mid 0) = 1$, $p(0\mid 1) = 0.5$, $p(1\mid 1) = 0.5$. Logs base 2. Write $a = \Pr[X=1]$.
:::

:::solution
Induced output.** $\Pr[Y=1] = a(1-e) = 0.5a$ and $\Pr[Y=0] = 1 - 0.5a$. Conditional output entropy: $H(Y\mid X) = (1-a)\cdot 0 + a\cdot H_2(e) = a\,H_2(0.5) = a$ (since only $X=1$ injects noise, and $H_2(0.5)=1$).
**Step 2 — Mutual information.** $I(a) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X) = H_2(0.5a) - a$.
**Step 3 — Optimize (what BA converges to).** Setting $I'(a) = 0$ gives the closed form $a^\star = \dfrac{1}{(1-e)\big(1 + 2^{H_2(e)/(1-e)}\big)} = \dfrac{1}{0.5\,(1 + 2^{2})} = \dfrac{1}{0.5\cdot 5} = 0.4$. Note $a^\star = 0.4 \ne 0.5$ — the asymmetry pushes the optimal input off uniform, and BA discovers this automatically.
**Step 4 — Capacity.** With $a^\star = 0.4$: $\Pr[Y=1] = 0.2$, so $I = H_2(0.2) - 0.4 = 0.7219 - 0.4 \approx 0.322$ bits.
**Step 5 — Verify the KKT fixed point.** At $a^\star$, each supported input must satisfy $I(x;Y) = D(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)) = C$. For $x=0$: $D = 1\cdot\log\frac{1}{0.8} \approx 0.322$. For $x=1$: $D = 0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.8} + 0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.2} \approx 0.322$. Both equal $C \approx 0.322$. ✓ This equality is exactly the fixed-point condition that stops the BA iteration.
:::

## Convergence and Practical Use

<!-- section: convergence-use -->

Under mild conditions the iterations converge to the global optimum because of convexity. In practice one stops when the change in R (or C) or in the distributions falls below a tolerance. The algorithm can be implemented with careful normalization to avoid under/overflow (use log domain).

Modern variants use accelerated methods, stochastic approximations for very large alphabets, or combine with neural nets for continuous/high-dimensional sources. The same alternating-minimization idea underlies the EM algorithm and many other procedures in information theory and machine learning (e.g., expectation propagation).

:::intuition
Because the math problem is 'bowl-shaped' (convex), no matter where you start the iteration rolls downhill to the same bottom.

In code you just keep updating two arrays (the conditional and the marginal) until they stop changing much; the final mutual information is your answer.
:::

:::example
Estimate the cost of computing $R(D)$ for a $256$-ary source (e.g. an 8-bit image pixel histogram) with $256$ reproduction levels and a chosen $256 \times 256$ distortion matrix.
:::

:::solution
Cost per iteration.** Each BA iteration does two passes over the $256 \times 256$ conditional array: one to update $p(\hat x\mid x) \propto p(\hat x)\exp(s\,d(x,\hat x))$ (with a row-normalization), one to re-induce $p(\hat x) = \sum_x p(x)\,p(\hat x\mid x)$. That is $\Theta(256^2) = \Theta(65{,}536)$ multiply-adds per iteration — microseconds on a laptop.
**Step 2 — Iteration count.** BA converges linearly; for small-to-moderate alphabets reaching a change below $10^{-3}$ bits typically takes tens to a few hundred iterations (consistent with the $|X|=|Y|=4$ rule of thumb). Say $\sim 200$ iterations here.
**Step 3 — Total work.** $200 \times 65{,}536 \approx 1.3\times 10^{7}$ operations for one point on the curve — a fraction of a second. Sweeping $\sim 50$ values of $s$ to trace the whole $R(D)$ curve is still well under a minute.
**Step 4 — Numerical hygiene.** Compute $\exp(s\,d)$ in the log domain and subtract the row max before exponentiating (a log-sum-exp), so that with $s \ll 0$ and large distortions the exponentials do not underflow to $0$.
**Sanity check:** verify $s = dR/dD$ at each converged point by finite-differencing neighbouring $(R,D)$ pairs; agreement with the returned $s$ confirms the iteration reached a genuine stationary point rather than stopping early. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Running the algorithm for a fixed D instead of using the s-parameter**: Direct fixed-D versions exist but the Lagrangian form is numerically more stable and naturally traces the whole curve.
- **Forgetting to normalize the test-channel updates**: The proportionality must become a true conditional pmf for each x; missing normalization ruins the I calculation.
- **Assuming convergence from any initialization to the global optimum without checking**: While convexity guarantees it for the exact BA, floating-point or approximate versions can need multiple random starts for large problems.
- **Confusing the BA algorithm with actual code construction**: BA gives the information-theoretic limit R(D) or C; it does not directly produce the encoder/decoder codebooks (though it can guide quantizer design).
- **Using it on continuous alphabets without discretization**: The algorithm is for finite alphabets; continuous problems require discretization, kernel methods, or parametric assumptions.
- **Ignoring that s = dR/dD at the solution**: After convergence you can read the slope from the final s to verify consistency with the computed (R,D) point.
