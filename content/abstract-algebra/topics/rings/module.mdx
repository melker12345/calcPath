# Rings

Rings generalize the arithmetic of integers and polynomials. They have two operations — addition and multiplication — that interact via the distributive laws. Addition always forms an abelian group; multiplication is associative and distributes over addition, but need not have inverses or even an identity.

Rings are the setting in which we study factorization, ideals, and modules. Many of the deepest results in algebra (unique factorization, Hilbert's basis theorem, etc.) live in ring theory.

This topic covers the axioms, basic examples, units, zero-divisors, and subrings.

## Ring axioms and definition {#definition}

Rings capture the essential algebraic properties of familiar number systems (and many others) using just two operations. To ensure addition and multiplication "play nicely" together — so that we can expand, factor, and manipulate expressions reliably — we impose a short list of axioms. These are the minimal requirements; many extra properties (such as commutativity or inverses) may or may not hold.

:::definition[Ring]{#ring-definition}
A **ring** $(R, +, \cdot)$ is a set $R$ with two binary operations satisfying:

1. $(R, +)$ is an abelian group.
2. Multiplication is associative: $(ab)c = a(bc)$.
3. Distributivity: $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ and $(b+c)a = ba + ca$.
:::

:::definition[Ring with 1, commutative ring]
A ring with **multiplicative identity** (or unity) 1 such that $1r = r1 = r$ for all r is called a **ring with 1** (or unital ring). A ring is **commutative** if $ab=ba$ for all a,b.
:::

We usually assume rings have 1 unless stated otherwise.

:::intuition
Think of a ring as "numbers you can add and multiply" but the multiplication might not let you divide. Addition is always nice and reversible; multiplication may have zero divisors or lack units.

The distributive law is what makes multiplication "compatible" with addition — the heart of algebra.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a commutative ring with 1]
We check every axiom against the usual $+$ and $\cdot$.
:::

:::solution
**$(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is an abelian group.** Addition is closed ($a+b \in \mathbb{Z}$), associative, and commutative; the identity is $0$; the inverse of $a$ is $-a$. ✓
**Step 2 — Multiplication is associative.** $(ab)c = a(bc)$ for all integers (a standard property of integer multiplication). ✓
**Step 3 — Distributivity.** $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ and $(b+c)a = ba + ca$; both hold, so multiplication is compatible with addition. ✓
**Step 4 — Extra structure.** Multiplication is commutative ($ab = ba$) and has the identity $1$, so $\mathbb{Z}$ is a **commutative ring with 1**.

**Sanity check:** try one instance of distributivity, $3(4+5) = 3\cdot 9 = 27$ and $3\cdot 4 + 3\cdot 5 = 12 + 15 = 27$. ✓ The two sides agree, as the axiom demands.
:::

## Important examples of rings {#examples}

- $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ — commutative rings with 1.
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — commutative ring with 1 (not a domain if n composite).
- $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ — matrix rings, non-commutative, with 1 (the identity matrix).
- Polynomial ring $R[x]$ over any ring R — if R commutative with 1 then so is $R[x]$.
- The zero ring (one element, 0=1) — usually excluded in definitions that require 1 ≠ 0.

:::intuition
Polynomial rings let you treat $x$ formally as a variable and do algebra with coefficients from another ring.

Matrix rings show that non-commutative multiplication arises naturally when composing linear transformations.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>$\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring with zero divisors</strong>
:::

:::solution
The elements are the residue classes $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$, with addition and multiplication taken **modulo 6**.

**It is a ring.** Modular addition makes $(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}, +)$ an abelian group (identity $0$, inverse of $a$ is $6-a$), multiplication mod 6 is associative and commutative, and distributivity is inherited from $\mathbb{Z}$. So it is a commutative ring with $1$.
**Step 2 — Find a zero divisor.** Take $2$ and $3$, both nonzero classes. Their product is $2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$. Since neither factor is $0$ yet the product is $0$, both $2$ and $3$ are **zero divisors**.
**Step 3 — Contrast with $\mathbb{Z}$.** In $\mathbb{Z}$ a product of two nonzero numbers is never zero, so $\mathbb{Z}$ has *no* zero divisors. Reducing mod a **composite** number introduces them.

**Sanity check:** $6$ is composite ($6 = 2\cdot 3$), and exactly those factors are the zero divisors we found. In general $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors iff $n$ is composite; when $n$ is prime it is a field.
:::

:::example[$M_2(\mathbb{R})$ is non-commutative and has zero divisors]
Matrix rings are the standard source of "badly behaved" multiplication. Take
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
:::

:::solution
**Non-commutativity.** Compute both products:
$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Since $AB \neq BA$, multiplication is **not commutative**.
**Step 2 — A zero divisor.** From Step 1, $BA = 0$ even though $B \neq 0$ and $A \neq 0$. So $A$ and $B$ are **zero-divisors** in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$.
**Step 3 — It is still a ring with $1$.** Matrix addition is an abelian group, matrix multiplication is associative and distributes over addition, and the identity matrix $I_2$ is the multiplicative $1$.

**Sanity check:** compute
$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
the zero matrix. So $A$ is also **nilpotent** with $A^2 = 0$. A nonzero nilpotent cannot exist in a domain, which is exactly why the matrix ring $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ fails to be an integral domain.
:::

## Units and zero-divisors {#units}

These two notions capture the opposite extremes of "how well-behaved is multiplication by this element." A unit is as good as it gets — you can undo multiplying by it. A zero-divisor is the pathological case — multiplying by it can collapse a nonzero element to $0$.

:::definition[Unit, zero-divisor, nilpotent]{#unit-definition}
In a ring $R$ with $1$:

- A **unit** is an element $u$ with a multiplicative inverse $v$, meaning $uv = vu = 1$. The set of units $R^\times$ forms a group under multiplication.
- A **zero-divisor** is a nonzero $a$ such that there exists a nonzero $b$ with $ab = 0$ or $ba = 0$.
- A **nilpotent** element satisfies $a^k = 0$ for some integer $k > 0$.
:::

:::proposition[A unit is never a zero-divisor]
In a ring with $1$, no element is both a unit and a zero-divisor.
:::

:::proof
If $u$ is a unit and $ub = 0$, then $b = u^{-1}(ub) = u^{-1}0 = 0$, so $u$ kills nothing nonzero.
:::

An **integral domain** is a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ and no zero-divisors.

:::proposition[Domains have no nonzero nilpotents]
In an integral domain the only nilpotent element is $0$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $a^k = 0$ with $k$ minimal and $a \neq 0$. Then $a\cdot a^{k-1} = 0$ exhibits a zero-divisor, a contradiction.
:::

In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (with $n \ge 2$), the units are exactly the classes $\overline{a}$ with $\gcd(a, n) = 1$; there are $\phi(n)$ of them. Every remaining nonzero class is a zero-divisor.

:::intuition
Units are the "invertible numbers" inside the ring — the ones you can divide by while staying inside the ring.

Zero-divisors are "poison" elements that kill other nonzero things when multiplied — like 2 and 3 in mod 6: their product disappears.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Units in $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$]
We find every unit and identify the group they form.
:::

:::solution
**Apply the coprimality test.** The units are the classes coprime to $8$. Checking $\gcd(a, 8)$ for $a = 1, \dots, 7$: coprime are $1, 3, 5, 7$ (the odd residues); the even ones share the factor $2$. So $(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ and $\phi(8) = 4$.
**Step 2 — Verify each is invertible.** $3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 1$, $5 \cdot 5 = 25 \equiv 1$, $7 \cdot 7 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$. So each nonidentity unit is its own inverse.
**Step 3 — Identify the group.** Since every element squares to the identity, the group has exponent $2$; a group of order $4$ in which $x^2 = e$ for all $x$ is the Klein four-group $C_2 \times C_2$ (not the cyclic $C_4$, which would need an element of order $4$).

**Sanity check:** confirm closure with a mixed product, $3 \cdot 5 = 15 \equiv 7 \pmod 8$, and $7$ is indeed in the set. ✓

Contrast with the zero-divisors: the nonzero **non**-units $2, 4, 6$ are all zero-divisors, e.g. $2 \cdot 4 = 8 \equiv 0 \pmod 8$.
:::

## Subrings {#subrings}

:::definition[Subring]{#subring-definition}
A subset $S$ of a ring $R$ is a **subring** if it is closed under addition, subtraction (or negatives), and multiplication, and contains the same $1$ (if we require rings to have $1$).
:::

The **subring test** is analogous to the subgroup test: rather than re-checking every axiom, you only verify closure, because associativity, commutativity, and distributivity are automatically inherited from $R$.

:::proposition[Subring test]{#subring-test}
A nonempty $S \subseteq R$ is a subring iff for all $a, b \in S$,

$$a - b \in S \quad\text{and}\quad ab \in S,$$

where closure under subtraction also gives $0$ and negatives — plus $1 \in S$ under the with-$1$ convention.
:::

Examples: $\mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$.

:::intuition
A subring is a smaller "number system" living inside a bigger one that is still self-contained under $+$ and $\times$ — like a fenced-off yard where, no matter which two things inside you add, subtract, or multiply, you never have to step outside the fence to name the result.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Is $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ a subring of $\mathbb{C}$?]
$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the set of Gaussian integers. We run the subring test.
:::

**Nonempty and contains $1$.** Taking $a = 1, b = 0$ gives $1 \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$. ✓
**Step 2 — Closed under subtraction.** $(a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)i$, and $a - c, b - d \in \mathbb{Z}$, so the difference is again in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. ✓
**Step 3 — Closed under multiplication.** $(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$. Both $ac - bd$ and $ad + bc$ are integers, so the product stays in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. ✓
**Conclusion.** All conditions hold, so $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$.

**Sanity check:** try $(2 + i)(1 - i) = 2 - 2i + i - i^2 = 2 - i + 1 = 3 - i$, which has integer parts $3$ and $-1$ — still a Gaussian integer. ✓

**Non-example:** $2\mathbb{Z}$ (the even integers) is closed under subtraction and multiplication, but $1 \notin 2\mathbb{Z}$. So under the convention that a subring must share the $1$ of $R$, it fails the test; without that convention it is a *rng* (a ring without unity).

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting that addition must form an abelian group (including commutativity of + and additive inverses).
- Assuming every ring has a 1, or that 1 ≠ 0 (the zero ring is degenerate).
- Confusing "commutative ring" with "field" — fields require multiplicative inverses for nonzero elements.
- Thinking matrix rings are commutative.
- Forgetting distributivity when checking if a set is a ring (the hard part is usually verifying the two distributive laws or that multiplication stays inside).
- In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ thinking every nonzero element is a unit (only true when n prime).
