# Polynomial Rings and Unique Factorization

Polynomial rings are the most important examples of rings beyond the integers. Over a field, F[x] is a Euclidean domain — the gold standard for "nice" factorization.

The hierarchy is: Euclidean domain (ED) ⇒ principal ideal domain (PID) ⇒ unique factorization domain (UFD). Each implication is strict in general.

Understanding these classes tells you exactly when you have unique factorization into irreducibles (up to units and order), and when you have a Euclidean algorithm for gcds.

## Polynomial rings over fields {#polynomial-rings}

Polynomial rings F[x] over a field F are the direct generalisation of the integers that lets us do "arithmetic with indeterminates." They are the setting in which we prove unique factorization for polynomials, run the Euclidean algorithm, and build field extensions by adjoining roots. Almost everything that works for Z also works for F[x] (and vice versa), which is why they are the prototype for all Euclidean domains.

The key extra structure is the degree function, which plays the role of absolute value and makes division with remainder possible. Because F is a field, there are no zero-divisors among the leading coefficients, so the degree of a product is exactly the sum of the degrees. This turns F[x] into a Euclidean domain and therefore into a PID and a UFD.

In this section you will learn the basic arithmetic of F[x], the division algorithm, the fact that it is a Euclidean domain, and how irreducibles behave like primes.

Let $F$ be a field. Then $F[x]$ is the set of all polynomials $a_n x^n + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$ with coefficients $a_i \in F$, added and multiplied in the usual way.

- It is a commutative ring with $1$ (the constant polynomial $1$).
- It is an integral domain: a product of two nonzero polynomials is nonzero.
- Degree function: for nonzero $f, g$, $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$. This holds precisely because $F$ has no zero-divisors, so the leading coefficient of $fg$, which is $\operatorname{lead}(f)\cdot\operatorname{lead}(g)$, is again nonzero.
- Division algorithm: for any $f, g$ with $g \neq 0$ there exist unique $q, r$ with $f = qg + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$. (This is where we need the leading coefficient of $g$ to be invertible, which is automatic in a field.)

Just as prime numbers are the multiplicative building blocks of $\mathbb{Z}$, the **irreducible** polynomials — those that cannot be written as a product of two lower-degree polynomials — are the building blocks of $F[x]$, and they play the role of "primes".

**ELI5**

- $F[x]$ is like the integers, but with a "ruler" that measures each polynomial by its degree instead of its size. Long division, gcds, and prime factorization all carry over — degree just replaces "how big is the number."
- The only elements you can divide by freely (the units) are the nonzero constants. A polynomial like $x$ has no inverse, because multiplying by anything only makes the degree go *up*, never back down to the constant $1$.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

<strong>Division with remainder in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$</strong>

Divide $f = x^3 + 2x^2 - x + 1$ by $g = x - 1$.

Step 1 — Set up synthetic division with the root $x = 1$ of $g$. Write the coefficients of $f$: $1, 2, -1, 1$.

Step 2 — Bring down the leading $1$. Multiply by $1$ and add to the next coefficient: $2 + 1 = 3$. Multiply by $1$ and add: $-1 + 3 = 2$. Multiply by $1$ and add: $1 + 2 = 3$.

Step 3 — Read off the result. The quotient coefficients are $1, 3, 2$, so $q = x^2 + 3x + 2$, and the last number $3$ is the remainder $r$. Since $g$ has degree $1$, we need $\deg r < 1$, and indeed $r = 3$ is a constant. ✓

Step 4 — Sanity check by expanding $qg + r$:
$$(x-1)(x^2 + 3x + 2) + 3 = (x^3 + 2x^2 - x - 2) + 3 = x^3 + 2x^2 - x + 1 = f. \checkmark$$

Because $r = 3 \neq 0$, the divisor $x - 1$ is *not* a factor of $f$ — equivalently, $f(1) = 3 \neq 0$, which matches the remainder (this is the Remainder Theorem: dividing by $x - a$ leaves remainder $f(a)$).

## Euclidean domains {#euclidean-domains}

A Euclidean domain is an integral domain equipped with a Euclidean function that lets you perform division with strictly smaller remainders, exactly as you do with integers or with polynomials. This single extra piece of structure is enough to guarantee that gcds exist via the Euclidean algorithm and that every ideal is principal.

The two classical examples are the integers (with N(n) = |n|) and polynomial rings over fields (with N(f) = deg f). A few quadratic integer rings also qualify, but most number rings do not; the Euclidean property is rare and precious.

In this section you will learn the definition, verify that both Z and F[x] satisfy it, see the Euclidean algorithm at work, and understand why every Euclidean domain is automatically a PID (which sets up the next section on unique factorization).

A domain $D$ is a **Euclidean domain** if there is a function $N \colon D \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ (a **Euclidean function**) such that for any $a, b \in D$ with $b \neq 0$ there exist $q, r \in D$ with
$$a = qb + r, \qquad \text{where } r = 0 \text{ or } N(r) < N(b).$$
The point of $N$ is to give a notion of "size" that strictly decreases at each division step, which guarantees the Euclidean algorithm terminates. Note $q, r$ need not be unique in a general ED (they are for $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$).

Classic examples:

- $\mathbb{Z}$ with $N(n) = |n|$.
- $F[x]$ ($F$ a field) with $N(f) = \deg f$.
- Certain quadratic integer rings, e.g. the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$.

In an ED you can run the Euclidean algorithm to compute gcds, and **every ED is a PID** (proved in the next section).

**ELI5**

- A Euclidean domain is a set of "numbers" that comes with a tape measure. Whenever you divide, the leftover (remainder) must measure strictly shorter than what you divided by. Because a length can only shrink so many times before hitting $0$, repeatedly dividing eventually stops — and that stopping point is the gcd.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**gcd in $\mathbb{Z}$ via the Euclidean algorithm**

Compute $\gcd(252, 198)$. At each step, divide and keep the remainder; the size $N(n) = |n|$ strictly drops.

$$
\begin{aligned}
252 &= 1 \cdot 198 + 54 \\
198 &= 3 \cdot 54 + 36 \\
 54 &= 1 \cdot 36 + 18 \\
 36 &= 2 \cdot 18 + 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last nonzero remainder is $18$, so $\gcd(252, 198) = 18$.

Sanity check: $252 = 14 \cdot 18$ and $198 = 11 \cdot 18$, and $\gcd(14, 11) = 1$, so $18$ is indeed the *largest* common divisor. ✓

**Worked Example:**

**gcd of polynomials in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$**

The same algorithm works verbatim in $F[x]$, with $N(f) = \deg f$ dropping each step. Compute $\gcd(x^3 - 1,\ x^2 - 1)$.

Step 1 — Divide $x^3 - 1$ by $x^2 - 1$. Since $x \cdot (x^2 - 1) = x^3 - x$, we get $x^3 - 1 = x\,(x^2 - 1) + (x - 1)$, with remainder $x - 1$ of degree $1 < 2$. ✓

Step 2 — Divide $x^2 - 1$ by $x - 1$: $x^2 - 1 = (x+1)(x - 1) + 0$.

The last nonzero remainder is $x - 1$, so $\gcd = x - 1$ (a gcd is only defined up to a unit factor; we may take it monic).

Sanity check: $x^3 - 1 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1)$ and $x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$, and the cofactors $x^2 + x + 1$ and $x + 1$ share no common factor, so $x - 1$ is correct. ✓

## PIDs and UFDs {#pids-ufds}

The chain Euclidean domain ⇒ PID ⇒ UFD organises the rings in which "unique factorization" makes sense. A PID is the middle ground where every ideal is generated by one element; a UFD is the weaker setting where factorization into irreducibles is still unique (up to units and order) even if not every ideal is principal.

Every Euclidean domain is a PID (the Euclidean function produces a generator for any ideal via the algorithm), and every PID is a UFD (the usual proof by Noetherian induction plus the fact that irreducibles are prime). The converses fail: Z[x] is a UFD but not a PID, and there are PIDs that are not Euclidean (rare).

In this section you will learn the precise definitions, the theorem relating the three classes, the counter-examples that separate them, and why in a UFD the notions of prime and irreducible coincide.

A **principal ideal** is one of the form $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$, generated by a single element. A **PID** (principal ideal domain) is an integral domain in which *every* ideal is principal.

A **UFD** (unique factorization domain) is an integral domain in which every nonzero non-unit element factors into irreducibles, and the factorization is unique up to units and ordering.

**Theorem**: Every ED is a PID, and every PID is a UFD. So $\text{ED} \Rightarrow \text{PID} \Rightarrow \text{UFD}$.

*Why ED $\Rightarrow$ PID:* given a nonzero ideal $I$, pick $d \in I \setminus \{0\}$ with $N(d)$ minimal. For any $a \in I$, divide: $a = qd + r$ with $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(d)$. But $r = a - qd \in I$, and minimality of $N(d)$ forbids $N(r) < N(d)$, so $r = 0$ and $a \in (d)$. Hence $I = (d)$.

Counter-examples the other way (both implications are strict): $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID (shown below); and there exist PIDs that are not EDs (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$), though these are rare.

In a UFD, "prime" and "irreducible" coincide. (In a general domain, *prime* — $p \mid ab \Rightarrow p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$ — is the stronger condition; primes are always irreducible, but not conversely.)

In a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal (a key fact used in building fields as quotients $F[x]/(p)$).

**ELI5**

- **PID:** every ideal is the set of multiples of a single element — like how in $\mathbb{Z}$ every ideal is $n\mathbb{Z}$, "all multiples of $n$." One number does all the work.
- **UFD:** the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic still holds — every element breaks into "primes" in essentially one way. A UFD may need *several* elements to describe some ideals, but factorization is still unique.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**A UFD that is not a PID: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$**

We show the ideal $I = (2, x) = \{2f(x) + x\,g(x) : f, g \in \mathbb{Z}[x]\}$ is not principal.

Step 1 — Identify $I$. A polynomial $2f + xg$ has constant term $2f(0)$, which is even; conversely any polynomial with even constant term $2c + x\cdot(\text{rest})$ lies in $I$. So $I$ is exactly the polynomials with even constant term.

Step 2 — Suppose for contradiction $I = (d)$ for some $d \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$. Then $d \mid 2$. Because $\deg(d) \le \deg(2) = 0$ (degrees add and $2 \neq 0$), $d$ must be a constant, so $d \in \{\pm 1, \pm 2\}$.

Step 3 — Rule out each. If $d = \pm 1$ then $(d) = \mathbb{Z}[x]$, but $1 \notin I$ (its constant term $1$ is odd), contradiction. If $d = \pm 2$ then every element of $(d)$ has all coefficients even, but $x \in I$ has an odd coefficient, so $d \nmid x$, contradiction.

Conclusion — No single $d$ generates $I$, so $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID. It *is* a UFD (Gauss's lemma: $R$ a UFD $\Rightarrow R[x]$ a UFD, applied to $R = \mathbb{Z}$), which shows UFD $\not\Rightarrow$ PID. ✓

## Factorization in F[x] {#factorization}

Over a field $F$, $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID and a UFD. So every polynomial factors uniquely into irreducibles — and *which* polynomials are irreducible depends heavily on $F$.

- A non-constant $f \in F[x]$ is **irreducible over $F$** if it cannot be written as a product of two polynomials in $F[x]$ of strictly lower degree. (Irreducibility is relative to the field: $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but factors as $(x-i)(x+i)$ over $\mathbb{C}$.)
- Low-degree test: a polynomial of degree $2$ or $3$ is irreducible over $F$ **iff it has no root in $F$**, because any nontrivial factorization must include a linear (degree-$1$) factor, and a linear factor corresponds to a root. This test *fails from degree $4$ on*: e.g. $x^4 + 2x^2 + 1 = (x^2 + 1)^2$ has no real root yet is reducible over $\mathbb{R}$.
- For $F = \mathbb{Q}$ or finite fields there are further tests: Eisenstein's criterion, reduction mod a prime $p$, and the Rational Root Theorem.
- Unique factorization takes the form $f = c \cdot p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$, where $c \in F^\times$ is a unit and the $p_i$ are distinct monic irreducibles.

Gauss's lemma relates factorization over $\mathbb{Z}$ and over $\mathbb{Q}$: a primitive polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$ iff it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, so you can hunt for factorizations with integer coefficients.

**ELI5**

- Irreducibility depends on where you're allowed to "cut." Over $\mathbb{C}$ everything crumbles into linear factors (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra); over $\mathbb{R}$ the smallest indivisible pieces are lines and "twist" quadratics like $x^2 + 1$; over $\mathbb{Q}$ even more polynomials stay in one piece because you're forbidden from using irrational or complex numbers to break them.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**Irreducibility of a quadratic over different fields**

Is $x^2 - 2$ irreducible? It has degree $2$, so by the low-degree test it is irreducible iff it has no root in the field.

- Over $\mathbb{Q}$: a root would be $\pm\sqrt{2}$, which is irrational, so no rational root exists ⇒ **irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$**.
- Over $\mathbb{R}$: $\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{R}$, so $x^2 - 2 = (x - \sqrt{2})(x + \sqrt{2})$ ⇒ **reducible over $\mathbb{R}$**.

This makes the "depends on the field" point concrete: the *same* polynomial is prime in one ring and composite in a larger one. ✓

**Worked Example:**

**Factor $x^4 + 4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$**

This has no rational (indeed no real) root since $x^4 + 4 > 0$ always, so it has no linear factor over $\mathbb{Q}$. But no root does **not** mean irreducible at degree $4$ — we must also check for a factorization into two quadratics.

Step 1 — Use the Sophie Germain identity $a^4 + 4b^4 = (a^2 + 2b^2 + 2ab)(a^2 + 2b^2 - 2ab)$ with $a = x$, $b = 1$:
$$x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2).$$

Step 2 — Verify by expanding:
$$(x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2) = (x^2 + 2)^2 - (2x)^2 = x^4 + 4x^2 + 4 - 4x^2 = x^4 + 4. \checkmark$$

Step 3 — Confirm each quadratic factor is itself irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Their discriminants are $2^2 - 4(1)(2) = -4 < 0$ and $(-2)^2 - 4(1)(2) = -4 < 0$, so neither has a real (hence rational) root; being degree $2$, each is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.

So the full factorization into irreducibles over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x^4 + 4 = (x^2 + 2x + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)$. This is the cautionary example: a degree-$4$ polynomial with no roots can still factor. ✓

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting that in F[x] the units are the nonzero constants, not just ±1.
- Applying Euclidean algorithm without a Euclidean function (only works in EDs).
- Claiming Z[x] is a PID because Z and x are PIDs — product of PIDs need not be (and isn't).
- Confusing irreducible with prime (they coincide in UFDs, but definition of prime is stronger in general rings).
- Thinking every UFD is a PID (e.g. certain polynomial rings provide counterexamples).
- In polynomial division, forgetting that the coefficients must live in a field to guarantee the division algorithm works (leading coeff must be invertible).
- Assuming unique factorization always holds — it fails dramatically in some rings (e.g. Z[√-5]).
