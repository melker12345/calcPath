# Ideals and Quotient Rings

Ideals are the ring-theoretic analogue of normal subgroups. They are the subsets you can "mod out by" to form quotient rings while preserving the ring operations.

The construction of quotient rings $R/I$ generalizes $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and is the foundation for all of commutative algebra and algebraic geometry (where ideals correspond to varieties).

Ring homomorphisms have kernels that are ideals, and the isomorphism theorems carry over beautifully.

## Ideals {#ideals}

Ideals generalise the notion of "normal subgroups" from group theory to rings: they are the subsets you can safely "divide by" to form quotient structures while keeping the ring operations well-defined. They are indispensable in commutative algebra, algebraic number theory, and algebraic geometry, where they correspond to geometric objects called varieties.

The definition is deliberately asymmetric at first (left vs. right) because rings need not be commutative. The absorption property (multiplying an ideal element by anything from the ring stays inside the ideal) is what makes the quotient construction work. In commutative rings the distinction disappears and we simply speak of ideals.

In this section you will learn the precise axioms for left, right, and two-sided ideals, see the basic examples that appear everywhere, and understand why kernels of ring homomorphisms are always ideals.

:::definition[Ideal]{#ideal-definition}
Let $R$ be a ring. A subset $I \subseteq R$ is a **left ideal** if:

- $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of $(R, +)$
- For all $r \in R$, $a \in I$, $r a \in I$ (absorption)

It is a **right ideal** if instead $a r \in I$ for all $r \in R$, $a \in I$, and a **two-sided ideal** (or just "ideal" when $R$ is commutative) if it is both.
:::

In a commutative ring, left = right = two-sided.

The whole ring R and {0} are always ideals.

:::intuition
An ideal is a "black hole" inside the ring: once something falls in (by multiplying from outside), it stays in. You can add and subtract inside it freely.

It is the right kind of subset to collapse to zero when forming quotients.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[the even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ form an ideal in $\mathbb{Z}$]
We check the two axioms for $I = 2\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \dots\}$.
:::

:::solution
**additive subgroup.** $0 = 2\cdot 0 \in I$, so $I$ is nonempty. If $2m, 2n \in I$ then $2m - 2n = 2(m-n) \in I$, so $I$ is closed under subtraction. That is exactly the subgroup test for $(I,+)$.
**Step 2 — absorption.** Take any ring element $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ and any $a = 2m \in I$. Then $k \cdot (2m) = 2(km) \in I$, since $km \in \mathbb{Z}$. Because $\mathbb{Z}$ is commutative we get $a k \in I$ too, so $I$ is two-sided.

Both axioms hold, so $2\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal. **Sanity check:** $3 \cdot 2 = 6 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$ ✓, and $6 - 2 = 4 \in 2\mathbb{Z}$ ✓.
:::

:::example[a subring that is *not* an ideal]
Consider $I = \mathbb{Z}$ sitting inside the ring $R = \mathbb{Q}$. It is a subring (closed under $+$, $-$, $\times$, contains $1$). But it fails absorption: take $r = \tfrac12 \in R$ and $a = 1 \in I$; then $r a = \tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. So $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ but not an ideal. This is exactly why "subring" and "ideal" are different notions — only ideals let you form quotient rings.
:::

## Principal ideals {#principal-ideals}

In many familiar rings the ideals are particularly simple: they are all generated by a single element. Such ideals are called principal, and the rings in which every ideal is principal (PIDs) are among the nicest rings for factorization and divisibility theory.

The construction is straightforward once you have a ring with identity: just take all multiples of a fixed element a. This immediately gives you the ideals (n) inside the integers, and the same pattern works in polynomial rings over fields. Not every ring is a PID—polynomials in two variables already provide counter-examples—but the principal ones are the ones where the Euclidean algorithm and unique factorization behave best.

In this section you will learn the definition, see why Z is a PID while Z[x] is not for the ideal (2,x), and connect principal ideals to the broader hierarchy of Euclidean domains, PIDs, and UFDs that appears in the next topic.

:::definition[Principal ideal]{#principal-ideal}
In a commutative ring $R$ with 1, the **principal ideal** generated by $a$ is

$$(a) = \{ r a \mid r \in R \},$$

all multiples of $a$. More generally $(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ is the ideal of all $r_1 a_1 + \cdots + r_k a_k$.
:::

In $\mathbb{Z}$, every ideal is principal: $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$.

:::proposition[A non-principal ideal]
In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal $(2, x)$ is *not* principal.
:::

:::proof
A single generator $g$ would have to divide both $2$ and $x$; the only common divisors of $2$ and $x$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ are the units $\pm 1$, which would force $(g) = \mathbb{Z}[x]$ — but $(2,x) \neq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ since it contains no polynomial with odd constant term. So no single generator works.
:::

:::definition[Principal ideal ring, PID]
A ring in which every ideal is principal is a **principal ideal ring** (PIR); if it is also an integral domain, it is a **PID** (principal ideal domain).
:::

$\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ for a field $F$ are PIDs; $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not.

:::intuition
The principal ideal $(a)$ is everything you can "make" by multiplying $a$ by arbitrary ring elements — like all the multiples $\dots, -2a, -a, 0, a, 2a, \dots$ marching along a number line. A *principal ideal ring* is one where every ideal is such a single-generator "number line"; needing two generators like $(2, x)$ means the ideal is genuinely two-dimensional and can't be flattened to one.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[the ideal generated by two integers is principal]
Find a single generator of $(6, 9)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::solution
**write out the ideal.** By definition $(6,9) = \{6a + 9b \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, the set of all integer combinations of $6$ and $9$.
**Step 2 — smallest positive element is the gcd.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the set of combinations $6a + 9b$ is exactly the multiples of $\gcd(6,9) = 3$. Concretely, Bézout gives $3 = 6\cdot(-1) + 9\cdot 1$, so $3 \in (6,9)$, hence $(3) \subseteq (6,9)$.
**Step 3 — the reverse inclusion.** Both $6 = 3\cdot 2$ and $9 = 3\cdot 3$ are multiples of $3$, so $6, 9 \in (3)$, giving $(6,9) \subseteq (3)$.

Therefore $(6,9) = (3)$. **Sanity check:** $6a + 9b$ is always divisible by $3$ ✓, and we exhibited $3$ itself as a combination ✓. This is the general fact that makes $\mathbb{Z}$ a PID: $(m, n) = (\gcd(m,n))$.
:::

:::example[a principal ideal in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$]
Describe the ideal $(4)$ in $R = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::solution
**list the multiples.** $(4) = \{r\cdot 4 \mid r \in R\}$. Computing $4\cdot 0 = 0$, $4\cdot 1 = 4$, $4\cdot 2 = 8$, $4\cdot 3 = 12 \equiv 0$, and it repeats. So $(4) = \{0, 4, 8\}$.
**Step 2 — count.** The ideal has $3$ elements.
**Step 3 — different generators.** Note $8 \equiv -4 \pmod{12}$, and $8\cdot 2 = 16 \equiv 4$, so $8$ generates the same set: $(8) = \{0, 8, 4\} = (4)$. A principal ideal can have several generators.

**Sanity check:** the size $3$ divides $|R| = 12$ ✓ (an ideal is in particular an additive subgroup, so Lagrange applies).
:::

## Quotient rings {#quotient-rings}

Quotient rings are the ring-theoretic version of modular arithmetic: once you have an ideal I, you can "set everything in I to zero" and obtain a new ring R/I whose elements are cosets. This construction is exactly how we obtain Z/nZ from Z, and it is the central tool that lets us move between rings and study their structure via homomorphic images.

The key technical point is that the ideal absorption property guarantees the operations on cosets are independent of the choice of representatives. Without that, the multiplication table would be ambiguous. Every ring homomorphism factors through the quotient by its kernel, so quotients classify the "views" of a ring.

In this section you will learn how to define the operations on R/I, verify they are well-defined, recognise the universal property of the quotient map, and see the first isomorphism theorem in action.

:::definition[Quotient ring]{#quotient-ring}
Given an ideal $I$ of $R$, the **quotient ring** $R/I$ is the set of cosets $\{r + I\}$, with operations

$$(r + I) + (s + I) = (r+s) + I, \qquad (r + I)(s + I) = (rs) + I.$$
:::

:::proposition[The quotient operations are well-defined]
The two operations above do not depend on the chosen coset representatives, precisely because $I$ is an ideal: absorption makes multiplication independent of representatives.
:::

:::proof
If $r + I = r' + I$ and $s + I = s' + I$, then $r' = r + a$ and $s' = s + b$ for some $a, b \in I$, and $r's' = rs + (rb + as + ab)$. Absorption forces $rb, as, ab \in I$, so $r's' - rs \in I$, i.e. $r's' + I = rs + I$. The answer does not depend on which representatives you picked.
:::

The map $\pi: R \to R/I$, $r \mapsto r+I$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $I$.

**Prime and maximal ideals.** The quotient $R/I$ inherits nice structure exactly when $I$ is special. Assume throughout that $R$ is commutative with $1$.

:::definition[Prime ideal]{#prime-ideal}
An ideal $I$ is a **prime ideal** if $I \neq R$ and

$$ab \in I \ \Rightarrow\ a \in I \ \text{ or }\ b \in I.$$
:::

:::definition[Maximal ideal]{#maximal-ideal}
An ideal $I$ is a **maximal ideal** if there is no ideal strictly between $I$ and $R$.
:::

:::theorem[Prime and maximal ideals read off the quotient]{#prime-maximal-quotients}
Let $I$ be an ideal of a commutative ring $R$ with $1$.

- $I$ is prime $\iff$ $R/I$ is an integral domain.
- $I$ is maximal $\iff$ $R/I$ is a field.
:::

Since every field is a domain, every maximal ideal is prime.

:::intuition
Quotienting by $I$ means "declare everything in $I$ to be zero." Because of the absorption property, this doesn't break the multiplication rule. It is like arithmetic on a clock: on a 12-hour clock you have "modded out by 12," so $12 = 0$, $13 = 1$, and multiplication still works ($3 \times 5 = 15 = 3$).

"$I$ is prime" is the quotient version of "no accidental zeros": two nonzero things can't multiply to zero. "$I$ is maximal" is even stronger — everything nonzero becomes invertible, so the quotient is a field.

Classic example: $\mathbb{Z}/(n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and this is a field exactly when $n$ is prime (because $(n)$ is a maximal ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $n$ is prime).
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[computing in $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2, x)$]
Let $I = (2, x)$ in $R = \mathbb{Z}[x]$. What is the image of $3 + x$ in $R/I$?
:::

:::solution
**what dies in the quotient.** In $R/I$ we set every element of $I$ to zero. Since $2 \in I$ and $x \in I$, we have $2 \equiv 0$ and $x \equiv 0 \pmod I$.
**Step 2 — reduce the polynomial.** $3 + x \equiv 3 + 0 = 3 \pmod I$.
**Step 3 — reduce the constant.** Because $2 \equiv 0$, we get $3 = 2 + 1 \equiv 0 + 1 = 1 \pmod I$. (Formally $3 - 1 = 2 \in I$, so $3 + I = 1 + I$.)

So the image of $3 + x$ is $1$. **Sanity check:** every integer reduces mod $2$ and every positive-degree term reduces to $0$, so $R/I$ has just the two classes $\{0, 1\}$; indeed $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2,x) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{F}_2$, and $1$ is the correct nonzero class ✓.
:::

:::example[$\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$]
**Step 1 — cosets have a normal form.** Divide any $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ by $x^2 + 1$: $f(x) = q(x)(x^2+1) + (a + bx)$ with remainder of degree $< 2$. Since $q(x)(x^2+1) \in (x^2+1)$, we get $f \equiv a + bx \pmod{(x^2+1)}$. So every coset has a unique representative $a + bx$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$.
**Step 2 — the key relation.** In the quotient $x^2 + 1 \equiv 0$, i.e. $x^2 \equiv -1$. Write $\alpha$ for the image of $x$; then $\alpha^2 = -1$.
**Step 3 — match multiplication with $\mathbb{C}$.** $(a + b\alpha)(c + d\alpha) = ac + (ad + bc)\alpha + bd\,\alpha^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\alpha$. This is exactly the rule $(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i$.
:::

:::solution
1. Sending $a + b\alpha \mapsto a + bi$ is therefore a ring isomorphism $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$. **Sanity check:** $\alpha^2 = -1$ corresponds to $i^2 = -1$ ✓. (Note: over $\mathbb{Q}$ the same computation gives $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{Q}(i)$, the Gaussian rationals, not all of $\mathbb{C}$.)
:::

## The ring homomorphism theorems {#homomorphism-theorems}

The isomorphism theorems for rings are the direct analogues of the three isomorphism theorems for groups. They give precise relationships between a ring, its ideals (kernels), its homomorphic images, and the quotients you can form. Mastering them is essential for any serious work in algebra; almost every structural result about rings ultimately reduces to one of these theorems or the lattice isomorphism (correspondence) theorem for ideals.

Just as in groups, the first theorem says that the image of any homomorphism is isomorphic to the quotient by its kernel. The higher theorems let you manipulate "fractions" of ideals and rings with the same algebraic rules you use for numbers. The correspondence theorem tells you that ideals of a quotient R/I are in one-to-one order-preserving correspondence with ideals of R that contain I.

In this section you will learn the statements of the three isomorphism theorems plus the correspondence theorem, see why kernels are ideals, and work through the universal examples that appear in every textbook and research paper.

:::proposition[Kernels are ideals, images are subrings]
For a ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S$, the kernel $\ker \phi$ is an ideal of $R$ and the image $\operatorname{im} \phi$ is a subring of $S$.
:::

:::proof
Here $\ker\phi = \{r \in R \mid \phi(r) = 0\}$. It is an additive subgroup because $\phi$ is a group homomorphism on $(R,+)$. For absorption, take $a \in \ker\phi$ and any $r \in R$: then $\phi(ra) = \phi(r)\phi(a) = \phi(r)\cdot 0 = 0$, so $ra \in \ker\phi$ (and likewise $ar \in \ker\phi$). That is precisely the ideal axiom — which is why kernels are ideals, not merely subrings.
:::

:::theorem[First isomorphism theorem for rings]{#first-isomorphism-rings}
For a ring homomorphism $\phi: R \to S$,

$$R / \ker\phi \cong \operatorname{im}\phi$$

as rings.
:::

There are second and third isomorphism theorems too, and a correspondence theorem.

:::theorem[Correspondence theorem for ideals]
The ideals of $R/I$ are in bijection with the ideals of $R$ containing $I$, and this bijection preserves inclusions (it is a lattice isomorphism).
:::

:::intuition
A ring homomorphism is a machine that "renames" elements while respecting $+$ and $\times$. The kernel is everything the machine crushes to $0$. The first isomorphism theorem says: if you first crush the kernel yourself (form $R/\ker\phi$) and then rename, nothing is lost — $R/\ker\phi$ is a perfect copy of the output $\operatorname{im}\phi$.

Kernels of ring maps are ideals, not just additive subgroups — anything in $R$ times a crushed element is still crushed (it maps to $r \cdot 0 = 0$).
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[the natural projection $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$]
Let $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, $n \mapsto n + 6\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::solution
**the kernel.** $\phi(n) = 0 \iff n \equiv 0 \pmod 6 \iff n \in 6\mathbb{Z}$. So $\ker\phi = (6) = 6\mathbb{Z}$.
**Step 2 — the image.** $\phi$ is surjective (every class $k + 6\mathbb{Z}$ is $\phi(k)$), so $\operatorname{im}\phi = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.
**Step 3 — apply the first isomorphism theorem.** $\mathbb{Z}/\ker\phi = \mathbb{Z}/(6) \cong \operatorname{im}\phi = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$.

**Sanity check:** $|\mathbb{Z}/(6)| = 6$ matches $|\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}| = 6$ ✓.
:::

:::example[the evaluation map]
Fix a commutative ring $R$ with $1$ and an element $c \in R$. Define evaluation at $c$: $\phi: R[x] \to R$, $f \mapsto f(c)$.
:::

:::solution
**$\phi$ is a ring homomorphism.** Evaluating respects sums and products: $(f+g)(c) = f(c)+g(c)$ and $(fg)(c) = f(c)g(c)$, and $\phi(1) = 1$.
**Step 2 — the kernel is $(x - c)$.** If $f(c) = 0$, divide by the monic polynomial $x - c$: $f(x) = q(x)(x-c) + r$ with $r \in R$ constant. Plugging in $x = c$ gives $0 = f(c) = r$, so $f = q(x)(x-c) \in (x-c)$. Conversely every multiple of $x-c$ vanishes at $c$. Hence $\ker\phi = (x-c)$. (Division by $x-c$ works over any commutative ring because $x-c$ is monic.)
**Step 3 — the image is all of $R$.** For any $b \in R$, the constant polynomial $b$ maps to $b$, so $\phi$ is surjective.

By the first isomorphism theorem, $R[x]/(x-c) \cong R$. **Sanity check:** in the quotient $x \equiv c$, so every polynomial collapses to its value at $c$, a plain element of $R$ ✓.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting that ideals must be additive subgroups (closed under subtraction).
- In non-commutative rings, confusing left, right, and two-sided ideals.
- Trying to form R/I when I is only a subring, not an ideal — multiplication of cosets fails to be well-defined.
- Assuming every ideal is principal (false in most rings; e.g. $(2, x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$).
- Confusing $(2)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (all polynomials with *every* coefficient even) with $(2, x)$ (all polynomials with *even constant term*). These are different ideals: $x \in (2,x)$ but $x \notin (2)$.
- Thinking every prime ideal is maximal or vice versa: in $\mathbb{Z}$, $(0)$ is prime (quotient $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain) but not maximal (quotient $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field); $(p)$ is both.
- Forgetting the correspondence: there is an inclusion-preserving lattice isomorphism between ideals of $R$ containing $I$ and ideals of $R/I$.
