# Group Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

Homomorphisms are the "structure-preserving maps" between groups. They are the morphisms in the category of groups. The most important invariants attached to a homomorphism are its kernel (elements sent to the identity) and image (elements hit).

The three isomorphism theorems (plus the correspondence theorem) are the fundamental tools for relating subgroups, quotients, and homomorphic images. They let you move information back and forth between a group and its quotients or images.

Isomorphisms are the "relabelings" that show two groups are essentially the same. Automorphisms are the symmetries of a single group.

## Group homomorphisms {#homomorphisms}

Homomorphisms are the structure-preserving functions between groups—the only maps that deserve to be called "group maps." They are the arrows in the category of groups and are the device that lets us compare different groups, transport theorems from one to another, and classify groups up to isomorphism.

The homomorphism property $\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)$ is deceptively simple: it automatically forces preservation of the identity and inverses, so you only ever have to check one identity. The kernel and image of any such map carry enormous information; they are the main invariants used throughout the rest of group theory.

In this section you will learn the definition and its immediate consequences, the most common examples you will meet in every course, and how kernels and images organise the landscape of homomorphisms.

A map $\phi: G \to H$ between groups is a **homomorphism** if $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$ for all $a,b \in G$.

It automatically preserves identity: $\phi(e_G) = e_H$, and inverses: $\phi(a^{-1}) = \phi(a)^{-1}$.

Examples:

- The map $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $k \mapsto k \bmod n$ (reduction mod n).
- Determinant $\det: GL_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$.
- Sign homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn}: S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$.

**ELI5**

- A homomorphism is a translation of operations: doing the operation in G and then translating gives the same result as translating first and then operating in H. It respects the "multiplication table."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**The exponential map**

Claim: $\exp: (\mathbb{R}, +) \to (\mathbb{R}^{>0}, \times)$, $x \mapsto e^x$ is a homomorphism.

- **Identify the operations.** The domain group is $\mathbb{R}$ under *addition*; the codomain group is the positive reals $\mathbb{R}^{>0}$ under *multiplication*. So the homomorphism property $\phi(a+b) = \phi(a)\,\phi(b)$ reads: exponentiating a sum equals the product of exponentials.
- **Check the property.** For all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$, the exponent law gives $\exp(x+y) = e^{x+y} = e^x e^y = \exp(x)\,\exp(y)$. That is exactly $\phi(x+y) = \phi(x)\phi(y)$, so the operation is preserved. ✓
- **Sanity-check the free consequences.** Preservation of the identity: $\phi(0) = e^0 = 1$, and $1$ is indeed the identity of $(\mathbb{R}^{>0}, \times)$. ✓ Preservation of inverses: $\phi(-x) = e^{-x} = 1/e^x = \phi(x)^{-1}$. ✓ We never had to check these separately — they came for free.

This map is in fact an isomorphism: its inverse is $\ln: (\mathbb{R}^{>0}, \times) \to (\mathbb{R}, +)$, which turns products back into sums.

**Worked Example:**

**A map that is *not* a homomorphism**

Consider $f: (\mathbb{Z}, +) \to (\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $f(x) = x + 1$.

- **Test the property.** We need $f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)$. The left side is $f(x+y) = x + y + 1$. The right side is $f(x) + f(y) = (x+1) + (y+1) = x + y + 2$.
- **Compare.** These are equal only if $1 = 2$, which is false. So $f$ fails the homomorphism property — for instance $f(1+1) = 3$ but $f(1) + f(1) = 4$.

The quick tell: a homomorphism must send the identity to the identity, but $f(0) = 1 \neq 0$. Any map that moves the identity is automatically disqualified, which is often the fastest way to rule one out.

## Kernel and image {#kernel-image}

Every homomorphism comes with two canonical subgroups attached to it: the kernel (what gets crushed to the identity) and the image (what actually gets hit in the target). These two subgroups control almost everything you can say about the map—injectivity, surjectivity, and the isomorphism type of the image.

The kernel is always normal (a fact that only becomes obvious after you check the conjugation criterion), while the image is always a subgroup of the codomain. The first isomorphism theorem then identifies the image with the quotient of the domain by the kernel, giving the fundamental dictionary between homomorphisms, normal subgroups, and quotients.

In this section you will learn the definitions, the four fundamental facts that follow immediately, and why these two subgroups are the most important pieces of data attached to any group homomorphism you will ever write down.

The **kernel** of $\phi: G\to H$ is $\ker \phi = \{g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H \}$.

The **image** is $\operatorname{im} \phi = \{\phi(g) \mid g \in G \} \subseteq H$.

**Fundamental facts**:

- $\ker \phi$ is always a normal subgroup of $G$.
- $\operatorname{im} \phi$ is always a subgroup of $H$.
- $\phi$ is injective iff $\ker \phi = \{e\}$.
- $\phi$ is surjective iff $\operatorname{im} \phi = H$.

By the first isomorphism theorem, $G / \ker \phi \cong \operatorname{im} \phi$.

**ELI5**

- Think of the map as a lossy photocopier. The **kernel** is everything that comes out blank (crushed to the identity) — it measures how much information the copy throws away. A trivial kernel means nothing is lost, so the copy is faithful (injective).
- The **image** is the set of pages you can actually produce — how much of the target you can reach. If the image is the whole codomain, the map is surjective.
- Slogan: kernel = what you *lose*, image = what you *hit*. The first isomorphism theorem says the domain, with the lost part folded away, is a perfect copy of the image.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**Kernel of reduction mod 2**

Let $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $k\mapsto k \bmod 2$.

- **Kernel.** By definition $\ker\phi = \{k \in \mathbb{Z} \mid k \bmod 2 = 0\}$, i.e. the integers whose remainder on division by $2$ is $0$ — the even integers. So $\ker\phi = 2\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Image.** Both residues $0$ and $1$ are hit ($\phi(0)=0$, $\phi(1)=1$), so $\operatorname{im}\phi = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$; the map is surjective.
- **Sanity-check with the first isomorphism theorem.** It predicts $\mathbb{Z}/\ker\phi \cong \operatorname{im}\phi$, i.e. $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. ✓ And since $\ker\phi = 2\mathbb{Z} \neq \{0\}$, the map is *not* injective — indeed every even integer collapses to $0$.

**Worked Example:**

**Kernel and image of the determinant**

Let $\det: GL_2(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$, where $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is the group of invertible $2\times2$ real matrices under multiplication and $\mathbb{R}^\times$ is the nonzero reals under multiplication.

- **It is a homomorphism** because $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ for all matrices.
- **Kernel.** $\ker(\det) = \{A \in GL_2(\mathbb{R}) \mid \det(A) = 1\}$. The number $1$ is the identity of $\mathbb{R}^\times$, and by definition the set of invertible matrices with determinant $1$ is the special linear group $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$. So $\ker(\det) = SL_2(\mathbb{R})$.
- **Image.** Every nonzero real $r$ is a determinant: e.g. $\det\begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = r$. So $\operatorname{im}(\det) = \mathbb{R}^\times$; the map is surjective.
- **Consequence.** Because the kernel is a kernel, $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is automatically a *normal* subgroup of $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$, and the first isomorphism theorem gives $GL_2(\mathbb{R}) / SL_2(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^\times$.

**Worked Example:**

**Kernel of the sign homomorphism**

Let $\operatorname{sgn}: S_3 \to \{\pm 1\}$ send a permutation to $+1$ if it is even and $-1$ if it is odd.

- **Kernel.** $\ker(\operatorname{sgn}) = \{\sigma \in S_3 \mid \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) = +1\}$ = the even permutations = the alternating group $A_3$.
- **List them.** In $S_3$ the even permutations are the identity and the two $3$-cycles: $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$, so $|\ker(\operatorname{sgn})| = 3$.
- **Sanity check.** $|S_3| = 6$ and the image $\{\pm1\}$ has order $2$; the first isomorphism theorem forces $|\ker| = |S_3| / |\operatorname{im}| = 6/2 = 3$. ✓

## The isomorphism theorems {#isomorphism-theorems}

The three isomorphism theorems (together with the correspondence theorem) are the central structural results of group theory. They tell you exactly how subgroups, quotients, and homomorphic images of a group are related, and they let you translate statements about one into statements about the others with almost no extra work.

The first theorem is the most used: it identifies every homomorphic image with a quotient by the kernel. The second and third let you "cancel" subgroups in fractions of groups the way you cancel common factors in ordinary fractions. The correspondence theorem says that the subgroup lattice of a quotient is the interval in the original lattice lying above the kernel.

In this section you will learn the precise statements, the short proofs that rely only on the first isomorphism theorem plus the isomorphism theorems for the ambient group, and why these four results are the workhorses that appear in virtually every argument in the subject.

**First Isomorphism Theorem**: $G / \ker\phi \cong \operatorname{im}\phi$.

**Second Isomorphism Theorem**: If $H,K \leq G$ and $H$ normalizes $K$ (or one is normal), then $HK / K \cong H / (H \cap K)$.

**Third Isomorphism Theorem**: If $N \trianglelefteq G$ and $M/N \trianglelefteq G/N$ (i.e. $N \leq M \trianglelefteq G$), then $(G/N) / (M/N) \cong G/M$.

These, together with the lattice isomorphism (correspondence) theorem, are the workhorses of group theory.

**ELI5**

- The first says: "the group you get after crushing the kernel is exactly the picture you painted in the image." If a map forgets some information (its kernel), then the group with that information forgotten *is* the group of outputs.
- The third is the cancellation rule $\frac{a/c}{b/c} = \frac{a}{b}$ transplanted to groups: quotient by $N$, then quotient again by $M/N$, and the $N$'s cancel, leaving $G/M$.
- The second is the "diamond" rule: slide a subgroup $H$ up past a normal subgroup $K$; the jump from $K$ up to $HK$ looks identical to the jump from $H\cap K$ up to $H$ — the two parallel edges of the diamond are the same size and shape.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**First isomorphism theorem: identifying a quotient**

Let $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$ be $\phi(k) = 2k \bmod 8$. What is $\mathbb{Z}/\ker\phi$?

- **Verify it is a homomorphism.** $\phi(k+\ell) = 2(k+\ell) = 2k + 2\ell = \phi(k) + \phi(\ell) \pmod 8$. ✓
- **Kernel.** $2k \equiv 0 \pmod 8 \iff 8 \mid 2k \iff 4 \mid k$, so $\ker\phi = 4\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Image.** As $k$ ranges over $\mathbb{Z}$, $2k \bmod 8$ takes the values $\{0,2,4,6\}$ — the even residues — so $\operatorname{im}\phi$ has order $4$.
- **Apply the theorem.** $\mathbb{Z}/\ker\phi = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z} \cong \operatorname{im}\phi$, and both sides have order $4$. ✓ The theorem turned an infinite-domain map into a clean statement about a size-$4$ group.

**Worked Example:**

**Third isomorphism theorem: cancelling quotients**

Take $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. Let $N = \langle 6 \rangle = \{0,6\}$ and $M = \langle 2 \rangle = \{0,2,4,6,8,10\}$, so that $N \leq M \trianglelefteq G$ (everything is normal since $G$ is abelian).

- **The pieces.** $|N| = 2$, $|M| = 6$, so $M/N$ has order $6/2 = 3$, and $G/N$ has order $12/2 = 6$.
- **Apply the theorem.** $(G/N)/(M/N) \cong G/M$. The right side is $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z} \big/ \langle 2 \rangle$, which has order $12/6 = 2$, i.e. $\cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
- **Sanity check the sizes directly.** $|(G/N)/(M/N)| = |G/N| / |M/N| = 6/3 = 2$, matching $|G/M| = 2$. ✓ The $N$'s "cancel," exactly like $\frac{12/2}{6/2} = \frac{12}{6}$.

**Worked Example:**

**Second isomorphism theorem: a concrete diamond**

Inside $G = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ (abelian, so both subgroups are normal), take $H = \langle 3 \rangle = \{0,3,6,9\}$ and $K = \langle 4 \rangle = \{0,4,8\}$.

- **$HK$.** Since $G$ is abelian, $HK = H + K$ is the subgroup generated by $3$ and $4$; because $\gcd(3,4)=1$, it contains $1$, so $HK = G$, order $12$.
- **$H \cap K$.** An element in both $\langle 3\rangle$ and $\langle 4\rangle$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(3,4)=12 \equiv 0$, so $H \cap K = \{0\}$.
- **Apply the theorem.** $HK/K \cong H/(H\cap K)$ becomes $G/K \cong H/\{0\}$, i.e. $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\big/\langle 4\rangle \cong H$.
- **Check the orders.** Left: $|G/K| = 12/3 = 4$. Right: $|H| = 4$. ✓ Both are cyclic of order $4$.

## Automorphisms {#automorphisms}

We now specialize to maps from a group *to itself*. First recall the vocabulary. An **isomorphism** is a homomorphism that is also a bijection; when one exists between $G$ and $H$ we write $G \cong H$ and treat the two groups as "the same group under different names." An **automorphism** turns this idea inward: it is a relabeling of a single group's own elements that leaves the multiplication table intact. Studying these self-symmetries is how we measure how much internal symmetry a group has, and it is the engine behind semidirect products and the classification of many groups.

An **automorphism** of $G$ is an isomorphism $\phi: G \to G$.

The set of all automorphisms forms a group $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ under composition.

An **inner automorphism** is one of the form $\phi_g(x) = g x g^{-1}$ (conjugation by fixed $g$).

The inner automorphisms form a normal subgroup $\operatorname{Inn}(G) \trianglelefteq \operatorname{Aut}(G)$, and $\operatorname{Aut}(G)/\operatorname{Inn}(G) \cong \operatorname{Out}(G)$ (outer automorphism group).

For abelian groups, all inner autos are trivial (conjugation does nothing).

Examples:

- $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, order $\phi(n)$.
- $\operatorname{Aut}(S_3) \cong S_3$ (all autos inner).

**ELI5**

- An automorphism is a way to relabel the elements of the group so that the multiplication table looks identical — a symmetry of the group's own structure.
- Inner ones come from "rotating the labels using the group itself."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example:**

**Automorphisms of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$**

We find every automorphism of the additive cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$.

- **A homomorphism from a cyclic group is determined by where a generator goes.** Since $1$ generates the group, once we fix $\phi(1)$ the value $\phi(k) = k\cdot\phi(1)$ is forced for every $k$.
- **Which targets give a bijection?** For $\phi$ to be an automorphism, $\phi(1)$ must again be a generator, i.e. an element of order $6$. The elements of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ of order $6$ are exactly those coprime to $6$: that is $1$ and $5$ (the elements $2,3,4$ have smaller order, and $0$ has order $1$).
- **List the two automorphisms.** $\phi_1(k) = k$ (the identity) and $\phi_5(k) = 5k \bmod 6$. For instance $\phi_5$ sends $1\mapsto5,\ 2\mapsto4,\ 3\mapsto3,\ 4\mapsto2,\ 5\mapsto1$ — a genuine relabeling.
- **Identify the group.** $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})$ has $2$ elements, so $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. This matches the general fact $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$: the units mod $6$ are $\{1,5\}$. ✓

**Worked Example:**

**The size of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})$ via $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$**

- **Use the general theorem.** For any $n$, $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, the group of units mod $n$ under multiplication, whose order is Euler's totient $\varphi(n)$.
- **Count the units mod $8$.** The residues coprime to $8$ are $\{1,3,5,7\}$, so $\varphi(8) = 4$ and $|\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})| = 4$.
- **Caution — this is *not* $\cong \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$.** The automorphism group has order $4$, far smaller than the group's own order $8$. In general $\operatorname{Aut}(G) \not\cong G$; assuming so is a common trap.

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting to prove that a proposed map preserves the operation (the homomorphism property is the only thing you need to check; the rest follows).
- Claiming a map is an isomorphism just because it is bijective — you must verify it is a homomorphism (or its inverse is).
- Confusing kernel with the set of elements mapped to zero when the groups are additive.
- Thinking every homomorphism is surjective or injective. Most interesting ones are neither.
- Forgetting that kernels are normal (this is why we can form $G/\ker\phi$).
- Assuming $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ is always isomorphic to $G$ (false in general; e.g. for prime p, $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})$ has order p-1, not p).
