# Subgroups, Cosets, and Quotient Groups

Once you have a group, the next natural questions are: what are its "sub-collections" that are still groups? How do you "divide" a group by a subgroup? These ideas lead directly to Lagrange's theorem (one of the most useful counting results in algebra), normal subgroups, and quotient groups — the group-theoretic analogue of modular arithmetic.

Quotient groups let you "mod out" by a normal subgroup exactly as you mod out by $n$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. This construction is ubiquitous: it produces factor groups, simplifies symmetry, and appears in the isomorphism theorems and Galois theory.

## Subgroups {#subgroups}

Not every subset of a group forms a group on its own. The subsets that do — closed under the operation and inverses, and containing the identity — are called subgroups. They are the "symmetry sub-systems" inside a larger symmetry group. The one-step subgroup test gives an efficient way to check the axioms without verifying all four separately.

A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$ if $H$ is itself a group under the same operation as $G$.

The **subgroup test** (one-step): $H$ is a subgroup iff $H$ is nonempty and for all $a,b \in H$, $a b^{-1} \in H$.

Trivial subgroups: $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. Proper subgroups are neither.

Examples:

- $2\mathbb{Z}$ (even integers) is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
- The alternating group $A_n$ of even permutations is a subgroup of $S_n$ (index 2, hence normal).
- In $(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the set $\{0,3,6,9\}$ is a subgroup.

:::intuition
A subgroup is a "club inside the bigger club" that is closed under the club's rules and has its own "do nothing" and "undo" moves that work inside the club.

If the big group is all dance moves, a subgroup might be the moves that preserve a certain pattern (e.g. only rotations, no reflections).
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Checking $3\mathbb{Z}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ under +]
We use the one-step test. Here the operation is $+$, so "$ab^{-1}$" means $a - b$ (the inverse of $b$ under addition is $-b$).
:::

:::solution
Nonempty: $0 = 3\cdot 0 \in 3\mathbb{Z}$, so $3\mathbb{Z} \neq \varnothing$.

Closed under $a - b$: take any $a = 3m$ and $b = 3n$ with $m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then
$$a - b = 3m - 3n = 3(m-n),$$
and since $m - n \in \mathbb{Z}$, this is again a multiple of $3$, i.e. $a - b \in 3\mathbb{Z}$.

Conclusion: both conditions of the one-step test hold, so $3\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Z}$.

Sanity check: the test automatically gives us the identity ($a - a = 0 \in 3\mathbb{Z}$) and inverses ($0 - a = -a = 3(-m) \in 3\mathbb{Z}$), which is why one line replaced all four axioms.
:::

:::example[A subset that fails: the positive integers in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$]
Let $H = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. It is nonempty and closed under addition, so a beginner might guess it is a subgroup. But the one-step test needs $a - b \in H$: take $a = 1$, $b = 2$, then $a - b = -1 \notin H$. Equivalently, $H$ has no identity ($0 \notin H$) and no inverses. So closure under the operation alone is **not** enough — this is exactly the trap in the Common Mistakes list.
:::

## Cosets and Lagrange's theorem {#lagrange}

Once you have a subgroup, the next natural objects are its cosets—the "parallel copies" of the subgroup obtained by translating it by elements of the big group. Cosets partition the group into equal-sized pieces; their number (the index) is therefore a divisor of the group order when the group is finite. This simple counting fact is Lagrange's theorem, one of the most frequently applied results in all of finite group theory.

The proof is almost immediate once you observe that left (or right) multiplication by a fixed element is a bijection, so every coset has exactly the same cardinality as the subgroup. The consequences for element orders, possible subgroup sizes, and the structure of finite groups are immediate and powerful.

In this section you will learn the definition of left and right cosets, prove they partition the group, derive Lagrange's theorem together with its standard corollaries, and see how the index controls the size of quotient sets even before you have a group structure on them.

Let $H \leq G$ and $g \in G$. The **left coset** of $H$ containing $g$ is $gH = \{gh \mid h \in H\}$. Right coset $Hg$.

Cosets partition $G$: every element is in exactly one left (resp. right) coset. The number of distinct left cosets is the **index** $[G:H]$.

**Lagrange's theorem**: If $G$ is finite and $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and $[G:H] = |G|/|H|$.

Consequences:

- Order of any element divides $|G|$.
- $|G|$ is divisible by the order of every subgroup.
- Possible subgroup orders are the divisors of $|G|$.

:::intuition
Cosets are like "parallel copies" of the subgroup. If you take the evens inside integers, the cosets are the evens and the odds. Lagrange says the size of any such copy divides the total size — you can't have a club whose size doesn't fit evenly into the whole population.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Listing the cosets of $H = \{0,3\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ and reading off the index]
Take $G = \mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z} = \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ under addition mod $6$, and $H = \{0,3\}$ (order $2$). The left coset of an element $g$ is $g + H$.
:::

:::solution
Compute cosets until $G$ is exhausted:
$$0 + H = \{0,3\},\quad 1 + H = \{1,4\},\quad 2 + H = \{2,5\}.$$
Every element of $G$ now appears, so we stop. (Note $3 + H = \{3,0\} = 0 + H$, confirming cosets repeat rather than give new sets.)

Check the partition: $\{0,3\}$, $\{1,4\}$, $\{2,5\}$ are disjoint and their union is all of $G$. So the three cosets partition the six elements into blocks of size $|H| = 2$.

Read off the index and verify Lagrange: there are $3$ distinct cosets, so $[G:H] = 3$, and indeed
$$[G:H] = \frac{|G|}{|H|} = \frac{6}{2} = 3.\ \checkmark$$
:::

:::example[Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$]
Lagrange says every subgroup order must divide $|G| = 6$, so the only candidate orders are the divisors $1, 2, 3, 6$. Because $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic, there is in fact exactly one subgroup of each divisor order:
:::

:::solution
Order 1: $\{0\}$ (the trivial subgroup).
Order 2: $\{0,3\}$ (generated by $3$, since $3 + 3 = 6 \equiv 0$).
Order 3: $\{0,2,4\}$ (generated by $2$: $2, 4, 6\equiv 0$).
Order 6: the whole group (generated by $1$).

Sanity check: each listed order — $1, 2, 3, 6$ — divides $6$, as Lagrange requires. (The converse, "every divisor gives a subgroup," is special to cyclic groups; it can fail in general, as the $A_4$ example in Common Mistakes shows.)
:::

## Normal subgroups and quotient groups {#quotients}

Not every subgroup can be "divided out" to produce a quotient group; only the normal ones can. Normality is the precise condition that makes left and right cosets coincide, so that the set of cosets inherits a well-defined group operation from G. Quotient groups are the group-theoretic version of modular arithmetic and are the source of almost all the groups you meet in nature (Z/nZ, symmetry groups of objects, Galois groups, etc.).

A subgroup N is normal when it is invariant under conjugation by every element of G. In abelian groups every subgroup is automatically normal; in non-abelian groups normality is a strong symmetry condition. Once you have a normal subgroup the quotient map is a surjective homomorphism whose kernel is exactly N, and the first isomorphism theorem applies.

In this section you will learn the definition and equivalent characterisations of normality, how to form the quotient group G/N, verify the operation is well-defined, and see the universal example of Z/nZ arising as Z modulo the normal subgroup nZ.

A subgroup $N \leq G$ is **normal** ($N \trianglelefteq G$) if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g \in G$ (left and right cosets coincide: $gN = Ng$).

Equivalently: $g n g^{-1} \in N$ for all $g,n$.

The **quotient group** $G/N$ is the set of (left) cosets of $N$, with operation $(gN)(hN) = (gh)N$. This is well-defined precisely when $N$ is normal.

The quotient map $\pi: G \to G/N$, $g \mapsto gN$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $N$.

:::intuition
Normal subgroups are "symmetric" with respect to conjugation by the whole group — conjugation ($g n g^{-1}$) means "view the move $n$ from $g$'s point of view," and normality says that changing viewpoint never takes you out of $N$. Because of that, you can "move $N$ around" with any group element and it looks the same.

The quotient is like a clock: on a 12-hour clock you treat $12$ o'clock and $0$ o'clock as the same, because you're throwing away multiples of $12$. Forming $G/N$ does the same thing — you glue together any two elements that differ by something in $N$. In fact $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ literally **is** clock arithmetic: $\mathbb{Z}$ quotiented by the normal subgroup $12\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>$n\mathbb{Z} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. For any positive integer $n$, the subgroup $n\mathbb{Z}$ of multiples of $n$ is normal in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ (since $\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian, all subgroups are normal). The quotient $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the cyclic group of residues modulo $n$.
:::

:::example[Building the quotient $S_3 / A_3$ and its multiplication]
Let $G = S_3$ (the six permutations of $\{1,2,3\}$) and $N = A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$, the three even permutations.
:::

:::solution
Check $N$ is normal. $A_3$ has index $[G:N] = 6/3 = 2$, and any subgroup of index $2$ is automatically normal (its two left cosets are $N$ and "everything else," which must equal the two right cosets). So the quotient exists.

List the cosets. With any transposition, say $\tau = (1\,2)$:
$$N = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}\ \text{(the even permutations)},$$
$$\tau N = \{(1\,2), (1\,3), (2\,3)\}\ \text{(the odd permutations)}.$$
These two cosets partition the six elements, so $|G/N| = 2$.

Multiply cosets using $(gN)(hN) = (gh)N$. Writing $E = N$ (even) and $O = \tau N$ (odd):
$$E\cdot E = E,\quad E\cdot O = O,\quad O\cdot E = O,\quad O\cdot O = E.$$
For instance $O \cdot O$: pick representatives $(1\,2)$ and $(1\,3)$; their product $(1\,2)(1\,3) = (1\,3\,2)$ is even, landing in $E$. This is just the "even $\times$ odd" sign rule.

Identify the result. The table above is exactly the group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (with $E \leftrightarrow 0$, $O \leftrightarrow 1$), so $S_3/A_3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

Sanity check on well-definedness: in Step 3 we chose specific representatives, but any even times any odd is odd, so the product coset never depends on the choice — precisely the guarantee normality provides.
:::

## Sylow theorems and simple groups {#sylow}

For finite groups the Sylow theorems give extraordinarily precise counting information about the p-subgroups (subgroups whose order is the highest power of a given prime p dividing |G|). They are the main tool for proving existence of normal subgroups in finite groups and for classifying groups of small order.

The three theorems together say that Sylow p-subgroups exist, that they are all conjugate (hence "the same up to relabelling"), and that the number of them satisfies strong congruence and divisibility conditions. A group with no nontrivial normal subgroups is called simple; the classification of finite simple groups is one of the great achievements of twentieth-century mathematics, and the Sylow theorems are indispensable in the proof.

In this section you will learn the statements of the Sylow theorems, the definition of simple groups, and how these results are used to force normal subgroups or to prove that certain groups must be semidirect products.

Let $G$ be finite of order $p^k m$ with $p \nmid m$.

A **Sylow $p$-subgroup** is a subgroup of order $p^k$ (maximal $p$-power order).

**Sylow theorems**:

1. Sylow $p$-subgroups exist.
2. All Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate.
3. The number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ and $n_p$ divides $m$.

A group is **simple** if it has no nontrivial normal subgroups.

- $A_5$ is the smallest non-abelian simple group (order 60).
- Simple groups are the "building blocks" in the classification of finite groups (Jordan–Hölder).

:::intuition
Think of prime-power "building blocks" you must pack inside a group. Sylow says: blocks of the biggest size for each prime always fit (they exist), they're all interchangeable (conjugate), and the number of copies is pinned down so tightly by two rules ($\equiv 1 \bmod p$ and divides $m$) that often only **one** value is possible. When only one copy fits, it can't be shuffled around, so it's stuck in place — that's a normal subgroup handed to you for free.

A simple group has no "normal pieces" you can quotient by — it is like a prime number of groups: you can't break it into a smaller normal factor. Simple groups are the atoms, and every finite group is built from them.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Sylow subgroups of $S_3$ (both primes)]
Factor the order: $|S_3| = 6 = 2 \cdot 3$. So the primes to consider are $p = 2$ (with $p^k = 2^1$, $m = 3$) and $p = 3$ (with $p^k = 3^1$, $m = 2$).
:::

:::solution
Sylow 2-subgroups (order $2$): the third Sylow theorem forces $n_2 \equiv 1 \pmod 2$ and $n_2 \mid 3$, so $n_2 \in \{1, 3\}$. Each order-2 subgroup is $\{e, t\}$ for a transposition $t$, and there are three transpositions, giving
$$\{e,(1\,2)\},\quad \{e,(1\,3)\},\quad \{e,(2\,3)\}.$$
Hence $n_2 = 3$. Check: $3 \equiv 1 \pmod 2$ $\checkmark$ and $3 \mid 3$ $\checkmark$. By Sylow II they are all conjugate (indeed conjugating $(1\,2)$ by $(2\,3)$ gives $(1\,3)$).

Sylow 3-subgroups (order $3$): now $n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ and $n_3 \mid 2$, so $n_3 \in \{1\}$ (the value $2$ is excluded since $2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$). Thus $n_3 = 1$: the unique Sylow 3-subgroup is $A_3 = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$.

Sanity check / payoff: because $n_3 = 1$, that single Sylow 3-subgroup has no distinct conjugates, so it is **normal** — which is exactly why $A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$ and the quotient $S_3/A_3$ made sense above.
:::

:::example[Using Sylow to force a normal subgroup in a group of order 15]
Let $|G| = 15 = 3 \cdot 5$. We show $G$ must have a normal subgroup of order $5$ (in fact $G$ is forced to be cyclic).
:::

:::solution
1. Count Sylow 5-subgroups: $n_5 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$ and $n_5 \mid 3$. The divisors of $3$ are $1, 3$; of these only $1 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. So $n_5 = 1$.
2. A unique Sylow subgroup is normal (no other conjugates to move to), so the order-$5$ subgroup is normal in $G$.
3. Step 3 (bonus) — Likewise $n_3 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ and $n_3 \mid 5$ gives $n_3 \in \{1\}$ (since $5 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3$), so the Sylow 3-subgroup is also unique and normal. Two normal subgroups of coprime orders $3$ and $5$ meeting only in $e$ force $G \cong \mathbb{Z}/3 \times \mathbb{Z}/5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/15$.
4. This is the archetype of how Sylow "forces" normal subgroups from nothing but the order of the group.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing "subgroup" with "subset closed under the operation" — you must also have the identity and inverses (or use the one-step test correctly).
- Assuming every subgroup is normal. $H = \{e, (12)\}$ is not normal in $S_3$.
- Forgetting that Lagrange gives a necessary condition on orders but not sufficient (there may be no subgroup of a given divisor order, e.g. no subgroup of order 6 in $A_4$).
- Writing $G/H$ without checking normality first.
- Thinking all groups of prime order are cyclic — they are, but that's a consequence of Lagrange + cyclic generation.
