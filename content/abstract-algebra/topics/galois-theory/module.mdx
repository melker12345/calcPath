# Field Extensions and Galois Theory

Galois theory is the jewel of classical abstract algebra. It establishes a deep dictionary between field extensions and groups (the Galois groups), allowing one to translate hard questions about solvability of polynomials into questions about finite groups.

The crowning achievement is the proof that there is no general formula by radicals for solving polynomial equations of degree 5 or higher — the general quintic is not solvable by radicals — because symmetric groups S_n for n ≥ 5 are not solvable groups.

This topic introduces the language of extensions, algebraic elements, splitting fields, Galois groups, and the fundamental theorem that gives the lattice anti-isomorphism between subfields and subgroups.

## Field extensions {#field-extensions}

Field extensions are the algebraic setting in which we adjoin new elements (roots, transcendental quantities) to a base field while keeping all the field operations. They are the direct generalisation of "number systems built on top of the rationals" and are the objects Galois theory ultimately classifies by their symmetry groups.

The degree [K : F] measures the "size" of the extension as a vector-space dimension; when the degree is finite the extension is called finite. Not every finite extension is obtained by adjoining a single element, but many important ones are. Transcendental extensions (such as the reals over the rationals) have infinite degree and lie outside the reach of classical Galois theory.

In this section you will learn the basic vocabulary of extensions and degree, the distinction between algebraic and transcendental elements, and the notion of a simple extension that prepares the ground for the Galois correspondence.

A **field extension** $K/F$ is a field $K$ containing $F$ as a subfield. We call $F$ the **base field** and $K$ the **top field**.

The **degree** $[K : F]$ is the dimension of $K$ as a vector space over $F$. (Every field $K$ containing $F$ is automatically an $F$-vector space: you can add elements of $K$ and scale them by elements of $F$.) If this dimension is finite we call the extension **finite**.

- A finite extension is always algebraic, but the converse fails: an algebraic extension can be infinite (transcendental extensions, whose adjoined elements satisfy no polynomial over $F$, always have infinite degree).
- **Simple extension:** $K = F(\alpha)$, the smallest field containing both $F$ and a single element $\alpha$.
- If $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$ with minimal polynomial of degree $n$, then $[F(\alpha):F] = n$, and $\{1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}\}$ (the **power basis**) is a basis. Any higher power $\alpha^n$ is rewritten in lower powers using the minimal-polynomial relation.

A tool you will use constantly is the **tower law**: if $F \subseteq E \subseteq K$ are fields, then
$$[K:F] = [K:E]\,[E:F].$$
Degrees multiply up a tower of extensions.

Examples: $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ has infinite degree (it contains transcendentals like $\pi$); $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ has degree $2$ with basis $\{1, i\}$; $\mathbb{F}_p(\alpha)$ where $\alpha$ is a root of an irreducible polynomial of degree $d$ has degree $d$.

:::intuition
A field extension is a bigger "number world" that contains your original field. The degree is how many "independent directions" (basis vectors) you need to pin down a new number using only old ones. Just as a point in a plane needs two coordinates over the reals, every element of $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ needs two rational coordinates $(a, b)$ to write it as $a + bi$ — so the degree is $2$. The tower law is like nested boxes: to count directions from the ground floor to the roof, multiply the directions on each staircase between floors.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>$\mathbb{Q}(i)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has degree 2</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. The minimal polynomial of $i$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x^2 + 1$. It is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ because it has no rational (indeed no real) root, so it cannot factor into two rational linear factors.
2. Because the minimal polynomial has degree $2$, the power basis is $\{1, i\}$, so every element is $a + bi$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$.
3. Sanity check — $i^2 = -1$ reduces back into the base field, so we never need $i^2$ as a separate basis element. The two directions $1$ and $i$ suffice. Hence $[\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$. $\checkmark$
:::

:::example
<strong>Degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ via the tower law</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. Build the extension one root at a time: $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$.
2. The bottom step: $\sqrt{2}$ has minimal polynomial $x^2 - 2$ (irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by the rational root test), so $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$.
3. The top step: over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, does $x^2 - 3$ stay irreducible? It would factor only if $\sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. Suppose $\sqrt{3} = a + b\sqrt{2}$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. Squaring gives $3 = a^2 + 2b^2 + 2ab\sqrt{2}$, forcing $ab = 0$. If $b = 0$ then $\sqrt{3} = a$ is rational (false); if $a = 0$ then $3 = 2b^2$, i.e. $\sqrt{3/2}$ rational (false). So $\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and $x^2 - 3$ is still irreducible there: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})] = 2$.
4. Tower law: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$, with basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{6}\}$.
5. Sanity check: this is the splitting field of $(x^2-2)(x^2-3)$ (question `abstract-alg-2`), and indeed a degree-$4$ answer matches. $\checkmark$
:::

## Algebraic extensions {#algebraic}

An element is algebraic over a field F when it satisfies a polynomial equation with coefficients in F. The algebraic numbers (roots of polynomials over Q) form an enormous field that is the algebraic closure of Q inside C; most explicit numbers you meet in algebra are algebraic.

The minimal polynomial is the monic polynomial of least degree that annihilates the element; it is irreducible and unique. Adjoining one algebraic element produces a finite extension whose degree equals the degree of that minimal polynomial. Not every algebraic extension is finite (the algebraic closure of Q is algebraic but infinite), but every finite extension is algebraic.

In this section you will learn the definitions of algebraic element and algebraic extension, the existence and uniqueness of minimal polynomials, and the relationship between degree and the splitting behaviour of polynomials that prepares for Galois groups.

An element $\alpha \in K$ is **algebraic** over $F$ if it is a root of some nonzero polynomial in $F[x]$. Otherwise it is **transcendental** over $F$.

The **minimal polynomial** $m(x) \in F[x]$ is the monic polynomial of least degree having $\alpha$ as a root. Two facts make it well-behaved: it is **irreducible** over $F$ (if it factored, one factor would be a lower-degree polynomial with $\alpha$ as a root, contradicting minimality), and it is **unique** (any two monic polynomials of least degree annihilating $\alpha$ differ by a lower-degree remainder that must be $0$). Moreover, any polynomial in $F[x]$ with $\alpha$ as a root is a multiple of $m(x)$.

$K/F$ is an **algebraic extension** if every element of $K$ is algebraic over $F$.

- Every finite extension is algebraic (if $[K:F] = n$, then for any $\alpha \in K$ the $n+1$ powers $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^n$ are linearly dependent over $F$, giving a polynomial relation).
- Algebraic extensions need not be finite (e.g. the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ of $\mathbb{Q}$ is algebraic but of infinite degree, since it contains $\sqrt[n]{2}$ for every $n$).
- If $\alpha$ is algebraic, $F(\alpha)/F$ is finite (of degree $\deg m$) and hence algebraic.

The **splitting field** of a polynomial $f \in F[x]$ over $F$ is the smallest extension of $F$ in which $f$ factors completely into linear factors — equivalently, the field generated over $F$ by all roots of $f$.

:::intuition
Algebraic numbers are the "reachable" numbers: those you can trap as a root of a polynomial with rational coefficients ($\sqrt{2}$, the golden ratio, $\sqrt[3]{5}$). Transcendental numbers like $\pi$ and $e$ slip through every such net.

The minimal polynomial is a number's "shortest fingerprint" over $F$: the simplest polynomial equation it satisfies. Every other equation it satisfies is just a multiple of this one.

The splitting field is the smallest world in which your polynomial "shatters all the way" into $(x - \text{root})$ pieces — you keep adjoining missing roots until nothing is left to factor.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>Minimal polynomial of $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. Let $\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$. Square it: $\alpha^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$.
2. Isolate the radical: $\alpha^2 - 5 = 2\sqrt{6}$. Square again: $(\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, i.e. $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 25 = 24$, so $\alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0$.
3. So $\alpha$ is a root of $m(x) = x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$. This is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$: it has no rational roots (the rational root test allows only $\pm 1$, and $1 - 10 + 1 \neq 0$), and one checks it has no quadratic factorization over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hence $\deg m = 4$.
4. Sanity check against the tower law — $\alpha$ lives in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$, which has degree $4$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (previous section). In fact $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$ matches $\deg m = 4$. $\checkmark$
:::

:::example
<strong>Splitting field of $(x^2 - 2)(x^2 - 3)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. The roots are $\pm\sqrt{2}$ and $\pm\sqrt{3}$. To split completely we must adjoin $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ (their negatives come for free once we have the field operations). So the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$.
2. From the tower-law example above, $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.
3. Final answer: the splitting field has degree $4$. $\checkmark$
:::

:::example
<strong>Splitting field of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ (degree 6)</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. The roots are $\sqrt[3]{2}$, $\omega\sqrt[3]{2}$, $\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$, where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a primitive cube root of unity. Adjoining just the real root $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not enough — the other two roots are complex, so $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ does not contain them.
2. The splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$. Compute its degree by a tower. First $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$, since $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisenstein at $p = 2$).
3. Now adjoin $\omega$, whose minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x^2 + x + 1$ (degree $2$). Since $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subset \mathbb{R}$ but $\omega \notin \mathbb{R}$, we have $\omega \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, so $x^2 + x + 1$ stays irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$: the top step has degree $2$.
4. Tower law: $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega):\mathbb{Q}] = 3 \cdot 2 = 6$. This matches the fact that the Galois group embeds in $S_3$ (permutations of the three roots) and here fills it out completely, giving $|S_3| = 6$. $\checkmark$
:::

## Galois groups {#galois-group}

Before the definition, the two words in the hypothesis. An extension is **normal** if whenever an irreducible polynomial over $F$ has one root in $K$, it has *all* its roots in $K$ (splitting fields are exactly the normal extensions). It is **separable** if the minimal polynomial of every element has no repeated roots (automatic in characteristic $0$ and over finite fields). An extension that is finite, normal, and separable is called a **Galois extension** — these are precisely the extensions to which the theory applies cleanly.

Let $K/F$ be a Galois extension.

The **Galois group** $\mathrm{Gal}(K/F) = \mathrm{Aut}(K/F) = \{$field automorphisms of $K$ that fix $F$ pointwise$\}$. ("Fixes $F$ pointwise" means $\sigma(a) = a$ for every $a \in F$.)

- It is a finite group of order exactly $[K:F]$ (this equality is what "Galois" guarantees; for a general finite extension one only has $|\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)| \le [K:F]$).
- The splitting field of a separable polynomial is always a Galois extension.
- **Action on roots:** any $\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$ permutes the roots of every polynomial over $F$, because applying $\sigma$ to $f(\alpha) = 0$ with coefficients fixed gives $f(\sigma\alpha) = 0$. So an automorphism is completely determined by where it sends a generating set of roots — this is why Galois groups embed into permutation groups.

For a quadratic $x^2 - d$ (with $\sqrt{d} \notin F$), $\mathrm{Gal} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: the nontrivial element is the conjugation sending $\sqrt{d} \mapsto -\sqrt{d}$.

For cyclotomic fields, $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, the group of units mod $n$. The automorphism $\sigma_k$ sends $\zeta_n \mapsto \zeta_n^k$ for each $k$ coprime to $n$; composition of $\sigma_j$ and $\sigma_k$ multiplies exponents, matching multiplication mod $n$.

:::intuition
A Galois group is the collection of all "symmetries" of the big field that leave the base field frozen in place. Think of the roots of a polynomial as interchangeable puzzle pieces: a symmetry may swap $\sqrt{2}$ with $-\sqrt{2}$, but it can never turn a root into a non-root, and it must respect every equation the base field already knows.

The group "measures the ambiguity" in the roots — how many ways you can relabel them without any equation over $F$ noticing the difference. If the base field can already tell two roots apart (like $\sqrt{2}$ vs. $\sqrt{3}$), no symmetry may swap them.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(i)/\mathbb{Q}) = \{\mathrm{id}, \sigma\}$ where $\sigma(a+bi) = a - bi$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. Any $\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}$ must fix $\mathbb{Q}$ and send $i$ to another root of its minimal polynomial $x^2 + 1$. The roots are $i$ and $-i$, so there are at most two choices: $\sigma(i) = i$ or $\sigma(i) = -i$.
2. $\sigma(i) = i$ gives the identity. $\sigma(i) = -i$ gives complex conjugation $a + bi \mapsto a - bi$; one checks it respects addition and multiplication, so it is a genuine automorphism.
3. Sanity check — the group has order $2$, matching $[\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}] = 2$, and $\mathrm{Gal} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. $\checkmark$
:::

:::example
<strong>$\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)/\mathbb{Q}) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. $\zeta_5 = e^{2\pi i/5}$ is a primitive $5$th root of unity; its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_5(x) = x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$ (degree $4$). So $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$.
2. An automorphism must send $\zeta_5$ to another primitive $5$th root, i.e. $\zeta_5^k$ for $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ (these are exactly the residues coprime to $5$). Call this $\sigma_k$.
3. The group is $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ under multiplication mod $5$, which is cyclic of order $4$ (generated by $2$: $2, 4, 3, 1$). So $\mathrm{Gal} \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.
4. Sanity check — $|(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times| = \varphi(5) = 4$, which matches the degree $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$. $\checkmark$
:::

## The fundamental theorem of Galois theory {#fundamental-theorem}

Let $K/F$ be Galois with $G = \mathrm{Gal}(K/F)$.

There is a lattice-reversing bijection (an **anti-isomorphism** of lattices):
$$\{\text{intermediate fields } E : F \subseteq E \subseteq K\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \{\text{subgroups } H \le G\}.$$
It is given in both directions by $E \mapsto \mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ and $H \mapsto$ the **fixed field** $K^H = \{x \in K : \sigma(x) = x \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}$. "Lattice-reversing" means inclusion flips: $E_1 \subseteq E_2 \iff \mathrm{Gal}(K/E_2) \subseteq \mathrm{Gal}(K/E_1)$.

Properties:

- $[K:E] = |\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)|$ (the top piece), and $[E:F] = [G : \mathrm{Gal}(K/E)]$ (the index of the corresponding subgroup).
- $E/F$ is normal (Galois) if and only if $\mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$ is a **normal** subgroup of $G$; and then $\mathrm{Gal}(E/F) \cong G / \mathrm{Gal}(K/E)$.

The practical payoff: to find every intermediate field of a Galois extension, you list the subgroups of $G$ — a finite, purely group-theoretic task — and take fixed fields. The two endpoints always correspond: $H = \{1\}$ gives $K^{\{1\}} = K$, and $H = G$ gives $K^G = F$.

:::intuition
Think of the base field $F$ as fully "locked" and the top field $K$ as fully "free." A subgroup is a bag of symmetries; the more symmetries you keep, the more of $K$ gets held fixed, so the smaller the fixed subfield. Big group $\leftrightarrow$ small field, small group $\leftrightarrow$ big field — the ladder is flipped upside down.

Normal subgroups are the "self-contained" bags of symmetries; they correspond to the intermediate fields that are themselves nicely symmetric (Galois) over $F$, and the leftover quotient group is that field's own Galois group.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>Quadratic case: $G \cong C_2$</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. $G = \{1, \sigma\} \cong C_2$ has exactly two subgroups: $\{1\}$ and $G$ itself.
2. Their fixed fields are $K^{\{1\}} = K$ (the identity fixes everything) and $K^G = F$ (only the base survives all symmetries).
3. So there are no *proper* intermediate fields — only $F$ and $K$ — matching the fact that $C_2$ has no proper nontrivial subgroups. $\checkmark$
:::

:::example
<strong>$x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: counting intermediate fields ($G \cong S_3$)</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. The splitting field $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ has degree $6$ (earlier example), and $G = \mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$ acts by permuting the three roots.
2. List all subgroups of $S_3$: the trivial group $\{1\}$; three subgroups of order $2$ generated by the transpositions $\langle(12)\rangle, \langle(13)\rangle, \langle(23)\rangle$; one subgroup of order $3$, namely $A_3 = \langle(123)\rangle$; and $S_3$ itself. That is $6$ subgroups.
3. By the correspondence there are $6$ intermediate fields total, so $6 - 2 = 4$ lie *strictly* between $\mathbb{Q}$ and $K$.
4. Identify them. The order-$3$ subgroup $A_3$ has index $2$, so its fixed field has degree $2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ — it is $\mathbb{Q}(\omega) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$. The three order-$2$ subgroups each have index $3$, giving three cubic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\omega\sqrt[3]{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\omega^2\sqrt[3]{2})$. That is $1 + 3 = 4$ proper intermediate fields. $\checkmark$ (This is question `abstract-fund-2`.)
5. Note the direction check: the *normal* subgroup $A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$ corresponds to the field $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, which is Galois (normal) over $\mathbb{Q}$; the three non-normal order-$2$ subgroups correspond to the three cubic fields, which are *not* normal over $\mathbb{Q}$.
:::

:::example
<strong>Klein four group $V_4 = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$: intermediate fields</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. A concrete model is $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})$ with $G = \mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}) \cong V_4$, the automorphisms independently flipping the signs of $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$.
2. $V_4$ has exactly three subgroups of order $2$ (plus $\{1\}$ and the whole group), so there are three proper intermediate fields, each of degree $2$.
3. They are $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$, and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{6})$ — the fixed fields of the three order-$2$ subgroups. Final answer: $3$ strictly intermediate fields. $\checkmark$ (This is question `abstract-gal-NEW4`.)
:::

## Solvability by radicals and quintics (sketch) {#solvable}

A polynomial is **solvable by radicals** if its roots can be expressed using $+, -, \times, \div$ and $n$th roots $\sqrt[n]{\;}$, starting from the coefficients.

**Galois's criterion:** a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting field is a **solvable group** — meaning it has a chain of subgroups $\{1\} = G_0 \trianglelefteq G_1 \trianglelefteq \dots \trianglelefteq G_m = G$ in which each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ is abelian. Intuitively, taking one $n$th root corresponds to one abelian ("cyclic") layer, so a radical expression exists exactly when the group can be built up from abelian layers.

- $S_n$ for $n \le 4$ is solvable $\Rightarrow$ quadratics, cubics, and quartics have radical formulas (the quadratic formula, Cardano for cubics, Ferrari for quartics).
- $S_n$ for $n \ge 5$ is not solvable: it has $A_n$ as a composition factor, and $A_n$ is simple and non-abelian for $n \ge 5$, so it cannot be broken into abelian layers. Hence the general quintic is not solvable by radicals.

This is the historical origin of Galois theory (Galois's criterion, building on Abel and Ruffini, who first proved the quintic's unsolvability).

:::intuition
Solving by radicals is like unlocking a safe by peeling off layers, one "take an $n$th root" at a time — and each layer must be simple (abelian). For degree $5$, the symmetries of the five roots include $A_5$, a knot with no loose thread: it has no proper nontrivial normal subgroup to peel off. Because you cannot break it into abelian layers, no finite tower of root-extractions can reach the roots in general.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example
<strong>Why $S_4$ is solvable but $S_5$ is not</strong>
:::

:::solution
1. For $S_4$, take the chain $\{1\} \trianglelefteq V_4 \trianglelefteq A_4 \trianglelefteq S_4$, where $V_4$ is the Klein four subgroup $\{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$. Check the quotients: $S_4/A_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ (abelian), $A_4/V_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$ (abelian), and $V_4/\{1\} \cong V_4$ (abelian). Every factor is abelian, so $S_4$ is solvable. Any quartic — whose Galois group is a subgroup of $S_4$, and subgroups of solvable groups are solvable — is therefore solvable by radicals (Ferrari's formula).
2. For $S_5$, its only composition series passes through $A_5$: $\{1\} \trianglelefteq A_5 \trianglelefteq S_5$. The quotient $S_5/A_5 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ is abelian, but $A_5$ (order $60$) is simple and non-abelian, so it is its own only nontrivial "factor" and that factor is not abelian.
3. A solvable group must have *all* composition factors abelian. Since $A_5$ is a non-abelian composition factor of $S_5$, $S_5$ is not solvable.
4. Consequence — a quintic whose Galois group is $S_5$ (for instance $x^5 - x - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, which has Galois group $S_5$) has no formula for its roots in terms of radicals. This is exactly question `abstract-galgrp-3`. $\checkmark$
5. Caveat: this fails only for the *general* quintic. Specific quintics can have solvable Galois groups — e.g. $x^5 - 1$ has cyclotomic (hence abelian, hence solvable) Galois group $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$, and its roots are radicals ($5$th roots of unity).
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing algebraic with finite: an algebraic extension can be infinite (union of all finite ones).
- Forgetting separability: in char p there are inseparable extensions; Galois theory in full generality requires normal + separable.
- Assuming every extension is Galois (e.g. the splitting field of an irreducible cubic may be degree 6 while a subextension by one root has degree 3 and is not normal).
- Mixing up fixed fields and Galois correspondence direction: larger field ↔ smaller group.
- Thinking the Galois group order is always the degree of the minimal polynomial of one primitive element (it is the degree of the whole extension).
- Claiming all quintics are unsolvable — only the general one; many specific quintics have solvable Gal groups (e.g. x^5 - x -1 may or may not, but x^5-1 is cyclotomic solvable).
