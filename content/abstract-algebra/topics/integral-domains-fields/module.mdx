# Integral Domains and Fields

Integral domains are the "number rings" without zero-divisors — the natural home of unique factorization ideas. Fields are the rings in which you can divide by every nonzero element; they are the analogues of the rational, real, and complex numbers.

The relationship is tight: every field is an integral domain, and the quotient of a domain by a maximal ideal is a field. The field of fractions construction lets you "localize" any domain into a field (like Z to Q).

Characteristic is a fundamental invariant that controls the arithmetic inside the structure (e.g. 1+1+...+1 =0 in finite fields).

## Integral domains {#domains}

Within rings, some are "well-behaved" for multiplication in the sense that you cannot have two nonzero elements whose product is zero. Such rings are called integral domains; they are the natural setting in which unique factorization and divisibility theory can be developed without extra complications.

An **integral domain** is a commutative ring with $1$ in which $1 \neq 0$ and there are no zero-divisors: that is, $ab = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. (The condition $1 \neq 0$ rules out the trivial zero ring; without it the definition would be vacuous.)

Examples: $\mathbb{Z}$, any field, and polynomial rings over a domain (such as $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ or $k[x]$ for a field $k$).

Non-examples include rings with zero-divisors such as $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (where $2 \cdot 3 = 0$) or the ring of $2 \times 2$ matrices over $\mathbb{R}$ (where $E_{12}E_{21}$-type products vanish).

**Cancellation** is valid in integral domains: if $a \neq 0$ and $ab = ac$, then $b = c$. This is exactly what "no zero-divisors" buys you, even though most domains have no inverses to divide by.

:::intuition
An integral domain is a place where multiplication behaves like ordinary integers: no "weird" nonzero things that multiply to zero and destroy information.

You can safely cancel nonzero factors.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Polynomial rings $k[x]$ over a field $k$ are integral domains.]
Let $f, g \in k[x]$ be nonzero. Write $f = a_m x^m + \dots$ with leading coefficient $a_m \neq 0$ (degree $m$) and $g = b_n x^n + \dots$ with leading coefficient $b_n \neq 0$ (degree $n$). The top-degree term of the product $fg$ is $a_m b_n \, x^{m+n}$, and its coefficient is $a_m b_n$.
:::

:::solution
1. Because $k$ is a field, it is a domain, so $a_m \neq 0$ and $b_n \neq 0$ force $a_m b_n \neq 0$. Hence $fg$ has a nonzero coefficient in degree $m + n$, so $fg \neq 0$. No two nonzero polynomials multiply to $0$, so $k[x]$ has no zero-divisors.
2. Note the identity $\deg(fg) = \deg f + \deg g$ we just used *requires* $k$ to be a domain — over $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ it fails, since $(2x)(2x) = 4x^2 = 0$.
3. Sanity check: with $k = \mathbb{Q}$, $(x-1)(x+1) = x^2 - 1 \neq 0$, and indeed $\deg = 1 + 1 = 2$. ✓
:::

:::example[Cancellation in $\mathbb{Z}$.]
From $3b = 3c$ we may cancel the nonzero $3$: $3b - 3c = 0 \Rightarrow 3(b - c) = 0$, and since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain with $3 \neq 0$, we get $b - c = 0$, i.e. $b = c$. Contrast $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, which is **not** a domain: there $2 \cdot 1 = 2 = 2 \cdot 4$, yet $1 \neq 4$. Cancellation genuinely relies on the no-zero-divisor property.
:::

## Fields {#fields}

Fields are the rings in which division by any nonzero element is always possible inside the structure. They are the algebraic counterparts of the rational, real, and complex numbers, and they underpin linear algebra, algebraic geometry, and much of number theory.

A **field** is a commutative ring with $1$ (and $1 \neq 0$) in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse — equivalently, the nonzero elements form a group under multiplication.

Equivalently: a commutative division ring, or a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ whose only ideals are $\{0\}$ and the whole ring.

Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime (the finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$), as well as function fields such as $\mathbb{Q}(x)$.

Every field is an integral domain: if $ab = 0$ with $a \neq 0$, multiply by $a^{-1}$ to get $b = 0$, so there are no zero-divisors. The converse fails — $\mathbb{Z}$ is a domain that is not a field, since $2$ has no integer inverse.

:::intuition
In a field you can always "divide" (multiply by inverse) as long as you don't divide by zero — just like fractions, decimals, or complex numbers.

Finite fields exist and are used heavily in coding theory and cryptography.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[Inverses in $\mathbb{F}_5 = \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.]
Since $5$ is prime, $\mathbb{F}_5$ is a field, so each of $1, 2, 3, 4$ must have a multiplicative inverse. We find them by searching for the partner that multiplies to $1 \pmod 5$:
:::

:::solution
$1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so $1^{-1} = 1$.
$2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 1$, so $2^{-1} = 3$ (and $3^{-1} = 2$).
$4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1$, so $4^{-1} = 4$.

Sanity check: every nonzero element appears exactly once as an inverse, confirming the nonzero elements form a group under multiplication. ✓
:::

:::example[Why $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field.]
For $2$ to be invertible we would need $2x \equiv 1 \pmod 6$. But $2x$ is always even, so it is never $\equiv 1$ (odd) mod $6$. No inverse exists. This is no accident: $6$ is composite, and $2$ is a zero-divisor ($2 \cdot 3 = 0$), and a zero-divisor can never be a unit — if $2u = 1$ then $3 = 3 \cdot 1 = 3(2u) = (3\cdot 2)u = 0 \cdot u = 0$, a contradiction. So $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime.
:::

## Characteristic {#characteristic}

Before the formal definition, here is the intuition: in some rings, adding $1$ to itself enough times eventually wraps back around to $0$ (as on a clock), while in others it never does. The characteristic records which case you are in, and if it wraps, how soon.

The **characteristic** of a ring $R$ (with $1$) is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $n \cdot 1 = \underbrace{1 + 1 + \dots + 1}_{n \text{ times}} = 0$ in $R$; or $0$ if no such $n$ exists.

- $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}) = \operatorname{char}(\mathbb{R}) = 0$.
- $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}) = p$.
- If $R$ has characteristic $n > 0$, then $n$ annihilates every element: $nr = 0$ for all $r$. (Reason: $nr = (n\cdot 1)r = 0 \cdot r = 0$, using $nr = r + \dots + r = (1 + \dots + 1)r$.)
- In a domain (hence in a field), the characteristic is either $0$ or a prime.

The prime subfield of a field of characteristic $p$ is $\mathbb{F}_p$; of characteristic $0$ it is $\mathbb{Q}$.

:::intuition
Characteristic tells you the "smallest number of 1's that add up to zero." In clock arithmetic mod p, p ones make a full cycle back to zero.

Char 0 means you can add 1 as many times as you want and never hit zero — like the integers.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[The characteristic of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ is $12$.]
Here $1$ is the residue class of $1$, and $k \cdot 1$ is the class of $k$. We need the smallest positive $k$ with $k \equiv 0 \pmod{12}$. Checking small values: $1, 2, 3, \dots, 11$ are all nonzero mod $12$ (e.g. $6 \cdot 1 = 6 \not\equiv 0$), and $12 \cdot 1 = 12 \equiv 0$. So the characteristic is $12$.
:::

:::solution
1. Note $12$ is **not** prime — and that is allowed, because $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ is not a domain (for instance $3 \cdot 4 = 12 \equiv 0$). The "characteristic is $0$ or prime" rule applies only to domains and fields.
2. Sanity check: in general $\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = n$, since the class of $k$ is zero iff $n \mid k$, and the smallest positive such $k$ is $n$. ✓
:::

:::example[Computing in characteristic $3$.]
In any ring of characteristic $3$ we have $3 \cdot 1 = 0$. Writing $2$ for $1 + 1$, compute $2 + 2 + 2 = 6 \cdot 1 = (2 \cdot 3) \cdot 1 = 2(3 \cdot 1) = 2 \cdot 0 = 0$. Equivalently, $6 \equiv 0 \pmod 3$. So repeated addition collapses modulo the characteristic, exactly like clock arithmetic. ✓
:::

## Prime and maximal ideals; field of fractions {#fractions}

Prime and maximal ideals matter because of what their quotients look like: quotienting by a prime ideal produces a domain, and quotienting by a maximal ideal produces a field. So these ideals are the ring-theoretic way to "manufacture" domains and fields.

In a commutative ring $R$ with $1$:

- $I$ is **prime** if $I \neq R$ and $ab \in I \Rightarrow a \in I$ or $b \in I$. (Equivalently: $R/I$ is an integral domain.)
- $I$ is **maximal** if $I \neq R$ and there is no ideal strictly between $I$ and $R$. (Equivalently: $R/I$ is a field.)

Every maximal ideal is prime (since every field is a domain). In $\mathbb{Z}$: the ideal $(p)$ for $p$ prime is maximal and prime; $(0)$ is prime but not maximal (its quotient $\mathbb{Z}/(0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain but not a field); and $(n)$ for composite $n > 1$ is neither (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has zero-divisors).

The **field of fractions** (or quotient field) of an integral domain $D$ is the smallest field containing $D$ (up to isomorphism). It is built from formal fractions $a/b$ with $a, b \in D$ and $b \neq 0$, under the equivalence $a/b \sim c/d \iff ad = bc$, with the usual addition $\tfrac{a}{b} + \tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{ad + bc}{bd}$ and multiplication $\tfrac{a}{b}\cdot\tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{ac}{bd}$. The requirement that $D$ be a **domain** is essential: it guarantees $bd \neq 0$ so the operations stay well-defined, and it is what makes $\sim$ an equivalence relation.

For $D = \mathbb{Z}$ we recover $\mathbb{Q}$.

:::intuition
A prime ideal is like a "sieve" for products: if a product lands in it, at least one factor already had to be in it — mirroring how a prime number dividing a product must divide a factor. Quotienting by it leaves no zero-divisors.

A maximal ideal is a proper ideal so big that nothing fits between it and the whole ring; collapsing by it is drastic enough to turn the ring into a field.

The field of fractions is "the smallest world where you're allowed to divide by every nonzero thing" — exactly how $\mathbb{Q}$ is the smallest world extending $\mathbb{Z}$ where $1/2$ makes sense.
:::

### Worked Examples

:::example[$\mathbb{Z}/(p)$ is a field for $p$ prime.]
We show $(p)$ is maximal, so the quotient is a field. Suppose $J$ is an ideal with $(p) \subseteq J \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$. Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain, $J = (d)$ for some $d \geq 0$. Then $(p) \subseteq (d)$ means $d \mid p$. Because $p$ is prime, $d = 1$ or $d = p$, giving $J = (1) = \mathbb{Z}$ or $J = (p)$. No ideal sits strictly between $(p)$ and $\mathbb{Z}$, so $(p)$ is maximal and $\mathbb{Z}/(p) = \mathbb{F}_p$ is a field.
:::

:::solution
1. Sanity check with $p = 5$: the only positive divisors of $5$ are $1$ and $5$, so the only ideals containing $(5)$ are $\mathbb{Z}$ and $(5)$ — nothing in between. ✓ Contrast $p = 6$ (composite): $(6) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$, so $(6)$ is not maximal and $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field.
:::

:::example[The field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.]
Apply the construction to the domain $D = \mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Gaussian integers). Its field of fractions consists of ratios $\dfrac{a + bi}{c + di}$ with $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $c + di \neq 0$. Rationalize by multiplying by the conjugate:
$$\frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(a+bi)(c-di)}{(c+di)(c-di)} = \frac{(ac+bd) + (bc-ad)i}{c^2 + d^2}.$$
Since $c^2 + d^2$ is a positive integer, this equals $p + qi$ with $p, q \in \mathbb{Q}$. So the field of fractions is $\mathbb{Q}(i) = \{p + qi : p, q \in \mathbb{Q}\}$, the Gaussian rationals.
:::

:::solution
1. Sanity check: $\dfrac{1}{1+i} = \dfrac{1-i}{(1+i)(1-i)} = \dfrac{1-i}{2} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 i \in \mathbb{Q}(i)$. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming every domain is a field (Z is the counter-example everyone knows).
- Confusing "no zero-divisors" with "every nonzero is a unit".
- Forgetting that characteristic is defined using the multiplicative identity 1, not arbitrary elements.
- In finite rings, thinking char must be prime (Z/4Z and Z/6Z have composite char).
- Claiming (0) is maximal in Z (it's prime but Z/(0) ≅ Z is not a field).
- Forgetting to check 1 ≠ 0 when defining domains/fields (the zero ring is usually excluded).
- In field of fractions, writing fractions without reducing or forgetting the equivalence relation ad=bc.
