# Limits & Continuity

Limits are the foundation of all of calculus. Every derivative and every integral is secretly a limit. Before you can do anything else, you need to know what it means for a function to *approach* a value.

The notation $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ means: as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $a$, the outputs $f(x)$ get arbitrarily close to $L$. Notice that we never require $f(a)$ itself to equal $L$, or even to exist. That single omission is what makes limits powerful — they describe a function at points where the function has nothing to say.

Continuity is the special case where the limit and the actual value agree. Most functions you meet are continuous; the interesting calculus happens at the exceptions.

## Functional representations: the language before the calculus

<!-- section: funcrep -->

Calculus does not invent new functions. It studies how a handful of familiar families change, so fluency with those families comes first.

Linear functions $f(x) = mx + b$ model constant rates of change. Their graphs are straight lines, and that simplicity is exactly what a derivative reproduces locally: the tangent line is a linear approximation.

Power functions $f(x) = x^n$ produce polynomial behaviour. Quadratics model projectile motion, cubics bend and change concavity. Polynomials are the best-behaved functions in the subject: continuous, differentiable, no surprises.

Exponential functions $f(x) = a^x$ — above all $e^x$ — model growth and decay: populations, radioactivity, compound interest. Their defining property is that the rate of growth is proportional to the current value, which is why $e^x$ turns out to be its own derivative.

Logarithmic functions $f(x) = \log_a(x)$ invert the exponentials. The natural logarithm $\ln x$ grows without bound, but astonishingly slowly, and it takes centre stage in integration through $\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C$.

Trigonometric functions $\sin x$, $\cos x$ and $\tan x$ model anything periodic: waves, oscillations, circular motion. Their derivatives cycle through one another, and one of their limits, $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, underpins all of trigonometric calculus.

Inverse functions swap input and output. If $f(x) = e^x$ then $f^{-1}(x) = \ln x$; for an inverse to exist at all, $f$ must be one-to-one. Derivatives of inverses obey

$$(f^{-1})'(x) = \frac{1}{f'\!\left(f^{-1}(x)\right)}.$$

Piecewise functions are defined by different rules on different intervals. They are the natural home of one-sided limits, of continuity and of differentiability — precisely the ideas that follow.

:::intuition
Function families are like vehicle types. Linear functions are bicycles: steady, predictable speed. Exponentials are rockets: the faster they go, the faster they accelerate. Logarithms are the opposite — a rapid start, then diminishing returns. Trigonometric functions are Ferris wheels, going up and down forever in the same cycle.
:::

The derivative measures the *speed* of a function and the integral measures the *total distance travelled*. Both begin by recognising which family you are holding.

## Building intuition: what does 'approaching' mean?

<!-- section: intuition -->

Take $f(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ and feed it values of $x$ marching toward $1$: $0.9$, $0.99$, $0.999$, then $1.001$, $1.01$, $1.1$. The outputs cluster around $2$, even though $f(1)$ is undefined. That clustering is the limit:

$$\lim_{x\to 1} \frac{x^2-1}{x-1} = 2.$$

The limit cares about the neighbourhood around a point, never the point itself. That is exactly why it is useful: it lets us analyse behaviour where the function breaks.

:::definition[Limit of a function]{#limit-definition}
Let $f$ be defined on an open interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself. We write $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ when for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that

$$0 < |x - a| < \delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon.$$
:::

Read the definition as a game. You name a tolerance $\varepsilon$ for the output; the definition promises a tolerance $\delta$ for the input that keeps you inside it. The clause $0 < |x-a|$ is what excludes the point $a$ itself.

:::intuition
Imagine walking toward a door, halving the remaining distance with every step. You never step through it — but you can get close enough to peek and see what is on the other side. That peek is the limit.

The formal definition just says: no matter how close you want the answer to be (that is $\varepsilon$), I can always get close enough to the input (that is $\delta$) to make it happen.
:::

:::example[Building a limit table]
Estimate $\lim_{x\to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$ numerically, then confirm the value algebraically.
:::

:::solution
The function is undefined at $x = 2$, where it reads $0/0$, so approach from both sides.

From the left, $f(1.9) = 3.9$, $f(1.99) = 3.99$, $f(1.999) = 3.999$. From the right, $f(2.1) = 4.1$, $f(2.01) = 4.01$, $f(2.001) = 4.001$. The outputs approach $4$ from both sides.

Algebra confirms it: for $x \neq 2$,

$$\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x+2,$$

and $\lim_{x\to 2}(x+2) = 4$.
:::

## One-sided limits

<!-- section: onesided -->

:::definition[One-sided limits]
The left-hand limit $\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x)$ uses only values $x < a$; the right-hand limit $\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x)$ uses only values $x > a$.
:::

:::theorem[Two-sided limits from one-sided limits]{#two-sided-criterion}
$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ if and only if both one-sided limits exist and $\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L$.
:::

:::proof
Both directions come straight from the definition. A $\delta$ that works for the two-sided limit works for each side separately. Conversely, if $\delta_-$ and $\delta_+$ handle the two sides for a given $\varepsilon$, then $\delta = \min(\delta_-, \delta_+)$ handles every $x$ with $0 < |x-a| < \delta$.
:::

The theorem is the standard tool for showing a limit *fails* to exist. For $f(x) = |x|/x$ we have $\lim_{x\to 0^-} f(x) = -1$ and $\lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x) = 1$; the one-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided limit at $0$ does not exist.

One-sided limits are also the right language at a domain boundary. For instance $\lim_{x\to 0^+} \ln x = -\infty$, while the left-hand limit is meaningless because $\ln x$ is undefined for $x < 0$.

:::intuition
Think of a one-way street. The left-hand limit is what you see driving in from the left, the right-hand limit what you see from the right. If both drivers report the same thing, the limit exists. If one sees a café and the other a parking lot, there is no single answer.
:::

:::example[One-sided limits of a piecewise function]
Evaluate the one-sided limits at $x = 3$ of

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x+1, & x < 3 \\ 5, & x = 3 \\ x^2 - 5, & x > 3. \end{cases}$$
:::

:::solution
1. Left-hand limit: the relevant piece is $x+1$, so $\lim_{x\to 3^-}(x+1) = 4$.
2. Right-hand limit: the relevant piece is $x^2-5$, so $\lim_{x\to 3^+}(x^2-5) = 9 - 5 = 4$.
3. The one-sided limits agree, so $\lim_{x\to 3} f(x) = 4$ by the two-sided criterion.
4. But $f(3) = 5 \neq 4$, so $f$ has a removable discontinuity at $x = 3$: the limit exists and simply fails to match the value.
:::

## Evaluating limits: direct substitution

<!-- section: directsub -->

Always try substitution first. If $f$ is continuous at $a$ then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$ and you are done.

Polynomials, rational functions away from zeros of the denominator, $e^x$, $\ln x$ for $x>0$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, and $\tan x$ away from its asymptotes are all continuous on their domains, as are sums, products and compositions of continuous functions. Between them they cover most of what you will be asked to evaluate.

Substitution fails in exactly two interesting ways, and they mean opposite things.

Indeterminate form: substitution returns $\tfrac{0}{0}$ or $\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}$. The limit may still exist, but the expression as written carries too little information to decide; you must simplify or apply L'Hôpital's Rule.

Nonzero over zero: substitution returns something like $\tfrac{5}{0}$. Then the function blows up and the limit is not a finite number — you are looking at a vertical asymptote.

:::intuition
Direct substitution asks: what happens if I just plug the number in? For well-behaved functions nothing strange happens at the point, so you simply evaluate.

It only breaks when the answer is meaningless, like $0/0$. That is the function telling you: it is not that simple here, dig deeper.
:::

:::example[When substitution works and when it fails]
Decide what substitution tells you about $\lim_{x\to 2}(x^3+1)$, $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}$ and $\lim_{x\to 3} \frac{x+1}{x-3}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\lim_{x\to 2}(x^3+1) = 2^3 + 1 = 9$. Polynomials are continuous everywhere, so substitution is the whole argument.
2. $\frac{\sin 0}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$ is indeterminate; substitution decides nothing. Another technique is needed, and the limit turns out to equal $1$.
3. $\frac{4}{0}$ is *not* indeterminate — the numerator is nonzero. The function blows up, and $x = 3$ is a vertical asymptote.
:::

## Key trigonometric limits

<!-- section: trig -->

These four limits appear constantly and are worth committing to memory.

$$\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{1-\cos x}{x} = 0, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = 1, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1.$$

The first is the load-bearing one; it is proved from the geometry of the unit circle with the Squeeze Theorem. The second follows by multiplying through by $\frac{1+\cos x}{1+\cos x}$ and using the first, and the third from $\frac{\tan x}{x} = \frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos x}$.

:::proposition[Generalised sine limit]
For constants $k$ and $m \neq 0$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin(kx)}{mx} = \frac{k}{m}$.
:::

:::proof
Write $\frac{\sin(kx)}{mx} = \frac{k}{m}\cdot\frac{\sin(kx)}{kx}$. As $x\to 0$ the argument $kx \to 0$ too, so the second factor tends to $1$ by the standard limit.
:::

:::intuition
For tiny angles measured in radians, the sine of the angle is almost exactly the angle itself, so their ratio approaches $1$. This is the same fact as $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x \big|_{x=0} = \cos 0 = 1$: near zero, $\sin x$ behaves like the identity.
:::

:::example[Using the generalised sine limit]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin(5x)}{3x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. The expression is not literally $\frac{\sin x}{x}$, so manufacture that form: multiply and divide by $5$ to match the argument of the sine with its denominator.
2. $\frac{\sin(5x)}{3x} = \frac{5}{3}\cdot\frac{\sin(5x)}{5x}$.
3. As $x\to 0$ we have $5x \to 0$, so $\frac{\sin(5x)}{5x} \to 1$.
4. The limit is $\frac{5}{3}$, in agreement with the proposition.
:::

## Indeterminate forms and algebraic tricks

<!-- section: indeterminate -->

The symbol $\tfrac{0}{0}$ is called indeterminate because the limit behind it could be anything: $0$, $5$, $\infty$, or nonexistent. The cure is always the same in spirit — rewrite the expression until substitution becomes legal. Four rewrites cover almost every exercise.

Factor and cancel: when numerator and denominator share a factor $(x-a)$, cancel it and substitute again.

Rationalise: when a square root appears, multiply by the conjugate. For $\frac{\sqrt{x+1}-1}{x}$, multiply above and below by $\sqrt{x+1}+1$.

Combine fractions: when two fractions are being subtracted, put them over a common denominator first.

Use identities: $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, the double-angle formulas, or $1-\cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ often collapse the whole expression.

:::intuition
An indeterminate form is the mathematical equivalent of "I don't know yet". The same symbol $\frac{0}{0}$ can stand for limits equal to $0$, to $7$, or to $\infty$; substitution simply does not carry enough information to decide, and algebra supplies the missing information.
:::

:::example[Factoring a removable discontinuity]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 3} \frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Substitution gives $\frac{9-9}{3-3} = \frac{0}{0}$: indeterminate, so simplify.
2. The numerator is a difference of squares, $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$.
3. Since $x \to 3$ but $x \neq 3$, the factor $(x-3)$ may be cancelled, leaving $\lim_{x\to 3}(x+3)$.
4. Substitution is now legal: the limit is $6$.

The graph has a hole at $x = 3$; the limit sees straight through it to the value the function approaches.
:::

:::example[Rationalising a radical expression]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1+x} - 1}{x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Substitution gives $\frac{\sqrt{1}-1}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$: indeterminate.
2. The obstacle is the root, so multiply by the conjugate $\frac{\sqrt{1+x}+1}{\sqrt{1+x}+1}$.
3. The numerator becomes $(\sqrt{1+x})^2 - 1^2 = x$, so the quotient is $\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}+1}$.
4. Substituting $x=0$ gives $\frac{1}{2}$.

The conjugate trick is the standard move whenever a limit contains $\sqrt{\text{something}} - \text{number}$.
:::

## L'Hôpital's Rule

<!-- section: lhopital -->

:::theorem[L'Hôpital's Rule]{#lhopital}
Suppose $f$ and $g$ are differentiable near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), that $g'(x) \neq 0$ near $a$, and that $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ has the form $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ as $x \to a$. Then

$$\lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},$$

provided the limit on the right exists (or is $\pm\infty$).
:::

Differentiate the numerator and the denominator *separately*. This is not the quotient rule, and using the quotient rule here is the single most common way to get the wrong answer.

Three cautions govern every application.

Check the form first. If substitution does not give $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ — say the denominator tends to a nonzero constant — the rule does not apply and using it produces a wrong answer.

Re-check after each application. If the new quotient is still indeterminate, apply the rule again. For instance $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}$ needs two passes: the first gives $\frac{e^x-1}{2x}$, still $\frac{0}{0}$, and the second gives $\frac{e^x}{2} \to \frac12$.

Convert other forms first. Products and powers of the shapes $0\cdot\infty$, $\infty-\infty$, $1^\infty$, $0^0$ or $\infty^0$ must be rewritten as a quotient before the rule applies. For $\lim_{x\to 0^+} x\ln x$, of form $0\cdot(-\infty)$, rewrite as $\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x}$, of form $\frac{-\infty}{\infty}$.

:::intuition
Both functions are heading to zero, so their values tell you nothing. The derivative measures *how fast* each one gets there, and the ratio of speeds is exactly the information the ratio of values lost.

If one application still gives $\frac{0}{0}$, the first-order speeds cancelled too. Applying the rule again compares curvature, and so on, until the rates finally differ enough to settle the limit.
:::

:::example[A single application]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{\sin x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Substitution gives $\frac{e^0-1}{\sin 0} = \frac{0}{0}$, so the rule applies.
2. Differentiate separately: $f'(x) = e^x$ and $g'(x) = \cos x$.
3. The new limit is $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^x}{\cos x} = \frac{1}{1} = 1$, no longer indeterminate.
:::

:::example[Repeated applications]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{x - \sin x}{x^3}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$; apply the rule to get $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1-\cos x}{3x^2}$.
2. Still $\frac{0}{0}$; apply it again to get $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{6x}$.
3. Still $\frac{0}{0}$; a third application gives $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{6}$.
4. This is a definite value, so the limit is $\frac{1}{6}$.

Each pass peeled away one order of cancellation between $x - \sin x$ and $x^3$.
:::

:::example[Converting a $0\cdot\infty$ form]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0^+} x \ln x$.
:::

:::solution
1. As $x\to 0^+$, $x \to 0$ while $\ln x \to -\infty$: a product, not a quotient, so the rule does not apply yet.
2. Rewrite $x\ln x = \frac{\ln x}{1/x}$, now of the form $\frac{-\infty}{\infty}$.
3. Differentiate: $\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{x} = -\frac{1}{x^2}$.
4. So the limit equals $\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}(-x) = 0$.

Even though $\ln x$ diverges, the factor $x$ shrinks fast enough to win.
:::

## Limits at infinity and horizontal asymptotes

<!-- section: atinfinity -->

A limit at infinity asks what a function settles down to as $x$ grows without bound, and the answer is decided by the dominant term. For a rational function, divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator; every term that still has an $x$ underneath then dies.

$$\lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1}{x} = 0, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{2x+1}{x+3} = 2.$$

A finite limit $L$ at infinity is exactly what it means for the line $y = L$ to be a horizontal asymptote of the graph — so the two limits above give the asymptotes $y = 0$ and $y = 2$.

Growth rates settle most of the remaining cases: $e^x$ grows faster than any polynomial, and $\ln x$ grows more slowly than any positive power of $x$. Whenever a quotient mixes these families, the faster-growing family decides the limit.

## Continuity

<!-- section: continuity -->

:::definition[Continuity at a point]{#continuity-definition}
A function $f$ is continuous at $x = a$ when all three conditions hold: $f(a)$ is defined; $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ exists; and

$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a).$$
:::

If any one of the three fails, $f$ has a discontinuity at $a$, and which condition fails names the type.

Removable discontinuity (a hole): the limit exists, but $f(a)$ is undefined or disagrees with it, as for $f(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$.

Jump discontinuity: both one-sided limits exist but differ, as for the floor function $\lfloor x \rfloor$ at every integer.

Infinite discontinuity: the function blows up, as for $1/x$ at $x=0$.

:::theorem[Intermediate Value Theorem]{#ivt}
If $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and $N$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f(c) = N$ for some $c$ in $[a,b]$.
:::

The theorem is the standard way to prove that an equation has a solution without solving it: check that a continuous function takes one sign at $a$ and the other at $b$, and a root must lie in between.

:::intuition
A continuous function is one you can draw without lifting your pen: no gaps, no jumps, no teleporting.

A hole is a missing stepping stone in an otherwise complete path. A jump is a staircase — you leap to a new height. An infinite discontinuity is a cliff.

The Intermediate Value Theorem is common sense: if it was $30^{\circ}$F in the morning and $70^{\circ}$F in the afternoon, then at some moment it was exactly $50^{\circ}$F. Temperature does not teleport.
:::

:::example[Continuity of a piecewise function]
Is $f(x) = \begin{cases} x+1, & x < 1 \\ 2, & x=1 \\ x^2, & x > 1 \end{cases}$ continuous at $x=1$?
:::

:::solution
1. Left-hand limit: $\lim_{x\to 1^-}(x+1) = 2$.
2. Right-hand limit: $\lim_{x\to 1^+} x^2 = 1$.
3. The one-sided limits differ, so $\lim_{x\to 1} f(x)$ does not exist and the second condition of the definition fails.
4. $f$ has a jump discontinuity at $x=1$. The value $f(1) = 2$ matches neither side, but the limit had already decided the question.
:::

## The squeeze theorem

<!-- section: squeeze -->

:::theorem[Squeeze Theorem]{#squeeze}
If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$), and

$$\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = \lim_{x\to a} h(x) = L,$$

then $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$.
:::

The name is the proof: $f$ is trapped between two functions that both arrive at $L$, so it has nowhere else to go. The theorem is the tool of choice when a factor oscillates without settling — the oscillation is bounded, and a factor shrinking to zero drags the product with it.

The classic instance is $\lim_{x\to 0} x^2\sin(1/x)$: from $-x^2 \le x^2\sin(1/x) \le x^2$ and $\lim_{x\to 0}(\pm x^2) = 0$, the limit is $0$. The same theorem, applied to the unit circle, is what proves $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$.

:::intuition
You are walking between two friends on a pavement, and both are heading for the same coffee shop. Stuck between them, you end up at the coffee shop too. You have no choice.
:::

:::example[Squeezing an oscillating function]
Compute $\lim_{x\to 0} x^2 \cos(1/x)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Direct substitution is hopeless: $\cos(1/x)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ ever faster as $x\to 0$, and $\cos(1/0)$ is meaningless.
2. Bound the oscillation instead: $-1 \le \cos(1/x) \le 1$ for every $x \neq 0$.
3. Multiply through by $x^2 > 0$, which preserves the inequalities: $-x^2 \le x^2\cos(1/x) \le x^2$.
4. Both outer functions tend to $0$ as $x\to 0$, so the Squeeze Theorem gives $\lim_{x\to 0} x^2\cos(1/x) = 0$.

The chaos never had to be understood — only bounded. The factor $x^2$ tames it.
:::

## Infinite limits and vertical asymptotes

<!-- section: infinite -->

Writing $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty$ says the outputs grow without bound as $x$ approaches $a$. Strictly the limit does not exist as a real number; the symbol records *how* it fails.

:::definition[Vertical asymptote]
The line $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote of $f$ if at least one of the one-sided limits of $f$ at $a$ is $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.
:::

For a rational function, asymptotes live where the denominator vanishes and the numerator does not, so factor the denominator first and cancel common factors. A cancelled factor is a hole, not a wall — this is the distinction the next example turns on.

Signs need care on each side separately: $\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac1x = +\infty$ while $\lim_{x\to 0^-} \frac1x = -\infty$. When the two sides carry opposite signs, the graph escapes in both directions.

For $\ln x$, $\tan x$ and $\csc x$ the asymptotes come from the nature of the function rather than from a visible denominator; $\tan x$ has one at every $x = \pm\pi/2, \pm 3\pi/2, \ldots$

:::intuition
A vertical asymptote is an invisible wall the graph races toward but never reaches. To find the walls of a fraction, look where the denominator is zero. If the numerator is nonzero there, you have a wall; if it is also zero, the factors may cancel and leave a pothole instead.
:::

:::example[Vertical asymptote or hole?]
Find the vertical asymptotes of $f(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x^2-x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Factor: $f(x) = \frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x(x-1)}$, so the denominator vanishes at $x=0$ and $x=1$.
2. At $x=1$ the factor $(x-1)$ cancels, leaving $\frac{x+1}{x}$ for $x \neq 1$. That is a hole, and the limit there is $\frac{2}{1} = 2$.
3. At $x=0$ the reduced numerator is $1 \neq 0$ while the denominator vanishes, so $x = 0$ is a vertical asymptote.
4. The two sides differ in sign: $\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{x+1}{x} = +\infty$ and $\lim_{x\to 0^-}\frac{x+1}{x} = -\infty$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Plugging in the value too early before simplifying an indeterminate form. Always check for $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ first.
- Concluding the limit does not exist just because $f(a)$ is undefined. The function not being defined at $a$ says nothing about the limit.
- Ignoring one-sided limits for piecewise functions and absolute values. You must check both sides.
- Confusing continuity with differentiability. Continuity means no jumps or holes; differentiability means no sharp corners either. $|x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable.
- Forgetting to cancel common factors before declaring a vertical asymptote. A common factor means a hole, not an asymptote.
- Using L'Hôpital's Rule when the form is not indeterminate. It only applies to $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$.
- Applying the quotient rule instead of differentiating numerator and denominator separately in L'Hôpital's Rule.
- Assuming a limit exists just because left and right 'look close' numerically — always check algebraically or with one-sided.
- Forgetting that $\lim \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ requires $x$ in radians and the variable in both numerator and denominator approaching $0$ together.
- Canceling factors without noting the restriction (e.g. after canceling $(x-4)$, the simplified function is valid for $x\neq4$, but the limit as $x\to4$ still uses the simplified).
- Thinking all discontinuities are 'bad' — removable ones are often easy to fix and the function can be made continuous with a redefinition.
