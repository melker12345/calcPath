# Derivatives

The derivative of a function measures its instantaneous rate of change. Geometrically, $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $x = a$; physically, it is the speed at which a quantity is changing at one instant rather than on average.

Derivatives power every optimisation problem, every physical law stated as a rate, and every approximation method in applied mathematics. The formal definition,

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h},$$

captures a single idea: zoom in on a curve until it looks straight, then measure the slope of that line. Everything in this chapter follows from it.

## The definition and what it means

<!-- section: definition -->

:::definition[Derivative at a point]{#derivative-definition}
Let $f$ be defined on an open interval containing $x$. The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h},$$

whenever this limit exists, in which case $f$ is said to be differentiable at $x$.
:::

The quotient inside the limit, $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$, is the slope of the secant line through two nearby points on the graph of $f$. As $h \to 0$ the secant pivots into the tangent, and its slope becomes the derivative. That is the geometric heart of the subject: zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it looks like a straight line; the derivative is the slope of that line.

:::theorem[Differentiability implies continuity]{#diff-implies-cont}
If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.
:::

:::proof
For $x \neq a$ write $f(x) - f(a) = \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\cdot(x-a)$. As $x \to a$ the first factor tends to $f'(a)$ and the second to $0$, so $f(x) - f(a) \to 0$, which is continuity at $a$.
:::

The converse is false, and the counterexample is worth memorising: $|x|$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, because the difference quotient approaches $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right.

Differentiability fails in exactly four visible ways: sharp corners such as $|x|$ at $0$; cusps such as $x^{2/3}$ at $0$; vertical tangents such as $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $0$; and discontinuities of any kind. At each of these the limit of the difference quotient fails to exist or is infinite.

:::intuition
Imagine zooming in on a curve with a magnifying glass. The more you zoom, the straighter it looks, and the slope of that zoomed-in line is the derivative.

A sharp corner never straightens out no matter how far you zoom: from the left it shows one slope, from the right another. That is exactly why corners have no derivative.
:::

:::example[Computing a derivative from the definition]
Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2 + 3x$ directly from the definition.
:::

:::solution
1. Start from the definition: $f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$.
2. Expand the numerator: $f(x+h) = (x+h)^2 + 3(x+h) = x^2 + 2xh + h^2 + 3x + 3h$, so $f(x+h)-f(x) = 2xh + h^2 + 3h$.
3. Divide by $h$: $\frac{2xh + h^2 + 3h}{h} = 2x + h + 3$, legitimate because $h \neq 0$ inside the limit.
4. Let $h \to 0$: $f'(x) = 2x + 3$, which agrees with the power rule applied term by term.
:::

## Core differentiation rules

<!-- section: rules -->

:::theorem[Basic differentiation rules]{#basic-rules}
Let $f$ and $g$ be differentiable and let $c$ be a constant. Then

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^{n} = nx^{n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[cf(x)\right] = cf'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x) \pm g(x)\right] = f'(x) \pm g'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}c = 0.$$
:::

The power rule holds for every real exponent, not just positive integers, which is what makes it so useful: $\frac{d}{dx}x^{-2} = -2x^{-3}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ are the same rule in disguise, once the root and the reciprocal are written as powers.

The constant-multiple and sum rules say that differentiation is linear. In practice that is permission to work term by term, which is how every polynomial is differentiated.

:::example[The power rule on a polynomial]
Differentiate $3x^4 - 5x^2 + 7x - 2$.
:::

:::solution
1. Linearity lets us take the four terms separately and pull the coefficients out front.
2. $\frac{d}{dx}(3x^4) = 3\cdot 4x^3 = 12x^3$: bring the exponent down, then reduce it by one.
3. $\frac{d}{dx}(-5x^2) = -10x$.
4. $\frac{d}{dx}(7x) = 7$, since $x = x^1$ and the power rule gives $1\cdot x^0 = 1$.
5. $\frac{d}{dx}(-2) = 0$ by the constant rule.
6. Adding up: $\frac{d}{dx}(3x^4 - 5x^2 + 7x - 2) = 12x^3 - 10x + 7$.
:::

## Derivatives of common functions

<!-- section: common -->

Every derivative you will ever compute reduces, through the product, quotient and chain rules, to the following list. It is the vocabulary of the subject and has to be known by heart.

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}a^{x} = a^{x}\ln a, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}\ (x>0), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\log_a x = \frac{1}{x\ln a}.$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x.$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\csc x = -\csc x\cot x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cot x = -\csc^2 x.$$

Two patterns make the list shorter than it looks. The exponential $e^x$ is the unique function equal to its own derivative, and every other exponential picks up a factor of $\ln a$ to correct for its base. Among the trigonometric functions, every co-function — $\cos$, $\csc$, $\cot$ — carries a minus sign, and the sine family cycles $\sin \to \cos \to -\sin \to -\cos \to \sin$.

:::intuition
Think of these as vocabulary words. You cannot write sentences without words, and you cannot differentiate without these; the product, quotient and chain rules are only the grammar that combines them.
:::

## Product rule

<!-- section: product -->

:::theorem[Product rule]{#product-rule}
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable, then

$$(fg)' = f'g + fg'.$$
:::

In words: the derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second. Symmetry is a useful check — if your answer is not symmetric in the two roles, you have dropped a term.

Before reaching for the rule, look for a simplification. The product $x^3\cdot x^{-1}$ is just $x^2$, and differentiating that directly is faster and safer.

:::example[Product rule with an exponential]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^3 e^x\right]$.
:::

:::solution
1. Name the factors: $f(x) = x^3$ and $g(x) = e^x$.
2. Differentiate each: $f'(x) = 3x^2$ and $g'(x) = e^x$.
3. Apply the rule: $(fg)' = f'g + fg' = 3x^2e^x + x^3e^x$.
4. Factor the common $x^2e^x$: $\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^3e^x\right] = x^2e^x(x+3)$.
:::

## Quotient rule

<!-- section: quotient -->

:::theorem[Quotient rule]{#quotient-rule}
If $f$ and $g$ are differentiable and $g(x) \neq 0$, then

$$\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}.$$
:::

Order matters here in a way it does not for the product rule: the term $f'g$ comes first and $fg'$ is subtracted. The classroom mnemonic is "low d-high minus high d-low, over the square of what's below", where *high* is the numerator, *low* the denominator, and *d-* means "derivative of".

The rule earns its keep when the denominator is a genuine function such as $\cos x$ or $e^x + 1$. When the denominator is a single power, rewriting $f/g$ as $f\cdot g^{-1}$ and using the product and chain rules — or just simplifying the algebra first — is usually cleaner.

:::example[Quotient rule with a trigonometric denominator]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^2}{\cos x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Identify the pieces: $f = x^2$, $g = \cos x$, $f' = 2x$, $g' = -\sin x$.
2. Substitute into the rule: $\frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2} = \frac{2x\cos x - x^2(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}$.
3. Simplify the double negative: $\frac{2x\cos x + x^2\sin x}{\cos^2 x}$.
4. Factor: $\frac{x(2\cos x + x\sin x)}{\cos^2 x}$.
:::

## Chain rule

<!-- section: chain -->

:::theorem[Chain rule]{#chain-rule}
If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(x)$, then $y = f(g(x))$ is differentiable at $x$ and

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f'\!\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x).$$
:::

In words: the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inner function, times the derivative of the inner function. For $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^3) = \cos(x^3)\cdot 3x^2$ the outer function is $\sin$ and the inner is $x^3$.

Compositions nest, and so does the rule. For $e^{\sin(x^2)}$ apply it twice, peeling one layer at a time, to get $e^{\sin(x^2)}\cdot\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x$.

This is the rule that matters most. Implicit differentiation, related rates and integration by substitution are all the chain rule wearing different clothes, and a missing inner derivative is the most common mistake in the whole of calculus.

:::intuition
Picture two connected gears: the big one turns the small one. To know how fast the output turns you need both ratios, and you multiply them.

Concretely: a $1^{\circ}$ rise in temperature sells $50$ more ice creams, and each earns $3$ dollars, so revenue rises by $50\times 3 = 150$ dollars per degree. Two rates, multiplied — that is the chain rule.
:::

:::example[A single composition]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(\cos x)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Outer function $\ln u$ with $u = \cos x$; inner function $\cos x$.
2. Derivative of the outer: $\frac{d}{du}\ln u = \frac{1}{u}$, which at $u = \cos x$ is $\frac{1}{\cos x}$.
3. Derivative of the inner: $\frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x$.
4. Multiply: $\frac{1}{\cos x}\cdot(-\sin x) = -\tan x$.
:::

:::example[Three nested layers]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}e^{\sin(x^2)}$.
:::

:::solution
1. The layers are $e^{(\cdot)}$, then $\sin(\cdot)$, then $x^2$; peel them from the outside in.
2. Outermost: $\frac{d}{du}e^u = e^u$, which at $u = \sin(x^2)$ is $e^{\sin(x^2)}$.
3. Middle: $\frac{d}{dv}\sin v = \cos v$, which at $v = x^2$ is $\cos(x^2)$.
4. Innermost: $\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x$.
5. Multiply all three: $\frac{d}{dx}e^{\sin(x^2)} = 2x\cos(x^2)\,e^{\sin(x^2)}$.
:::

## Inverse trigonometric derivatives

<!-- section: invtrig -->

$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\arccos x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

The first two are valid for $|x| < 1$, the domain inherited from $\arcsin$ and $\arccos$ themselves. That they differ only by a sign is no accident: $\arcsin x + \arccos x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ is constant, so the derivatives must cancel.

The arctangent is the one you will meet most often, because $\arctan x + C$ is the antiderivative of $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$. Composed with the chain rule it reads $\frac{d}{dx}\arctan(g(x)) = \frac{g'(x)}{1+[g(x)]^2}$, so for example $\frac{d}{dx}\arctan(3x) = \frac{3}{1+9x^2}$.

These formulas are not arbitrary; implicit differentiation produces them. If $y = \arcsin x$ then $\sin y = x$, so $\cos y\cdot y' = 1$ and

$$y' = \frac{1}{\cos y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2 y}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}.$$

:::intuition
An inverse trigonometric function answers "which angle has this sine?", and its derivative says how sensitive that angle is to a small change in the value.

The square roots come from the Pythagorean theorem: unwrap the inverse with a right triangle and the missing side is exactly the expression in the denominator.
:::

:::example[Inverse trigonometric derivative with the chain rule]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(x^2)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Outer function $\arcsin u$ with $u = x^2$.
2. Derivative of the outer: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}$, which at $u = x^2$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}$.
3. Derivative of the inner: $\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x$.
4. Multiply: $\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin(x^2) = \frac{2x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}$, valid where $|x| < 1$.
:::

## Implicit differentiation

<!-- section: implicit -->

Some curves refuse to be written as $y = f(x)$. A circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ fails the vertical line test, yet it has a perfectly good tangent line at every point. Implicit differentiation finds that slope without ever solving for $y$.

The method is one sentence long: differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a function of $x$, and then solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$. Every time a $y$ is differentiated the chain rule contributes a factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$ — that factor is the whole technique, and forgetting it is the standard error.

:::intuition
Usually $y$ sits alone on one side, as in $y = x^2+3$. Sometimes $x$ and $y$ are tangled together and cannot be separated.

Differentiate anyway. Every time you meet a $y$, remember it secretly depends on $x$ and attach $\frac{dy}{dx}$; then solve for that symbol. It is detective work: you learn how fast the hidden variable changes from the equation that traps it.
:::

:::example[Implicit differentiation of a circle]
Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, and evaluate it at $(3,4)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, remembering that $y$ depends on $x$.
2. The left side gives $2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx}$, where the second term is the chain rule applied to $y^2$; the right side gives $0$.
3. So $2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$, hence $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$.
4. At $(3,4)$ the slope is $-\frac{3}{4}$, which fits the geometry: on a circle centred at the origin the tangent at $(3,4)$ falls to the right, perpendicular to the radius.
:::

## Logarithmic differentiation

<!-- section: logdiff -->

When the variable appears in both the base and the exponent, as in $x^x$, neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies: the power rule needs a constant exponent, the exponential rule a constant base. Taking logarithms first repairs this.

The logarithm turns the structure that blocks differentiation into structure that permits it: $\ln(fg) = \ln f + \ln g$ turns products into sums, $\ln(f/g) = \ln f - \ln g$ turns quotients into differences, and $\ln(f^g) = g\ln f$ pulls exponents down into products.

:::recipe[Logarithmic differentiation]
Let $y = f(x)$. Take $\ln$ of both sides, simplify with the logarithm laws, differentiate implicitly — the left side becomes $\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}$ — and finish by multiplying through by $y$.
:::

The same method tames long products and quotients even when the exponents are constant, since it replaces one messy quotient rule with several easy sums.

:::intuition
Some expressions are nested puzzles where no single rule fits. The logarithm takes the puzzle apart into pieces you can differentiate, and multiplying by $y$ at the end puts it back together.
:::

:::example[Differentiating $x^x$]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}x^x$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
1. Set $y = x^x$ and take logarithms: $\ln y = x\ln x$.
2. Differentiate both sides. The left gives $\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}$ by the chain rule.
3. The right needs the product rule: $\frac{d}{dx}(x\ln x) = \ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1$.
4. So $\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \ln x + 1$, and multiplying by $y = x^x$ gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = x^x(\ln x + 1)$.
5. The derivative vanishes when $\ln x = -1$, that is at $x = 1/e$ — the minimum of $x^x$.
:::

## Higher-order derivatives

<!-- section: higherorder -->

:::definition[Second and higher derivatives]
The second derivative $f''$ is the derivative of $f'$, and in general $f^{(n)}$ is the derivative of $f^{(n-1)}$. Equivalent notations are $f''(x)$, $y''$ and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$, the last a reminder that $\frac{d}{dx}$ has been applied twice.
:::

The second derivative is the rate at which the rate is changing. If $s(t)$ is position then $s'(t)$ is velocity and $s''(t)$ is acceleration: positive under the accelerator, negative under the brake. (The third derivative even has a name, *jerk* — how abruptly the acceleration changes.)

Geometrically, $f'' > 0$ means concave up, bending like a bowl, and $f'' < 0$ means concave down, bending like a hill. This is what the second derivative test for extrema is reading.

Higher derivatives are also what a Taylor series is made of: the $n$th coefficient at $a$ is built from $f^{(n)}(a)$, which is how a smooth function is approximated by a polynomial. Functions such as $e^x$ and $\sin x$ have periodic patterns in their higher derivatives, and their Taylor series inherit that elegance.

:::example[Successive derivatives of a polynomial]
Find the successive derivatives of $f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 5x$.
:::

:::solution
1. $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x + 5$.
2. $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 6$.
3. $f'''(x) = 24x$ and $f^{(4)}(x) = 24$.
4. $f^{(n)}(x) = 0$ for all $n \ge 5$: differentiating a polynomial of degree $n$ more than $n$ times always gives zero.
:::

## Tangent lines and linearization

<!-- section: tangent -->

:::definition[Tangent line and linearization]{#linearization}
The tangent line to $f$ at $x = a$ is

$$y = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a),$$

and the approximation $f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ for $x$ near $a$ is called the linearization of $f$ at $a$.
:::

The linearization is the best straight-line approximation near $a$, and it turns awkward evaluations into arithmetic. To estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$, take $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $a = 4$, where $f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ gives $f'(4) = \frac14$:

$$\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + \tfrac14(0.1) = 2.025,$$

against a true value of $2.02485\ldots$ — an error of about one part in ten thousand.

The same idea in differential notation is $dy = f'(x)\,dx$: the approximate change in $y$ produced by a small change $dx$ in $x$.

:::intuition
Near a point, every smooth curve looks almost exactly like its tangent line, so the tangent line is the best straight-line guess for the function a short distance away. The further you travel, the worse the guess — curvature is what the second derivative measures.
:::

## Interpreting the derivative graphically

<!-- section: graphical -->

The sign of $f'$ reports the direction of travel: $f' > 0$ on an interval means $f$ is increasing there, $f' < 0$ means decreasing, and $|f'|$ says how steep the climb or descent is.

Where $f'(x) = 0$ the tangent is horizontal. These are the critical points, and they are candidates — nothing more — for local maxima, local minima and flat inflection points.

The sign *change* decides which. If $f'$ goes from $+$ to $-$ the function rose then fell, so the critical point is a local maximum; from $-$ to $+$ it is a local minimum. If the sign does not change, it is neither: $f(x) = x^3$ flattens at $x=0$ and keeps climbing.

Reading the graph of $f'$ therefore tells you the shape of $f$. Where $f'$ lies above the axis, $f$ climbs; where it lies below, $f$ falls; the $x$-intercepts of $f'$ mark the peaks, valleys and flat spots of $f$. The reverse translation works too: given $f$, sketch $f'$ by reading slopes — steep uphill for large positive values, near zero where the graph flattens, steep downhill for large negative ones.

:::intuition
The graph of $f'$ is a steepness report on $f$. Positive means uphill, negative means downhill, zero means a flat moment — a hilltop, a valley, or a brief rest.

It is the elevation profile of a trail: the derivative is the gradient you feel underfoot at each point along the walk.
:::

## Hyperbolic functions and their derivatives

<!-- section: hyperbolic -->

:::definition[Hyperbolic sine and cosine]
$$\sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}.$$
:::

They obey $\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1$, the hyperbolic twin of $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$, and the single sign change in that identity is what separates the two families.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x.$$

Notice what is missing: no minus signs. Where the circular functions alternate in sign, the hyperbolic ones do not, which is often the fastest way to remember which family you are in.

The inverses have closed logarithmic forms, such as $\sinh^{-1}x = \ln\!\left(x+\sqrt{x^2+1}\right)$, and their derivatives produce the expressions $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$ that turn up constantly in integration.

They are not a curiosity: a hanging chain follows the catenary $y = a\cosh(x/a)$, rapidity in special relativity is a hyperbolic angle, and $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ solve $y'' = y$ just as $\sin$ and $\cos$ solve $y'' = -y$.

:::example[A hyperbolic composition]
Find $\frac{d}{dx}\cosh(3x^2)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Outer function $\cosh u$ with $u = 3x^2$.
2. Derivative of the outer: $\frac{d}{du}\cosh u = \sinh u$, which at $u = 3x^2$ is $\sinh(3x^2)$.
3. Derivative of the inner: $\frac{d}{dx}(3x^2) = 6x$.
4. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx}\cosh(3x^2) = 6x\sinh(3x^2)$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Forgetting the inner derivative in the chain rule. This is the single most common error. Always ask: is there a function inside another function?
- Applying the power rule to $e^x$. The derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x$, not $xe^{x-1}$. The power rule is for $x^n$, not $n^x$.
- In implicit differentiation, forgetting to multiply by $\frac{dy}{dx}$ whenever you differentiate a $y$ term.
- Using the quotient rule when simple algebra would work. For instance, $\frac{x^3}{x} = x^2$ is easier to differentiate directly.
- Confusing the derivative of $\ln(f(x))$ with $\frac{1}{f(x)}$. The correct answer is $\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$ by the chain rule.
- Getting signs wrong on trig derivatives: $\cos x$ differentiates to $-\sin x$ (not $+\sin x$).
- Assuming differentiability implies smoothness everywhere. A function can be continuous but not differentiable at corners, cusps, or vertical tangents.
