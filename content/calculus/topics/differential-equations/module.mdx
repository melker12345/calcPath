# Differential Equations

A differential equation is an equation whose unknown is a function, and in which that function appears together with one or more of its derivatives. Solving it means finding the function. Everything so far in calculus has run in one direction — given $f$, compute $f'$ or $\int f$ — and this chapter runs the other way: given a law relating $f$ to its rate of change, recover $f$.

Almost every quantitative law of nature is stated this way. A population grows at a rate proportional to its size; a hot object cools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room; a mass on a spring accelerates in proportion to how far it has been displaced. None of these statements tells you the population, the temperature or the position directly. Each tells you how the quantity is *changing*, and a differential equation is the bridge from that local rule to the global behaviour.

The chapter has three parts. The first studies first-order equations: how to picture their solutions, how to approximate them numerically, and the handful of families (separable, linear, exact, Bernoulli) that can be solved in closed form, together with the models — growth, cooling, mixing, logistic populations, predator and prey — that motivate them. The second studies second-order linear equations with constant coefficients, where a single quadratic decides everything. The third applies those to springs and circuits, then closes with two short methods (Cauchy–Euler and power series) that reach beyond constant coefficients.

## What is a differential equation?

<!-- section: intro -->

:::definition[Differential equation, order, solution]{#de-definition}
A differential equation is an equation relating an unknown function $y(x)$ to one or more of its derivatives $y', y'', \ldots$. Its order is the order of the highest derivative that appears. A solution on an interval $I$ is a function $y$ defined on $I$ whose substitution into the equation makes it an identity for every $x$ in $I$.
:::

The equation $y' = 2xy$ is first order; $y'' + 4y = 0$ is second order; $y''' = x y'$ is third order. When the unknown depends on one variable, as in all of these, the equation is an *ordinary* differential equation; when the unknown depends on several variables and partial derivatives appear, it is a *partial* differential equation. This chapter is about the ordinary kind.

Because the unknown is a function, a solution is checked by substitution, exactly as a proposed root of a polynomial is checked by plugging it in. The check is not optional: it is the only way to be sure an answer is right, and it is the sanity check that closes every solved example below.

:::example[Verifying a solution]
Show that $y = e^{-x} + x - 1$ is a solution of $y' + y = x$ on the whole real line.
:::

:::solution
Differentiate: $y' = -e^{-x} + 1$. Then

$$y' + y = \left(-e^{-x} + 1\right) + \left(e^{-x} + x - 1\right) = x,$$

which is the right-hand side, for every $x$. So $y$ is a solution. Note that $y = x - 1$ alone also works, and so does $y = 5e^{-x} + x - 1$: the $e^{-x}$ term can carry any constant multiple, because $Ce^{-x}$ contributes $-Ce^{-x} + Ce^{-x} = 0$ to $y' + y$.
:::

The example shows the typical situation. A differential equation does not have *a* solution; it has a family of them, one for each value of one or more arbitrary constants. A first-order equation carries one constant (it undoes one differentiation, and every antiderivative comes with a $+C$); a second-order equation carries two. The family is called the general solution, and any single member is a particular solution.

:::definition[General and particular solution]{#general-solution}
The general solution of an $n$th-order differential equation is a family of solutions containing $n$ arbitrary constants that includes every solution of the equation (apart from occasional exceptional solutions noted when they arise). A particular solution is one member of that family, obtained by fixing the constants.
:::

:::definition[Linear differential equation]{#linear-de}
A differential equation is linear if it can be written as

$$a_n(x)\,y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}(x)\,y^{(n-1)} + \cdots + a_1(x)\,y' + a_0(x)\,y = g(x),$$

that is, $y$ and its derivatives appear only to the first power, never multiplied together, never inside another function. Otherwise it is nonlinear.
:::

So $y'' + 4y = 0$ and $x^2 y' + \sin(x)\,y = e^x$ are linear; $y' = y^2$, $y'' + \sin y = 0$ and $y\,y' = x$ are nonlinear. The distinction matters because linear equations have a complete theory — their solutions can be added and scaled, and their general solution has a predictable shape — while nonlinear equations must be handled family by family. Notice that the coefficients $a_k(x)$ may depend on $x$ in any way at all; linearity is a statement about how $y$ enters, not about $x$.

:::example[A family of solutions]
Show that every member of the family $y = \dfrac{1}{x + C}$ is a solution of $y' = -y^2$, and find the member through the point $(0, 2)$.
:::

:::solution
For $y = (x + C)^{-1}$ the chain rule gives

$$y' = -(x + C)^{-2} = -\left(\frac{1}{x+C}\right)^2 = -y^2,$$

for every value of $C$, so the whole family solves the equation. For the member through $(0, 2)$ we need $y(0) = 1/C = 2$, so $C = \tfrac12$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{x + 1/2} = \dfrac{2}{2x + 1}$. Sanity check: $y(0) = 2$, and $y' = -4/(2x+1)^2 = -y^2$.

One solution is missing from the family: $y = 0$ also satisfies $y' = -y^2$, and no choice of $C$ produces it. Such exceptional solutions are common with nonlinear equations and are discussed under separable equations.
:::

:::example[Exponential trial solutions]
For which values of $r$ is $y = e^{rx}$ a solution of $y'' + y' - 6y = 0$?
:::

:::solution
Substitute $y = e^{rx}$, $y' = re^{rx}$, $y'' = r^2 e^{rx}$:

$$r^2 e^{rx} + r e^{rx} - 6e^{rx} = e^{rx}\left(r^2 + r - 6\right) = 0.$$

Since $e^{rx}$ is never zero, this forces $r^2 + r - 6 = (r + 3)(r - 2) = 0$, so $r = 2$ or $r = -3$. Both $e^{2x}$ and $e^{-3x}$ are solutions, and so — by linearity — is $C_1 e^{2x} + C_2 e^{-3x}$ for any constants. That this two-parameter family is the *whole* general solution is the content of the second-order theory later in the chapter.
:::

:::intuition
A differential equation is a rule a car's dashboard could enforce: "your speed must always equal twice your distance from home." That rule does not tell you where you are; it tells you how where you are is changing. Many journeys obey the rule — every starting point gives a different one — and the family of all such journeys is the general solution. Telling the driver where they started at noon picks out one journey: that is a particular solution.
:::

:::pitfall
Checking a solution means substituting into the *original* equation, not into some rearranged version of it and not into the equation you got after integrating. A sign slip in a rearrangement will make a wrong answer check out against the wrong equation.
:::

## Initial-value problems and the existence–uniqueness theorem

<!-- section: ivp -->

:::definition[Initial-value problem]{#ivp}
An initial-value problem (IVP) consists of a differential equation together with enough initial conditions — the values of $y$ and its lower derivatives at a single point $x_0$ — to fix the arbitrary constants of the general solution. For a first-order equation one condition $y(x_0) = y_0$ is required; for a second-order equation two, $y(x_0) = y_0$ and $y'(x_0) = y_1$.
:::

Geometrically, a first-order condition selects the one solution curve that passes through the point $(x_0, y_0)$. A second-order condition selects the curve through $(x_0, y_0)$ with slope $y_1$ there — position and velocity, if $x$ is time. One condition per order is the rule, because each integration introduces one constant.

:::recipe[Solving an initial-value problem]{#solving-ivp}
1. Find the general solution, with its arbitrary constants.
2. Substitute each initial condition to obtain one equation per constant.
3. Solve for the constants.
4. Write down the particular solution and check it against the original equation *and* the initial conditions.
:::

:::example[A first-order IVP by direct integration]
Solve $y' = 2x$, $y(1) = 3$.
:::

:::solution
Integrating, $y = x^2 + C$. The condition $y(1) = 3$ gives $1 + C = 3$, so $C = 2$ and

$$y = x^2 + 2.$$

Check: $y' = 2x$ and $y(1) = 3$. Of the whole family of parabolas $x^2 + C$, the initial condition picked the one through $(1, 3)$.
:::

:::example[A second-order IVP by integrating twice]
Solve $y'' = 6x$, $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 2$.
:::

:::solution
Integrate once: $y' = 3x^2 + C_1$. The condition $y'(0) = 2$ gives $C_1 = 2$. Integrate again: $y = x^3 + 2x + C_2$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $C_2 = 1$. So

$$y = x^3 + 2x + 1.$$

Check: $y'' = 6x$, $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 2$. Two integrations, two constants, two conditions.
:::

Does an initial-value problem always have a solution, and is it the only one? For first-order equations the answer is yes under a mild smoothness condition. The theorem below is the reason differential equations can predict anything: it says that a rule of change plus a starting state determine a unique future, at least for a while.

:::theorem[Existence and uniqueness for first-order IVPs]{#existence-uniqueness}
Suppose $f(x, y)$ and $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ are both continuous on some open rectangle $R$ in the plane containing the point $(x_0, y_0)$. Then the initial-value problem

$$y' = f(x, y), \qquad y(x_0) = y_0$$

has a solution on some open interval $I$ containing $x_0$, and that solution is the only one on $I$.
:::

The proof belongs to real analysis: one builds the solution as the limit of the Picard iterates

$$y_{n+1}(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^x f\bigl(t, y_n(t)\bigr)\,dt,$$

and uses the bound on $\partial f/\partial y$ to show that the iterates converge and that two solutions cannot drift apart. The idea to keep is that continuity of $f$ buys existence, and the extra continuity of $\partial f/\partial y$ buys uniqueness. Drop the second hypothesis and uniqueness can genuinely fail.

:::example[Where uniqueness fails]
Show that the initial-value problem $y' = 3y^{2/3}$, $y(0) = 0$ has more than one solution, and explain why this does not contradict the theorem.
:::

:::solution
The constant function $y = 0$ is a solution: both sides are $0$. But $y = x^3$ is also a solution through the same point, since

$$y' = 3x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 3y^{2/3} = 3\left(x^3\right)^{2/3} = 3x^2,$$

and $y(0) = 0$. So are the functions that equal $0$ up to any $a > 0$ and $(x - a)^3$ afterwards: infinitely many curves leave the origin.

There is no contradiction. Here $f(x, y) = 3y^{2/3}$ is continuous, so a solution exists, but

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y^{-1/3}$$

blows up as $y \to 0$: it is not continuous on any rectangle containing a point with $y_0 = 0$. The theorem's uniqueness hypothesis fails exactly at the initial condition that causes trouble. Through any point with $y_0 \neq 0$ the solution *is* unique.
:::

Two further remarks. First, the theorem promises a solution only on *some* interval around $x_0$, not on the whole line — and the interval cannot be read off from $f$ in advance. Second, for linear first-order equations $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ with $P$ and $Q$ continuous on an interval, the solution exists and is unique on that entire interval; this is one of the ways linear equations are better behaved.

:::example[Existence is only local]
Solve $y' = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$, and find the largest interval on which the solution exists.
:::

:::solution
This equation is separable (the method is developed two sections on): dividing by $y^2$ and integrating gives $-1/y = x + C$, and $y(0) = 1$ forces $C = -1$, so

$$y = \frac{1}{1 - x}.$$

Check: $y' = 1/(1-x)^2 = y^2$ and $y(0) = 1$. But the solution has a vertical asymptote at $x = 1$; it exists on $(-\infty, 1)$ and no further. The function $f(x,y) = y^2$ and $\partial f/\partial y = 2y$ are continuous everywhere, so the theorem applies at every point — and still the solution lives on a bounded interval to the right. Existence is guaranteed near $x_0$, not globally.
:::

Second-order initial-value problems are solved by the same recipe, and several are worked in the second-order sections. There we will also meet boundary-value problems, in which the two conditions are imposed at *different* points, and see that those can have no solution or infinitely many — the existence–uniqueness theorem is a statement about conditions at a single point.

:::intuition
Imagine a hill and a marble. The slope of the hill at each point is the rule $f(x,y)$; where you place the marble is the initial condition. Continuity of the slope guarantees the marble has somewhere to roll. Continuity of $\partial f/\partial y$ guarantees the hill has no infinitely sharp crease at which two marbles placed at the same spot could set off in different directions — and $y' = 3y^{2/3}$ has exactly such a crease along the $x$-axis.
:::

## Direction fields (slope fields)

<!-- section: slopefields -->

A first-order equation $y' = f(x, y)$ tells you the slope of the solution curve at every point of the plane — before you know the curve. Drawing those slopes is the fastest way to see what solutions look like, and it works even when the equation cannot be solved by hand.

:::definition[Direction field]{#direction-field}
The direction field (or slope field) of $y' = f(x, y)$ is the picture obtained by drawing, at each point $(x, y)$ of a grid, a short line segment of slope $f(x, y)$. A solution curve is a curve that is tangent to the segment at every point it passes through.
:::

To sketch a field by hand, tabulate $f$ on a grid and draw each segment; to sketch a solution, start at the initial point and follow the segments, adjusting direction continuously. Two shortcuts save effort. Points where $f(x,y) = 0$ carry horizontal segments; if $f$ depends only on $y$, whole horizontal lines $y = c$ with $f(c) = 0$ are themselves solutions (equilibria), and the rest of the field is the same in every vertical strip. More generally the curves $f(x, y) = c$, called isoclines, are the curves along which every segment has the same slope $c$, and drawing three or four of them organises the picture.

:::example[Reading a direction field]
For $y' = x - y$, compute the slopes at the nine points with $x, y \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$, identify the isoclines, and find the one solution that is a straight line.
:::

:::solution
The slope at $(x, y)$ is $x - y$:

| | $x = -1$ | $x = 0$ | $x = 1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $y = 1$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ |
| $y = 0$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| $y = -1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |

The isoclines $x - y = c$ are the lines $y = x - c$ of slope $1$. On the isocline $c = 1$, that is $y = x - 1$, every segment has slope $1$ — the same as the slope of the line itself. So the line $y = x - 1$ is tangent to the field everywhere and is a solution: indeed $y' = 1$ and $x - y = x - (x - 1) = 1$. Above the line the slopes are smaller than $1$, below it larger, and following the segments shows every other solution bending toward $y = x - 1$ as $x$ increases. (The linear-equation method later gives the general solution $y = x - 1 + Ce^{-x}$, which confirms it.)
:::

:::example[Equilibria from a field]
Describe the direction field of $y' = y(2 - y)$ and sketch the solutions through $(0, 1)$, $(0, 3)$ and $(0, -1)$.
:::

:::solution
The slope depends on $y$ alone, so the field looks the same in every vertical strip. It is $0$ along $y = 0$ and $y = 2$, so those two horizontal lines are solutions. Between them, $y(2 - y) > 0$: segments point upward, and the solution through $(0, 1)$ rises from $0$ toward $2$ without ever crossing $y = 2$ (it cannot cross a solution, by uniqueness). Above $y = 2$ the slope is negative — at $y = 3$ it is $-3$ — so the solution through $(0, 3)$ falls toward $2$. Below $y = 0$ the slope is negative too, so the solution through $(0, -1)$ plunges downward, ever more steeply. The picture is the S-curve of logistic growth, obtained without solving anything.
:::

:::example[Isoclines that are curves]
Where are the segments of the direction field for $y' = x^2 + y^2 - 1$ horizontal, and what is the slope at $(2, 1)$?
:::

:::solution
Horizontal segments occur where $x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0$: on the unit circle. Inside the circle the slopes are negative, outside positive; at $(2, 1)$ the slope is $4 + 1 - 1 = 4$. The isoclines are the concentric circles $x^2 + y^2 = 1 + c$, so the field gets steeper as you move away from the origin.
:::

:::intuition
A direction field is a weather map of the plane: at every point a little arrow shows the wind. A solution is the path of a leaf carried by that wind from wherever you drop it. You can see where leaves converge, where they scatter and where the air is still, without computing a single trajectory.
:::

## Euler's method

<!-- section: euler -->

A direction field suggests a way to *compute* a solution curve: from the starting point, take a short step in the direction the field indicates, look up the field at the new point, take another short step, and so on. Making that precise is Euler's method, the simplest numerical method for differential equations and the ancestor of every better one.

:::recipe[Euler's method]{#eulers-method}
To approximate the solution of $y' = f(x, y)$, $y(x_0) = y_0$ with step size $h$:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n + h, \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + h\, f(x_n, y_n).$$

Then $y_n$ approximates the true value $y(x_n)$.
:::

The formula is the tangent-line approximation used repeatedly. Over one step of length $h$ the solution is replaced by its tangent line at the current point, whose slope $f(x_n, y_n)$ is known from the equation. The step ends at $y_n + h f(x_n, y_n)$; the true curve ends somewhere nearby, and the difference is the error of that step. Note that the slope used is always the one at the *start* of the step, evaluated with the current $y_n$, not with any exact value.

:::example[Euler's method by hand]
Use Euler's method with $h = 0.1$ to approximate $y(0.5)$ for $y' = x + y$, $y(0) = 1$. Compare with the exact solution $y = 2e^x - x - 1$.
:::

:::solution
Each row uses the slope $f(x_n, y_n) = x_n + y_n$ and the update $y_{n+1} = y_n + 0.1\,(x_n + y_n)$.

| $n$ | $x_n$ | $y_n$ | slope $x_n + y_n$ | $y_{n+1} = y_n + 0.1\cdot\text{slope}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1.1 |
| 1 | 0.1 | 1.1 | 1.2 | 1.22 |
| 2 | 0.2 | 1.22 | 1.42 | 1.362 |
| 3 | 0.3 | 1.362 | 1.662 | 1.5282 |
| 4 | 0.4 | 1.5282 | 1.9282 | 1.72102 |

So $y(0.5) \approx 1.7210$. The exact solution (found in the linear-equations section; check: $y' = 2e^x - 1 = x + y$ and $y(0) = 1$) gives $y(0.5) = 2e^{0.5} - 1.5 \approx 1.7974$. The error is about $0.076$. Every Euler step here undershoots because the true solution is concave up: the tangent line lies below the curve.
:::

:::example[Two steps with a larger step size]
Approximate $y(2)$ for $y' = xy$, $y(1) = 2$ using Euler's method with $h = 0.5$.
:::

:::solution
Step 1: at $(1, 2)$ the slope is $1 \cdot 2 = 2$, so $y_1 = 2 + 0.5 \cdot 2 = 3$ at $x_1 = 1.5$.

Step 2: at $(1.5, 3)$ the slope is $1.5 \cdot 3 = 4.5$, so $y_2 = 3 + 0.5 \cdot 4.5 = 5.25$ at $x_2 = 2$.

So $y(2) \approx 5.25$. The exact solution is $y = 2e^{(x^2 - 1)/2}$ (separable; check $y' = xy$, $y(1) = 2$), giving $y(2) = 2e^{3/2} \approx 8.96$. Two coarse steps badly undershoot a solution that curves upward this fast — the method is only as good as its step size.
:::

How good is the method, and how does the step size help? Repeating the first example with different step sizes gives the following approximations to $y(0.5) \approx 1.79744$:

| $h$ | $0.25$ | $0.1$ | $0.05$ | $0.025$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Euler estimate | $1.6250$ | $1.7210$ | $1.7578$ | $1.7772$ |
| error | $0.1724$ | $0.0764$ | $0.0397$ | $0.0202$ |

Halving $h$ roughly halves the error. That is the general rule: the error of a single step is proportional to $h^2$ (it is the error of a tangent-line approximation), but reaching a fixed $x$ takes $1/h$ steps, so the accumulated error is proportional to $h$. Euler's method is called a first-order method for this reason. Better methods (the improved Euler and Runge–Kutta methods) make cleverer choices of slope within each step and achieve errors proportional to $h^2$ or $h^4$, but they are built on exactly this idea.

:::remark
Applied to $y' = y$, $y(0) = 1$ with $n$ steps of size $h = 1/n$, Euler's method gives $y_n = (1 + 1/n)^n$ as its estimate of $y(1) = e$. The familiar limit $(1 + 1/n)^n \to e$ is Euler's method converging.
:::

:::intuition
You are driving across unfamiliar country with a device that only tells you your current heading. Every ten minutes you glance at it and drive straight in that direction until the next glance. You will drift off the true road on every bend, but glance twice as often and you drift half as far.
:::

:::pitfall
The slope for each step is $f(x_n, y_n)$ with the *current approximate* $y_n$ — not the exact $y(x_n)$ (which you do not know) and not the slope at the end of the step. A common error is to update $x$ before computing the slope, using $f(x_{n+1}, y_n)$.
:::

## Separable equations

<!-- section: separable -->

:::definition[Separable equation]{#separable-equation}
A first-order equation is separable if it can be written as

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)\,h(y),$$

a product of a function of $x$ alone and a function of $y$ alone.
:::

The equations $y' = xy$, $y' = x/y$, $y' = e^{x + y} = e^x e^y$ and $y' = y^2$ (take $g = 1$) are separable; $y' = x + y$ is not, because $x + y$ does not factor. The test is purely algebraic: try to write the right side as a product.

:::recipe[Separation of variables]{#separation-of-variables}
1. Write the equation as $\dfrac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx$, all $y$ on one side and all $x$ on the other.
2. Integrate both sides, collecting the two constants into a single $+C$ on one side.
3. Solve for $y$ if possible; otherwise leave the answer as an implicit relation between $x$ and $y$.
4. Check separately whether any constant $y = c$ with $h(c) = 0$ is a solution that step 1 discarded.
:::

Why is one allowed to treat $dy/dx$ as a fraction and "multiply through by $dx$"? Because the manipulation is shorthand for the chain rule. Let $H$ be an antiderivative of $1/h$ and $G$ one of $g$. If $y(x)$ satisfies $y' = g(x)h(y)$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx} H(y(x)) = H'(y)\,y' = \frac{1}{h(y)}\,g(x)\,h(y) = g(x) = \frac{d}{dx} G(x),$$

so $H(y) = G(x) + C$, which is exactly what steps 1 and 2 produce. The $dy$ and $dx$ bookkeeping is a reliable way of writing that argument down.

Step 4 is there because step 1 divides by $h(y)$, which is illegal wherever $h(y) = 0$. If $h(c) = 0$ then the constant function $y = c$ satisfies $y' = 0 = g(x)h(c)$ and is a solution; it may or may not reappear in the general formula.

:::example[Solving a separable equation with an initial condition]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x}{y}$ with $y(0) = 2$.
:::

:::solution
1. Separate: multiply by $y$ to get $y\,dy = x\,dx$.
2. Integrate: $\dfrac{y^2}{2} = \dfrac{x^2}{2} + C$, so $y^2 = x^2 + 2C$.
3. The initial condition gives $4 = 0 + 2C$, so $2C = 4$ and $y^2 = x^2 + 4$. Since $y(0) = 2 > 0$, take the positive root:

$$y = \sqrt{x^2 + 4}.$$

Check: $y' = x/\sqrt{x^2 + 4} = x/y$ and $y(0) = 2$. The general solution $y^2 - x^2 = \text{const}$ is a family of hyperbolas; the initial condition chose the upper branch of one of them.
:::

:::example[An implicit solution]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{6x^2}{2y + \cos y}$.
:::

:::solution
Separate: $(2y + \cos y)\,dy = 6x^2\,dx$. Integrate:

$$y^2 + \sin y = 2x^3 + C.$$

This cannot be solved for $y$ in closed form, and it does not need to be: the relation defines $y$ implicitly as a function of $x$, and differentiating it implicitly, $(2y + \cos y)\,y' = 6x^2$, recovers the equation. An implicit solution is a complete answer.
:::

:::example[Recovering a lost solution]
Find all solutions of $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x(y - 1)$.
:::

:::solution
Separating requires dividing by $y - 1$, so first note that $y = 1$ makes both sides zero and is a solution. For $y \neq 1$:

$$\int \frac{dy}{y - 1} = \int x\,dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln|y - 1| = \frac{x^2}{2} + C.$$

Exponentiate: $|y - 1| = e^C e^{x^2/2}$, so $y - 1 = \pm e^{C} e^{x^2/2}$. Writing $A = \pm e^C$, which is any nonzero constant,

$$y = 1 + A e^{x^2/2}.$$

The lost solution $y = 1$ corresponds to $A = 0$, a value the derivation excluded but the formula happily accepts. So the complete answer is $y = 1 + Ae^{x^2/2}$ with $A$ any real number. Check: $y' = Axe^{x^2/2} = x(y - 1)$.
:::

The pattern in the last example is universal. Integrating $dy/y$ produces $\ln|y|$; exponentiating produces $|y| = e^C e^{\ldots}$; absorbing the sign into a new constant $A = \pm e^C$ removes the absolute value; and allowing $A = 0$ restores the equilibrium solution. Writing the answer as $y = Ce^{\ldots}$ with $C$ arbitrary is standard, and correct, provided you know why.

Separable equations also answer a classical geometric question. Two curves are orthogonal if they meet at right angles, that is, if their tangent slopes at the intersection point are negative reciprocals. Given a one-parameter family of curves, its orthogonal trajectories are the curves that cut every member of the family at right angles — think of electric field lines cutting equipotential curves.

:::recipe[Orthogonal trajectories]{#orthogonal-trajectories}
1. Differentiate the family's equation implicitly and eliminate the parameter to get a differential equation $y' = F(x, y)$ satisfied by every member.
2. The orthogonal trajectories satisfy $y' = -1/F(x, y)$.
3. Solve that equation (it is often separable).
:::

:::example[Orthogonal trajectories of a family of parabolas]
Find the orthogonal trajectories of the family $x = k y^2$, $k$ a constant.
:::

:::solution
Differentiate $x = ky^2$ with respect to $x$: $1 = 2ky\,y'$. Eliminate $k = x/y^2$:

$$y' = \frac{1}{2ky} = \frac{y^2}{2xy} = \frac{y}{2x}.$$

The orthogonal trajectories have the negative reciprocal slope, $y' = -\dfrac{2x}{y}$. Separate and integrate: $y\,dy = -2x\,dx$ gives $\dfrac{y^2}{2} = -x^2 + C$, that is,

$$x^2 + \frac{y^2}{2} = C.$$

The orthogonal trajectories are a family of ellipses centred at the origin. Sanity check at the point $(1, 1)$, which lies on the parabola $x = y^2$ and the ellipse $x^2 + y^2/2 = 3/2$: the parabola's slope there is $1/2$, the ellipse's is $-2$, and their product is $-1$.
:::

:::intuition
Separation is sorting laundry. The equation arrives with $x$-things and $y$-things tangled together; if they can be pulled apart into an $x$ pile and a $y$ pile, each pile can be dealt with by ordinary integration. Equations like $y' = x + y$ are the sock that will not come apart, and they need a different machine.
:::

:::pitfall
After integrating $\dfrac{dy}{y}$ do not forget the absolute value, and after exponentiating do not forget that the constant $\pm e^C$ can be negative — and, once the equilibrium solution is restored, zero. Writing $y = e^{x^2/2 + C}$ and stopping there loses every negative solution.
:::

## Homogeneous first-order equations

<!-- section: homogeneous -->

Some equations that are not separable become separable after a change of variable. The most useful case is an equation in which the right-hand side depends only on the ratio $y/x$.

:::definition[Homogeneous first-order equation]{#homogeneous-first-order}
A first-order equation is homogeneous if it can be written as

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = F\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$$

for some function $F$ of one variable. Equivalently, $y' = f(x,y)$ with $f(tx, ty) = f(x, y)$ for all $t \neq 0$.
:::

Equations like $y' = (x^2 + y^2)/(xy)$ or $y' = (x + y)/x$ qualify: divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ and only $y/x$ survives. (The word "homogeneous" is also used, with an unrelated meaning, for linear equations with zero right-hand side; the context always makes clear which is meant.)

:::recipe[The substitution $y = vx$]{#homogeneous-substitution}
1. Set $v = y/x$, so $y = vx$ and, by the product rule, $y' = v + x\,v'$.
2. The equation becomes $v + x v' = F(v)$, that is, $x\,\dfrac{dv}{dx} = F(v) - v$, which is separable.
3. Solve for $v$ (possibly implicitly), then replace $v$ by $y/x$.
:::

The substitution works because $F(v)$ has no $x$ in it, so the only $x$-dependence in the transformed equation is the explicit factor $x$ on the left, and that separates: $dv/(F(v) - v) = dx/x$.

:::example[A homogeneous equation]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x + y}{x}$.
:::

:::solution
The right side is $1 + y/x$, so the equation is homogeneous with $F(v) = 1 + v$. With $y = vx$,

$$v + x v' = 1 + v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x v' = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dv = \frac{dx}{x}.$$

So $v = \ln|x| + C$ and $y = vx$ gives

$$y = x\ln|x| + Cx.$$

Check: $y' = \ln|x| + 1 + C$, and $(x + y)/x = 1 + \ln|x| + C$. They agree.
:::

:::example[A homogeneous equation with an initial condition]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x^2 + 3y^2}{2xy}$ with $y(1) = 1$.
:::

:::solution
Dividing top and bottom by $x^2$ gives $F(v) = \dfrac{1 + 3v^2}{2v}$. Substituting $y = vx$:

$$v + x v' = \frac{1 + 3v^2}{2v} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x v' = \frac{1 + 3v^2 - 2v^2}{2v} = \frac{1 + v^2}{2v}.$$

Separate and integrate:

$$\int \frac{2v\,dv}{1 + v^2} = \int \frac{dx}{x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln(1 + v^2) = \ln|x| + C.$$

Exponentiate: $1 + v^2 = A|x|$. Substituting $v = y/x$ and multiplying by $x^2$ gives $x^2 + y^2 = A|x|\,x^2$; for $x > 0$ (where the initial condition lives) this is $x^2 + y^2 = Ax^3$. The condition $y(1) = 1$ gives $2 = A$, so

$$x^2 + y^2 = 2x^3.$$

Check by implicit differentiation: $2x + 2yy' = 6x^2$, so $y' = \dfrac{3x^2 - x}{y} = \dfrac{6x^3 - 2x^2}{2xy}$. Substituting $2x^3 = x^2 + y^2$ in the numerator gives $\dfrac{3(x^2 + y^2) - 2x^2}{2xy} = \dfrac{x^2 + 3y^2}{2xy}$, the original equation, and $1 + 1 = 2$ confirms the initial condition.
:::

:::example[Where the integral in $v$ needs partial fractions]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y^2 + 2xy}{x^2}$.
:::

:::solution
Here $F(v) = v^2 + 2v$, so $x v' = v^2 + 2v - v = v^2 + v = v(v + 1)$. Separating,

$$\int \frac{dv}{v(v+1)} = \int \frac{dx}{x}.$$

By partial fractions $\dfrac{1}{v(v+1)} = \dfrac{1}{v} - \dfrac{1}{v + 1}$, so $\ln\left|\dfrac{v}{v+1}\right| = \ln|x| + C$, hence $\dfrac{v}{v + 1} = Cx$ (absorbing signs into $C$). Solve for $v$: $v = Cx(v + 1)$, so $v(1 - Cx) = Cx$ and $v = \dfrac{Cx}{1 - Cx}$. Finally

$$y = vx = \frac{Cx^2}{1 - Cx}.$$

The equilibria $v = 0$ and $v = -1$ that were divided out give $y = 0$ (the case $C = 0$) and $y = -x$ (not in the family; it is the "$C = \infty$" member). Check for $y = -x$: $y' = -1$ and $(x^2 - 2x^2)/x^2 = -1$.
:::

:::intuition
A homogeneous equation cannot tell the difference between the point $(x, y)$ and the point $(2x, 2y)$: the slope it prescribes is the same along every ray from the origin. So the natural coordinate is the direction of the ray, $v = y/x$, and in that coordinate the equation simplifies to something separable.
:::

## Exponential growth and decay

<!-- section: expgrowth -->

The simplest model of change says that a quantity grows or shrinks at a rate proportional to its current size. Bacteria in a dish, money at continuously compounded interest, and atoms of a radioactive isotope all obey it, at least for a while.

:::theorem[The natural growth equation]{#exponential-growth}
Every solution of

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = ky$$

is of the form $y(t) = Ce^{kt}$, and the solution with $y(0) = y_0$ is $y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}$.
:::

:::proof
That $Ce^{kt}$ is a solution is immediate: its derivative is $kCe^{kt}$. To see that there are no others, let $y$ be any solution and consider $u(t) = y(t)e^{-kt}$. By the product rule,

$$u' = y'e^{-kt} - ky e^{-kt} = (y' - ky)e^{-kt} = 0,$$

so $u$ is constant, say $C$, and $y = Ce^{kt}$. Setting $t = 0$ identifies $C = y_0$.
:::

The proof is worth remembering because it does not use separation of variables and so has no lost-solution issues: it shows directly that the family $Ce^{kt}$, including $C = 0$, is everything. Separation gives the same answer in the usual way: $dy/y = k\,dt$, $\ln|y| = kt + C$, $y = Ae^{kt}$.

When $k > 0$ the solution grows exponentially; when $k < 0$ it decays toward zero. The constant $k$ is the relative growth rate — the growth per unit time as a fraction of the current amount — and in applications it is usually determined from one measurement rather than given.

:::proposition[Doubling time and half-life]{#half-life}
For growth at rate $k > 0$, the time for the quantity to double is $t_d = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k}$. For decay at rate $k < 0$, the time for it to halve — the half-life — is $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{|k|}$.
:::

:::proof
Doubling means $y_0 e^{kt} = 2y_0$, that is $e^{kt} = 2$, so $t = (\ln 2)/k$. Halving means $e^{kt} = \tfrac12$, so $kt = -\ln 2$ and $t = (\ln 2)/|k|$.
:::

Both times are independent of $y_0$: it takes the same time to go from $100$ to $200$ as from $1{,}000{,}000$ to $2{,}000{,}000$. This is the defining feature of exponential change, and the reason a half-life is a meaningful constant of a substance. A population that doubles every $T$ units of time satisfies $y = y_0 \cdot 2^{t/T}$, which is the same as $y_0 e^{kt}$ with $k = (\ln 2)/T$.

:::example[A bacterial culture]
A culture triples in size in $3$ hours. Starting from $500$ cells, how many cells are there after $5$ hours, and when does the culture reach $10{,}000$ cells?
:::

:::solution
Let $y = 500e^{kt}$. Tripling in $3$ hours means $e^{3k} = 3$, so $k = \dfrac{\ln 3}{3}$ and it is cleanest to write $y = 500 \cdot 3^{t/3}$. After $5$ hours,

$$y(5) = 500 \cdot 3^{5/3} \approx 500 \times 6.24 \approx 3120 \text{ cells}.$$

For $y = 10{,}000$ we need $3^{t/3} = 20$, so $\dfrac{t}{3}\ln 3 = \ln 20$ and

$$t = \frac{3\ln 20}{\ln 3} \approx 8.18 \text{ hours}.$$

Sanity check: after $6$ hours the culture has tripled twice, to $4500$; after $9$ hours, to $13{,}500$. So $10{,}000$ should fall between $6$ and $9$ hours, as it does.
:::

:::example[Radiocarbon dating]
Carbon-14 has a half-life of $5730$ years. A bone fragment contains $70\%$ of the carbon-14 that a living sample contains. How old is it?
:::

:::solution
With $y = y_0 e^{kt}$ and half-life $5730$, the decay constant is $k = -\dfrac{\ln 2}{5730}$. We want $e^{kt} = 0.7$:

$$t = \frac{\ln 0.7}{k} = -\frac{5730\ln 0.7}{\ln 2} \approx 2950 \text{ years}.$$

Sanity check: $70\%$ remaining is less than one half-life of decay, so the answer should be less than $5730$ years — and about half a half-life, since $0.7 \approx 2^{-1/2} = 0.707$.
:::

:::example[Finding the half-life from data]
After $100$ years, $80\%$ of a radioactive sample remains. What is its half-life?
:::

:::solution
$e^{100k} = 0.8$ gives $k = \dfrac{\ln 0.8}{100}$, and then

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|k|} = \frac{100\ln 2}{\ln(1/0.8)} = \frac{100\ln 2}{\ln 1.25} \approx 311 \text{ years}.$$

Sanity check: in $311$ years the sample halves; in $100$ years it loses $20\%$; since $0.8^{3.11} \approx 0.50$, the numbers are consistent.
:::

:::example[Continuously compounded interest]
Money grows at $6\%$ per year, compounded continuously. How long does it take to double?
:::

:::solution
The balance satisfies $A' = 0.06A$, so $A = A_0 e^{0.06t}$ and $t_d = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.06} \approx 11.55$ years. The "rule of 70" — divide $70$ by the percentage rate — gives $11.7$; it is this formula with $\ln 2 \approx 0.693 \approx 0.70$.
:::

:::intuition
Exponential growth is a snowball rolling downhill: the bigger it is, the more snow it picks up per metre, so it grows faster the bigger it gets. Exponential decay is the same law run backward — a puddle that evaporates at a rate proportional to its size shrinks fast at first and then ever more slowly, never quite disappearing. In both, only the *fraction* gained or lost per unit time is fixed, which is why doubling time and half-life are constants.
:::

## Newton's law of cooling

<!-- section: cooling -->

:::theorem[Newton's law of cooling]{#newtons-cooling}
If an object at temperature $T(t)$ sits in surroundings at constant temperature $T_s$, and heat is exchanged at a rate proportional to the temperature difference, then

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\,(T - T_s), \qquad k > 0,$$

and the temperature is

$$T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)\,e^{-kt}, \qquad T_0 = T(0).$$
:::

:::proof
Let $u = T - T_s$. Since $T_s$ is constant, $u' = T' = -ku$, so by the natural growth theorem $u = u_0 e^{-kt}$ with $u_0 = T_0 - T_s$. Substituting back gives the formula.
:::

The law says nothing about whether the object is hotter or cooler than the room: if $T_0 < T_s$ the "cooling" is warming, and the same formula applies. In either case $T - T_s$ decays exponentially to zero, so $T \to T_s$ as $t \to \infty$, rapidly at first and then ever more slowly. The constant $k$ depends on the object's size, material and surroundings and is almost always found from one extra temperature reading.

:::example[Cooling coffee]
A cup of coffee at $90^\circ$C is placed in a $20^\circ$C room. After $5$ minutes it has cooled to $70^\circ$C. What is its temperature after $10$ minutes, and when will it reach $40^\circ$C?
:::

:::solution
The model is $T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-kt}$. From $T(5) = 70$:

$$70 = 20 + 70e^{-5k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-5k} = \frac{50}{70} = \frac{5}{7}.$$

There is no need to solve for $k$ itself. At $t = 10$, $e^{-10k} = (e^{-5k})^2 = 25/49$, so

$$T(10) = 20 + 70 \cdot \frac{25}{49} = 20 + \frac{250}{7} \approx 55.7^\circ\text{C}.$$

For $T = 40$ we need $70e^{-kt} = 20$, so $e^{-kt} = 2/7$; with $k = \tfrac15\ln(7/5) \approx 0.0673$ this gives $t = \dfrac{\ln(7/2)}{k} \approx 18.6$ minutes. Sanity check: the gap to room temperature shrinks by the factor $5/7$ every $5$ minutes — $70 \to 50 \to 35.7 \to 25.5$ at $t = 0, 5, 10, 15$ — so reaching a gap of $20$ should happen a little after $15$ minutes.
:::

:::example[Estimating time of death]
A body is found in a room kept at $20^\circ$C. Its temperature is $30^\circ$C when found and $28^\circ$C one hour later. Assuming a normal body temperature of $37^\circ$C at the moment of death, how long before it was found did death occur?
:::

:::solution
Measure time from the moment the body is found, so $T(0) = 30$, $T(1) = 28$, and $T(t) = 20 + 10e^{-kt}$. From $T(1) = 28$: $8 = 10e^{-k}$, so $e^{-k} = 0.8$. At the (negative) time $t_d$ of death, $T = 37$:

$$17 = 10e^{-kt_d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-kt_d} = 1.7 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -kt_d = \ln 1.7.$$

Since $k = -\ln 0.8 = \ln 1.25$, this gives $t_d = -\dfrac{\ln 1.7}{\ln 1.25} \approx -2.38$. Death occurred about $2.4$ hours before the body was found. Sanity check: every hour the gap above room temperature shrinks by $20\%$; running that backward, gaps of $10, 12.5, 15.6, 19.5$ at $t = 0, -1, -2, -3$ hours bracket the gap of $17$ between $2$ and $3$ hours ago.
:::

:::example[Warming up]
A bottle of water at $5^\circ$C is left in a $25^\circ$C room, and after $20$ minutes it is at $15^\circ$C. When is it at $22^\circ$C?
:::

:::solution
$T(t) = 25 - 20e^{-kt}$ (the gap is negative, and stays negative). From $T(20) = 15$: $20e^{-20k} = 10$, so $e^{-20k} = \tfrac12$ — the gap halves every $20$ minutes. For $T = 22$ the gap must be $3$, so $20e^{-kt} = 3$, $e^{-kt} = 0.15$, and

$$t = 20 \cdot \frac{\ln(1/0.15)}{\ln 2} \approx 20 \times 2.74 \approx 54.7 \text{ minutes}.$$

Sanity check: gaps of $20, 10, 5, 2.5$ at $t = 0, 20, 40, 60$; a gap of $3$ lands just before $60$ minutes.
:::

:::intuition
The law is about the *gap*, not the temperature. A coffee at $90^\circ$ in a $20^\circ$ room and a coffee at $80^\circ$ in a $10^\circ$ room cool identically, gap for gap. And the gap loses a fixed fraction of itself in each equal interval of time, which is why "the gap halves every $20$ minutes" is often the whole solution.
:::

## Linear first-order equations

<!-- section: linear -->

:::definition[Linear first-order equation, standard form]{#linear-first-order}
A first-order linear equation is one that can be written in the standard form

$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x),$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are continuous functions of $x$ on some interval.
:::

The equation $x y' + 2y = 4x^2$ is linear; divided by $x$ it reads $y' + (2/x)y = 4x$, so $P = 2/x$ and $Q = 4x$. The equation $y' = x + y$ is linear with $P = -1$, $Q = x$. The equation $y' = xy$ is both linear ($P = -x$, $Q = 0$) and separable; $y' = y^2$ is separable but not linear. The two families overlap, and the linear one has the great advantage of *always* being solvable by a single method.

The idea is to multiply the equation by a cleverly chosen function $\mu(x)$ so that the left side becomes the derivative of a product. We want

$$\mu\,y' + \mu P\,y = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\mu y\right) = \mu\,y' + \mu'\,y,$$

which holds precisely when $\mu' = \mu P$. That is a separable equation for $\mu$: $d\mu/\mu = P\,dx$, so $\ln\mu = \int P\,dx$ and

$$\mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}.$$

(Any one antiderivative will do; a constant of integration in the exponent only multiplies $\mu$ by a constant, which cancels.) With this $\mu$, the equation reads $(\mu y)' = \mu Q$, which integrates immediately.

:::recipe[The integrating-factor method]{#integrating-factor}
1. Write the equation in standard form $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ — divide through by the coefficient of $y'$ if necessary.
2. Compute the integrating factor $\mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$ (no constant needed).
3. Multiply the whole equation by $\mu$. The left side is now $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\mu y\right)$; verify this by differentiating.
4. Integrate: $\mu y = \displaystyle\int \mu(x)\,Q(x)\,dx + C$.
5. Divide by $\mu$ to get $y$.
:::

:::theorem[Solution of the linear first-order equation]{#linear-first-order-solution}
On any interval where $P$ and $Q$ are continuous, the general solution of $y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$ is

$$y = \frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left[\int \mu(x)\,Q(x)\,dx + C\right], \qquad \mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx},$$

and every initial-value problem $y(x_0) = y_0$ with $x_0$ in that interval has exactly one solution, defined on the whole interval.
:::

:::proof
The derivation above shows that $y$ solves the equation if and only if $(\mu y)' = \mu Q$, and since $\mu > 0$ this holds if and only if $\mu y$ is an antiderivative of $\mu Q$, which is the displayed formula. The constant $C$ is determined uniquely by $y(x_0) = y_0$, and the formula makes sense wherever the integrand is continuous.
:::

:::example[A constant-coefficient linear equation]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + 2y = 4e^{-x}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Already in standard form, with $P = 2$ and $Q = 4e^{-x}$.
2. $\mu = e^{\int 2\,dx} = e^{2x}$.
3. Multiply: $e^{2x}y' + 2e^{2x}y = 4e^{x}$, and the left side is $(e^{2x}y)'$.
4. Integrate: $e^{2x}y = \int 4e^x\,dx = 4e^x + C$.
5. Divide:

$$y = 4e^{-x} + Ce^{-2x}.$$

Check: $y' = -4e^{-x} - 2Ce^{-2x}$, so $y' + 2y = -4e^{-x} - 2Ce^{-2x} + 8e^{-x} + 2Ce^{-2x} = 4e^{-x}$. The term $Ce^{-2x}$ dies out as $x \to \infty$ whatever $C$ is — it is the transient — while $4e^{-x}$ is the response to the forcing term.
:::

:::example[A variable coefficient and an initial condition]
Solve $x\,\dfrac{dy}{dx} + 2y = 4x^2$ with $y(1) = 2$, for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
1. Divide by $x$: $y' + \dfrac{2}{x}y = 4x$, so $P = 2/x$, $Q = 4x$.
2. $\mu = e^{\int 2/x\,dx} = e^{2\ln x} = x^2$.
3. Multiply: $x^2 y' + 2xy = 4x^3$, and indeed $(x^2 y)' = x^2 y' + 2xy$.
4. Integrate: $x^2 y = x^4 + C$.
5. $y = x^2 + C/x^2$. The condition $y(1) = 2$ gives $1 + C = 2$, so $C = 1$ and

$$y = x^2 + \frac{1}{x^2}.$$

Check: $y' = 2x - 2/x^3$, so $xy' + 2y = 2x^2 - 2/x^2 + 2x^2 + 2/x^2 = 4x^2$, and $y(1) = 2$.
:::

:::example[The equation behind the Euler-method table]
Solve $y' = x + y$, $y(0) = 1$.
:::

:::solution
Standard form is $y' - y = x$, so $P = -1$ and $\mu = e^{-x}$. Then $(e^{-x}y)' = xe^{-x}$, and integrating by parts,

$$e^{-x}y = \int xe^{-x}\,dx = -xe^{-x} - e^{-x} + C.$$

So $y = -x - 1 + Ce^{x}$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $C = 2$:

$$y = 2e^x - x - 1.$$

Check: $y' = 2e^x - 1$ and $x + y = 2e^x - 1$. This is the exact solution against which Euler's method was measured.
:::

:::example[A trigonometric coefficient]
Solve $y' + (\tan x)\,y = \sec x$ on $-\pi/2 < x < \pi/2$.
:::

:::solution
$\mu = e^{\int \tan x\,dx} = e^{-\ln\cos x} = \sec x$ (positive on this interval). Multiply: $\sec x\,y' + \sec x\tan x\,y = \sec^2 x$, and the left side is $(y\sec x)'$. Integrate: $y\sec x = \tan x + C$, so

$$y = \sin x + C\cos x.$$

Check: $y' = \cos x - C\sin x$, and $y' + \tan x\, y = \cos x - C\sin x + \sin x\tan x + C\sin x = \cos x + \dfrac{\sin^2 x}{\cos x} = \dfrac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x$.
:::

A standard physical instance is the series circuit with a resistor and an inductor. Kirchhoff's voltage law says the electromotive force $E(t)$ supplied equals the sum of the voltage drops, $L\,dI/dt$ across the inductor and $RI$ across the resistor:

$$L\,\frac{dI}{dt} + R\,I = E(t).$$

:::example[An RL circuit]
A circuit has inductance $L = 4$ H, resistance $R = 12\ \Omega$ and a constant voltage $E = 60$ V. The switch is closed at $t = 0$ with no current flowing. Find $I(t)$ and the limiting current.
:::

:::solution
The equation is $4I' + 12I = 60$, or $I' + 3I = 15$. The integrating factor is $e^{3t}$: $(e^{3t}I)' = 15e^{3t}$, so $e^{3t}I = 5e^{3t} + C$ and $I = 5 + Ce^{-3t}$. With $I(0) = 0$, $C = -5$:

$$I(t) = 5\left(1 - e^{-3t}\right).$$

As $t \to \infty$, $I \to 5$ A, which is $E/R$ — Ohm's law, once the inductor has stopped resisting the change. The time constant $L/R = 1/3$ s is the time for the gap to shrink by the factor $1/e$.
:::

:::intuition
An integrating factor is a lens that makes a blurry expression snap into focus as a single derivative. The left side $y' + Py$ is "almost" the derivative of something; multiplying by the right $\mu$ supplies exactly the missing factor so that the product rule reassembles it into $(\mu y)'$. After that, solving the equation is just integrating.
:::

:::pitfall
Two errors account for most wrong answers here. First, computing $\mu$ from an equation not yet in standard form: for $xy' + 2y = 4x^2$ the coefficient is $2/x$, not $2$. Second, multiplying only the left side by $\mu$ — the right side $Q$ must be multiplied too, and it is $\int \mu Q$, not $\int Q$, that appears in the answer.
:::

## Mixing problems

<!-- section: mixing -->

A tank contains a solution — salt in water, pollutant in a lake, drug in a bloodstream. Liquid flows in at some concentration and flows out, and the question is how much of the substance is in the tank at time $t$. Every such problem is set up by the same balance sheet.

:::recipe[Setting up a mixing problem]{#mixing-setup}
Let $y(t)$ be the amount of substance in the tank and $V(t)$ the volume of liquid. Then

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = (\text{rate in}) - (\text{rate out}), \qquad \text{rate in} = c_{\text{in}}\, r_{\text{in}}, \qquad \text{rate out} = \frac{y(t)}{V(t)}\, r_{\text{out}},$$

where $r_{\text{in}}, r_{\text{out}}$ are the volume flow rates and $c_{\text{in}}$ the incoming concentration. The outgoing concentration is $y/V$ because the tank is assumed well mixed. If $r_{\text{in}} = r_{\text{out}}$ then $V$ is constant; otherwise $V(t) = V_0 + (r_{\text{in}} - r_{\text{out}})t$.
:::

The resulting equation is always linear in $y$ (the only $y$ is in the rate-out term, to the first power), so the integrating-factor method always applies; when $V$ is constant it is also separable, and either method works.

:::example[Constant volume]
A tank holds $100$ L of brine containing $10$ kg of dissolved salt. Brine with $0.5$ kg of salt per litre flows in at $4$ L/min, the tank is kept well mixed, and the mixture drains at $4$ L/min. Find the amount of salt at time $t$, its long-run value, and the time at which the tank holds $30$ kg.
:::

:::solution
The volume stays at $100$ L. Salt enters at $0.5 \times 4 = 2$ kg/min and leaves at $\dfrac{y}{100} \times 4 = \dfrac{y}{25}$ kg/min, so

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = 2 - \frac{y}{25}, \qquad y(0) = 10.$$

In standard form, $y' + \tfrac{1}{25}y = 2$ with $\mu = e^{t/25}$: $(e^{t/25}y)' = 2e^{t/25}$, so $e^{t/25}y = 50e^{t/25} + C$ and $y = 50 + Ce^{-t/25}$. From $y(0) = 10$, $C = -40$:

$$y(t) = 50 - 40e^{-t/25}.$$

As $t \to \infty$, $y \to 50$ kg, which is what $100$ L of the incoming brine would contain — the tank is gradually replaced by inflow. For $y = 30$: $40e^{-t/25} = 20$, so $t = 25\ln 2 \approx 17.3$ min. Check: $y' = \tfrac{40}{25}e^{-t/25}$ and $2 - y/25 = 2 - 2 + \tfrac{40}{25}e^{-t/25}$ agree, and $y(0) = 10$.
:::

:::example[Changing volume]
A $400$ L tank initially holds $200$ L of water with $10$ kg of salt dissolved. Brine at $0.1$ kg/L enters at $6$ L/min and the well-stirred mixture leaves at $4$ L/min. How much salt is in the tank at the moment it overflows?
:::

:::solution
The volume is $V(t) = 200 + 2t$, so the tank overflows when $200 + 2t = 400$, at $t = 100$ min. Salt enters at $0.6$ kg/min and leaves at $\dfrac{4y}{200 + 2t} = \dfrac{2y}{100 + t}$ kg/min:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{2}{100 + t}\,y = 0.6, \qquad y(0) = 10.$$

The integrating factor is $\mu = e^{\int 2/(100+t)\,dt} = e^{2\ln(100 + t)} = (100 + t)^2$. Then

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left[(100 + t)^2 y\right] = 0.6\,(100 + t)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (100 + t)^2 y = 0.2\,(100 + t)^3 + C,$$

so $y = 0.2(100 + t) + \dfrac{C}{(100 + t)^2}$. From $y(0) = 10$: $10 = 20 + C/10^4$, so $C = -10^5$ and

$$y(t) = 0.2\,(100 + t) - \frac{100{,}000}{(100 + t)^2}.$$

At $t = 100$: $y = 40 - \dfrac{100{,}000}{40{,}000} = 40 - 2.5 = 37.5$ kg. Sanity check: at overflow the tank holds $400$ L, and $400$ L of the incoming brine would hold $40$ kg; the tank has not yet fully caught up with the inflow concentration, so slightly less than $40$ kg is right.
:::

:::intuition
Think of a bathtub with the tap running and the plug half out. What flows in is fixed by the tap. What flows out is whatever is in the water right now, so the more concentrated the bath, the more escapes each minute. The equation is nothing but that bookkeeping — and "well mixed" is the assumption that lets a single number $y/V$ describe the outgoing concentration.
:::

## Exact equations

<!-- section: exact -->

Write a first-order equation in differential form, $M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0$; this is the same as $y' = -M/N$. If there happens to be a function $F(x,y)$ whose partial derivatives are $M$ and $N$, then the left side is the total differential $dF$, and the equation says $dF = 0$: the solutions are the level curves $F(x,y) = C$.

:::definition[Exact equation]{#exact-equation}
The equation $M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0$ is exact on a region if there is a function $F(x,y)$ with

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} = M \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = N$$

throughout the region. Its general solution is then the family of implicit curves $F(x, y) = C$.
:::

That $F(x,y) = C$ really does give solutions follows from the chain rule: along any curve $y(x)$ with $F(x, y(x)) = C$, differentiating gives $F_x + F_y\,y' = 0$, that is $M + Ny' = 0$. Conversely a solution of $M + Ny' = 0$ keeps $F$ constant.

:::theorem[Test for exactness]{#exactness-test}
Let $M$ and $N$ have continuous first partial derivatives on an open rectangle. Then $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ is exact on that rectangle if and only if

$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}.$$
:::

:::proof
If the equation is exact, then $M_y = F_{xy}$ and $N_x = F_{yx}$, and mixed partials of a function with continuous second derivatives are equal, so $M_y = N_x$.

Conversely, suppose $M_y = N_x$. Define $F(x, y) = \int M(x,y)\,dx + g(y)$, where the integral is with respect to $x$ with $y$ held fixed and $g$ is still to be chosen; then $F_x = M$ automatically. We need $F_y = N$, that is

$$g'(y) = N - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int M\,dx.$$

The right side is a function of $y$ alone, because its $x$-derivative is $N_x - \partial_x\partial_y \int M\,dx = N_x - M_y = 0$. So $g$ exists (integrate in $y$), and the $F$ built this way works. The rectangle hypothesis is what guarantees that the antiderivatives are defined throughout the region.
:::

The proof is also the method: it tells you exactly how to build $F$.

:::recipe[Solving an exact equation]{#solving-exact}
1. Identify $M$ (the coefficient of $dx$) and $N$ (the coefficient of $dy$) and confirm $M_y = N_x$.
2. Integrate $M$ with respect to $x$, holding $y$ constant: $F = \int M\,dx + g(y)$.
3. Differentiate this $F$ with respect to $y$, set the result equal to $N$, and solve for $g'(y)$; it must involve only $y$.
4. Integrate to find $g$, and write the solution $F(x,y) = C$.

(You may equally start from $N$: integrate with respect to $y$ and add an unknown $h(x)$.)
:::

:::example[A first exact equation]
Solve $(2xy + 3)\,dx + (x^2 - 1)\,dy = 0$.
:::

:::solution
$M = 2xy + 3$ and $N = x^2 - 1$, with $M_y = 2x = N_x$: exact. Integrate $M$ in $x$:

$$F = \int (2xy + 3)\,dx = x^2 y + 3x + g(y).$$

Differentiate in $y$: $F_y = x^2 + g'(y)$, and this must equal $N = x^2 - 1$, so $g'(y) = -1$ and $g = -y$. The solution is

$$x^2 y + 3x - y = C.$$

Check by implicit differentiation: $2xy + x^2 y' + 3 - y' = 0$, i.e. $(2xy + 3) + (x^2 - 1)y' = 0$. Here one can even solve for $y$: $y = (C - 3x)/(x^2 - 1)$.
:::

:::example[An exact equation with an exponential]
Solve $\left(ye^{xy} + 2x\right)dx + \left(xe^{xy} - 2y\right)dy = 0$ with $y(0) = 1$.
:::

:::solution
$M_y = e^{xy} + xye^{xy}$ and $N_x = e^{xy} + xye^{xy}$: exact. Integrating $M$ in $x$ (note $\int ye^{xy}\,dx = e^{xy}$):

$$F = e^{xy} + x^2 + g(y), \qquad F_y = xe^{xy} + g'(y) = xe^{xy} - 2y,$$

so $g' = -2y$ and $g = -y^2$. The solution is $e^{xy} + x^2 - y^2 = C$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $1 + 0 - 1 = 0$, so

$$e^{xy} + x^2 - y^2 = 0.$$

Sanity check: at $(0, 1)$ the left side is $1 - 1 = 0$.
:::

:::example[When the test fails]
Show that $(3xy + y^2)\,dx + (x^2 + xy)\,dy = 0$ is not exact, but that multiplying it by $x$ makes it exact, and solve it.
:::

:::solution
$M_y = 3x + 2y$ while $N_x = 2x + y$; not equal, so not exact. Multiply through by $x$:

$$(3x^2 y + xy^2)\,dx + (x^3 + x^2 y)\,dy = 0.$$

Now $M_y = 3x^2 + 2xy = N_x$. Integrate the new $M$ in $x$: $F = x^3 y + \tfrac12 x^2 y^2 + g(y)$; then $F_y = x^3 + x^2 y + g'(y)$, which equals $N$ when $g' = 0$. So

$$x^3 y + \tfrac12 x^2 y^2 = C.$$

Multiplying by $x$ did not change the solutions (it only rescales both sides), so this is the solution of the original equation too. A factor that makes an equation exact is again called an integrating factor; finding one in general is hard, but the linear equation's $\mu(x)$ is exactly such a factor, and factors of the form $x^a y^b$ are worth trying when the coefficients are polynomials.
:::

:::intuition
Exactness means the equation is secretly a contour map. Somewhere there is a landscape $F(x, y)$, and the solution curves are its contour lines — the equation just tells you the direction of the contour at each point. The test $M_y = N_x$ asks whether the prescribed directions are consistent with *any* landscape; if they are, the recipe reconstructs it.
:::

:::pitfall
When the equation is given as $y' = f(x,y)$, put it in the form $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ carefully: $y' = -\dfrac{M}{N}$ means $M$ and $N$ have opposite signs from the numerator and denominator of $f$. For instance $y' = \dfrac{2xy + 3}{1 - x^2}$ gives $M = 2xy + 3$, $N = x^2 - 1$.
:::

## Bernoulli equations

<!-- section: bernoulli -->

:::definition[Bernoulli equation]{#bernoulli-equation}
A Bernoulli equation has the form

$$y' + P(x)\,y = Q(x)\,y^n, \qquad n \neq 0, 1.$$

(For $n = 0$ it is linear, and for $n = 1$ it is linear and separable.)
:::

The only thing separating this from a linear equation is the power $y^n$ on the right. Dividing through by $y^n$ gives $y^{-n}y' + P y^{1-n} = Q$, and the combination $y^{-n}y'$ is, up to a constant, the derivative of $y^{1-n}$. That observation is the whole method.

:::recipe[The Bernoulli substitution]{#bernoulli-substitution}
1. Divide the equation by $y^n$: $y^{-n}y' + P(x)y^{1-n} = Q(x)$.
2. Set $v = y^{1-n}$, so that $v' = (1 - n)y^{-n}y'$ and $y^{-n}y' = \dfrac{v'}{1 - n}$.
3. The equation becomes the linear equation

$$v' + (1 - n)P(x)\,v = (1 - n)Q(x).$$

4. Solve for $v$ by an integrating factor, then recover $y = v^{1/(1-n)}$. Check separately whether $y = 0$ is a solution (it is whenever $n > 0$).
:::

:::example[A Bernoulli equation with $n = 2$]
Solve $y' + \dfrac{1}{x}\,y = x y^2$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
Here $n = 2$, so $v = y^{-1}$ and $v' = -y^{-2}y'$. Dividing by $y^2$: $y^{-2}y' + \dfrac{1}{x}y^{-1} = x$, that is $-v' + \dfrac{v}{x} = x$, or

$$v' - \frac{1}{x}\,v = -x.$$

The integrating factor is $e^{-\ln x} = 1/x$: $(v/x)' = -1$, so $v/x = -x + C$ and $v = Cx - x^2$. Therefore

$$y = \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{Cx - x^2},$$

together with the solution $y = 0$ lost in the division. Check: $y' = -\dfrac{C - 2x}{(Cx - x^2)^2}$, and $y' + y/x = \dfrac{-C + 2x + (Cx - x^2)/x}{(Cx - x^2)^2} = \dfrac{x}{(Cx - x^2)^2} = xy^2$.
:::

:::example[A Bernoulli equation with $n = 3$]
Solve $y' + y = x y^3$.
:::

:::solution
With $n = 3$, $v = y^{-2}$ and $v' = -2y^{-3}y'$. Dividing by $y^3$ and multiplying by $-2$:

$$-2y^{-3}y' - 2y^{-2} = -2x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v' - 2v = -2x.$$

The integrating factor is $e^{-2x}$: $(e^{-2x}v)' = -2xe^{-2x}$. Integrating by parts, $\int -2xe^{-2x}\,dx = xe^{-2x} + \tfrac12 e^{-2x} + C$, so $v = x + \tfrac12 + Ce^{2x}$ and

$$y^{-2} = x + \tfrac12 + Ce^{2x}, \qquad y = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{x + \tfrac12 + Ce^{2x}}}.$$

Check on $v$: $v' - 2v = (1 + 2Ce^{2x}) - (2x + 1 + 2Ce^{2x}) = -2x$.
:::

:::example[The logistic equation as a Bernoulli equation]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ky\left(1 - \dfrac{y}{M}\right)$ by the Bernoulli substitution.
:::

:::solution
Expand: $y' - ky = -\dfrac{k}{M}y^2$, a Bernoulli equation with $n = 2$, $P = -k$, $Q = -k/M$. With $v = 1/y$ the linear equation is

$$v' + kv = \frac{k}{M}.$$

Integrating factor $e^{kt}$: $(e^{kt}v)' = \dfrac{k}{M}e^{kt}$, so $e^{kt}v = \dfrac{1}{M}e^{kt} + C$ and $v = \dfrac{1}{M} + Ce^{-kt}$. Hence

$$y = \frac{1}{v} = \frac{M}{1 + MCe^{-kt}},$$

the logistic curve — derived again, by a different route, in the logistic section.
:::

:::intuition
A Bernoulli equation is a linear equation wearing a disguise: the power $y^n$ is the mask. The substitution $v = y^{1-n}$ is chosen so that the chain rule strips exactly that power off the derivative term, and the equation underneath turns out to be one you already know how to solve.
:::

## Autonomous equations, equilibria and stability

<!-- section: autonomous -->

Many models have no explicit time dependence: the rate of change of $y$ depends only on $y$. Such equations can be understood almost completely without solving them, and the understanding is often more useful than a formula.

:::definition[Autonomous equation, equilibrium]{#autonomous}
A first-order equation is autonomous if it has the form $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(y)$. A number $c$ with $f(c) = 0$ is an equilibrium (or critical point), and the constant function $y = c$ is an equilibrium solution.
:::

Between two consecutive equilibria $f$ has constant sign, so every solution starting there is monotone: increasing where $f > 0$ and decreasing where $f < 0$. By uniqueness a solution can never cross an equilibrium line, so a solution that starts between two equilibria stays between them forever, and being monotone and bounded it must tend to one of them. All of this is recorded on the phase line: draw the $y$-axis, mark the equilibria, and put an arrow in each gap pointing up where $f > 0$ and down where $f < 0$.

:::definition[Stability of an equilibrium]{#stability}
An equilibrium $c$ is stable (a sink) if every solution that starts close enough to $c$ tends to $c$ as $t \to \infty$; unstable (a source) if solutions starting arbitrarily close to $c$ on either side move away from it; and semistable if solutions approach it from one side and leave it from the other.
:::

:::theorem[Linearised stability]{#linearised-stability}
Let $f$ be differentiable with $f(c) = 0$. If $f'(c) < 0$ then $c$ is stable; if $f'(c) > 0$ then $c$ is unstable. If $f'(c) = 0$ the test is inconclusive.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $f'(c) < 0$. Then $f$ is decreasing through $c$: for $y$ slightly below $c$, $f(y) > 0$ and solutions rise toward $c$; for $y$ slightly above $c$, $f(y) < 0$ and solutions fall toward $c$. Since solutions cannot cross $y = c$ and are monotone, they converge to $c$. The case $f'(c) > 0$ is the same argument with the arrows reversed. When $f'(c) = 0$ the sign of $f$ near $c$ is not determined by the derivative — compare $f(y) = (y - c)^2$, $-(y-c)^2$ and $(y - c)^3$.
:::

Near the equilibrium the equation looks like $y' \approx f'(c)(y - c)$, whose solutions are $c + Ae^{f'(c)t}$: decaying to $c$ when $f'(c) < 0$, growing away when $f'(c) > 0$. That is why the derivative decides.

:::example[A phase-line analysis]
Find the equilibria of $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = (y - 1)(y - 3)$, classify them, and describe the solutions with $y(0) = 0$, $y(0) = 2$ and $y(0) = 4$.
:::

:::solution
The equilibria are $y = 1$ and $y = 3$. The sign of $f(y) = (y - 1)(y - 3)$ is positive for $y < 1$, negative for $1 < y < 3$ and positive for $y > 3$, so the phase line has arrows pointing up below $1$, down between $1$ and $3$, and up above $3$. Both arrows around $1$ point toward it: $y = 1$ is stable. Both arrows around $3$ point away: $y = 3$ is unstable. The derivative test agrees: $f'(y) = 2y - 4$, so $f'(1) = -2 < 0$ and $f'(3) = 2 > 0$.

The solution with $y(0) = 0$ increases toward $1$; the one with $y(0) = 2$ decreases toward $1$; the one with $y(0) = 4$ increases without bound (in fact it blows up in finite time, like $y' = y^2$).
:::

:::example[The logistic equation without solving it]
Analyse $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ky\left(1 - \dfrac{y}{M}\right)$ with $k, M > 0$, including where the solutions are concave up and concave down.
:::

:::solution
Equilibria: $y = 0$ and $y = M$. For $0 < y < M$ the right side is positive, so solutions increase; for $y > M$ it is negative, so they decrease; for $y < 0$ it is negative. With $f(y) = ky - ky^2/M$, $f'(y) = k - 2ky/M$, so $f'(0) = k > 0$ (unstable) and $f'(M) = -k < 0$ (stable). Every positive population tends to the carrying capacity $M$.

For concavity, differentiate the equation itself using the chain rule:

$$y'' = f'(y)\,y' = k\left(1 - \frac{2y}{M}\right) \cdot ky\left(1 - \frac{y}{M}\right).$$

For $0 < y < M$ the second factor is positive, so the sign of $y''$ is the sign of $1 - 2y/M$: solutions are concave up while $y < M/2$ and concave down once $y > M/2$. A solution starting below $M/2$ therefore has an inflection point exactly when it crosses $M/2$, and that is where it grows fastest, at rate $f(M/2) = kM/4$. This is the S-shape of the logistic curve, obtained from the equation alone.
:::

:::example[Harvesting a population]
A fish population obeys the logistic equation with $k = 0.08$ per year and $M = 1000$, and is harvested at a constant $h = 15$ fish per year:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = 0.08\,y\left(1 - \frac{y}{1000}\right) - 15.$$

Find and classify the equilibria, and determine the largest harvest rate the population can sustain.
:::

:::solution
Set the right side to zero and multiply by $12{,}500$: $y^2 - 1000y + 187{,}500 = 0$, so

$$y = \frac{1000 \pm \sqrt{1{,}000{,}000 - 750{,}000}}{2} = \frac{1000 \pm 500}{2} = 250 \text{ or } 750.$$

The right side $f(y) = -0.00008(y - 250)(y - 750)$ is negative below $250$, positive between $250$ and $750$, and negative above $750$. So $750$ is stable (arrows from both sides) and $250$ is unstable. A population above $250$ settles at $750$; one that ever falls below $250$ is driven to extinction, since $f < 0$ there and $y = 0$ is no longer an equilibrium.

The equilibria are the intersections of the parabola $0.08y(1 - y/1000)$ with the horizontal line $h$. The parabola's maximum is at $y = M/2 = 500$, where it equals $kM/4 = 20$. For $h < 20$ there are two equilibria; at $h = 20$ they merge into a single semistable one at $500$; for $h > 20$ there are none and every solution decreases to extinction. The maximum sustainable harvest is $20$ fish per year — and harvesting at exactly that rate is reckless, since any disturbance below $500$ is fatal.
:::

:::example[A semistable equilibrium]
Classify the equilibrium of $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = (y - 2)^2$.
:::

:::solution
The only equilibrium is $y = 2$, and $f'(2) = 0$, so the derivative test says nothing. But $f(y) = (y - 2)^2 \ge 0$ on both sides: solutions below $2$ rise toward it and solutions above $2$ rise away from it. The equilibrium is semistable. Explicitly, $y = 2 - \dfrac{1}{t + C}$ solves the equation; for $C > 0$ and $y(0) < 2$ it tends to $2$, while starting above $2$ it blows up at $t = -C$.
:::

:::intuition
A phase line is a landscape seen from the side, with the equilibria as the flat spots. A stable equilibrium is the bottom of a valley: nudge the ball and it rolls back. An unstable one is a hilltop: nudge it and it rolls away. A semistable one is a ledge — safe from one side, a cliff on the other. You learn all of this from the *sign* of $f$, which is far easier to find than a formula for the solutions.
:::

:::pitfall
The derivative test is one-directional. $f'(c) < 0$ guarantees stability and $f'(c) > 0$ guarantees instability, but $f'(c) = 0$ decides nothing: $y' = -(y - c)^3$ has a stable equilibrium at $c$, $y' = (y - c)^3$ an unstable one, and $y' = (y - c)^2$ a semistable one, all with $f'(c) = 0$. Fall back on the sign of $f$.
:::

## Logistic growth

<!-- section: logistic -->

Exponential growth cannot last: food, space and patience run out. The logistic model, proposed by Verhulst in the 1840s, keeps the natural-growth equation for small populations but makes the relative growth rate fall to zero as the population approaches a ceiling $M$, the carrying capacity.

:::definition[The logistic equation]{#logistic-equation}
The logistic equation is

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = k\,y\left(1 - \frac{y}{M}\right), \qquad k, M > 0.$$

The constant $M$ is the carrying capacity and $k$ the intrinsic growth rate.
:::

When $y$ is small compared with $M$, the factor $1 - y/M$ is nearly $1$ and the equation is nearly $y' = ky$: exponential growth. When $y$ is near $M$ the factor is nearly $0$ and growth stalls; above $M$ it is negative and the population declines. The previous section showed, without solving anything, that every positive solution tends to $M$ and has its inflection point at $M/2$. The equation is separable, and here is the solution.

:::theorem[Solution of the logistic equation]{#logistic-solution}
The solution of the logistic equation with $y(0) = y_0 > 0$ is

$$y(t) = \frac{M}{1 + Ce^{-kt}}, \qquad C = \frac{M - y_0}{y_0}.$$
:::

:::proof
Separate: $\dfrac{dy}{y(1 - y/M)} = k\,dt$, and multiply top and bottom by $M$ so that the left side is $\dfrac{M\,dy}{y(M - y)}$. Partial fractions give

$$\frac{M}{y(M - y)} = \frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{M - y},$$

(check: the right side over the common denominator is $\dfrac{(M - y) + y}{y(M-y)}$). Integrating,

$$\ln|y| - \ln|M - y| = kt + C_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\frac{y}{M - y}\right| = e^{C_1}e^{kt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{M - y}{y} = Ce^{-kt},$$

where $C = \pm e^{-C_1}$ absorbs the sign. Setting $t = 0$ gives $C = (M - y_0)/y_0$. Finally solve for $y$: $M - y = Cye^{-kt}$, so $M = y(1 + Ce^{-kt})$ and $y = M/(1 + Ce^{-kt})$. The equilibria $y = 0$ and $y = M$ (divided out at the start) correspond to $y_0 = 0$, which the formula excludes, and $C = 0$, which it includes.
:::

The formula confirms the picture. As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-kt} \to 0$ and $y \to M$. At $t = 0$ the formula gives $M/(1 + C) = y_0$. If $y_0 < M$ then $C > 0$ and $y$ rises toward $M$ from below; if $y_0 > M$ then $C < 0$ and $y$ falls toward $M$ from above. If $y_0$ is very small, $C$ is large and, for a while, $y \approx (M/C)e^{kt} = y_0 e^{kt}$: the exponential phase.

:::example[Solving a logistic initial-value problem]
A population satisfies $\dfrac{dP}{dt} = 0.1P\left(1 - \dfrac{P}{1000}\right)$ with $P(0) = 100$. Find $P(t)$, the time at which the population reaches $500$, and the growth rate at that moment.
:::

:::solution
Here $k = 0.1$, $M = 1000$ and $C = (1000 - 100)/100 = 9$, so

$$P(t) = \frac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-0.1t}}.$$

Check: $P(0) = 1000/10 = 100$. For $P = 500$ we need $1 + 9e^{-0.1t} = 2$, so $e^{-0.1t} = 1/9$ and $t = 10\ln 9 \approx 22.0$. At that moment the growth rate is $0.1 \cdot 500 \cdot \tfrac12 = 25$ per unit time, the largest it will ever be, since $500 = M/2$ is the inflection point. Sanity check: in the exponential phase the population would double every $10\ln 2 \approx 6.9$ time units, reaching $500$ in about $16$; the logistic brake makes it take longer, as it does.
:::

:::example[Fitting the growth rate to data]
A lake can support $1000$ fish. It is stocked with $100$, and after $10$ years there are $300$. Assuming logistic growth, find $k$, and predict the population after $20$ years.
:::

:::solution
$M = 1000$ and $C = 9$, so $P(t) = 1000/(1 + 9e^{-kt})$. From $P(10) = 300$:

$$1 + 9e^{-10k} = \frac{1000}{300} = \frac{10}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{-10k} = \frac{7}{27} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{1}{10}\ln\frac{27}{7} \approx 0.135.$$

Then $e^{-20k} = (7/27)^2 = 49/729$ and

$$P(20) = \frac{1000}{1 + 9 \cdot 49/729} = \frac{1000}{1 + 49/81} = \frac{1000 \cdot 81}{130} \approx 623.$$

Sanity check: from $100$ to $300$ in the first decade, and the population is now past the inflection point at $500$, so the second decade's gain ($323$) is larger than the first's ($200$) but the growth is beginning to slow.
:::

:::example[Starting above the carrying capacity]
Solve $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 2y\left(1 - \dfrac{y}{50}\right)$ with $y(0) = 100$ and describe the solution.
:::

:::solution
$C = (50 - 100)/100 = -\tfrac12$, so

$$y(t) = \frac{50}{1 - \tfrac12 e^{-2t}} = \frac{100}{2 - e^{-2t}}.$$

Check: $y(0) = 100/1 = 100$. The denominator increases from $1$ toward $2$, so $y$ decreases from $100$ toward $50$, concave up throughout: an overpopulated habitat shrinking to its capacity. It never reaches $50$, and the formula is valid for all $t \ge 0$ (the denominator would vanish only at $t = -\tfrac12\ln 2 < 0$).
:::

:::intuition
Picture a rumour spreading through a school of $M$ students. At first every student who knows it tells others who do not, and it spreads exponentially. But the more people already know, the harder it is to find someone who does not, so the spreading slows — fastest when exactly half know — and finally stops when everyone does. The product $y(M - y)$ counts pairs of a knower and a non-knower, and that is what the logistic equation says the rate is proportional to.
:::

:::pitfall
The constant $C$ in $M/(1 + Ce^{-kt})$ is not the initial population and is not $M$; it is $(M - y_0)/y_0$. And the carrying capacity is the value the solution tends to, not its starting value: in $y' = 0.1y(1 - y/1000)$ with $y(0) = 100$, the capacity is $1000$.
:::

## Predator–prey systems

<!-- section: predatorprey -->

Two species can be modelled by two coupled equations. In the Lotka–Volterra model, a prey population $R$ (rabbits) grows exponentially on its own but is eaten at a rate proportional to the number of encounters $RW$ with predators $W$ (wolves), who in turn die out on their own but breed in proportion to the same encounters:

$$\frac{dR}{dt} = kR - aRW, \qquad \frac{dW}{dt} = -rW + bRW, \qquad k, a, r, b > 0.$$

This system cannot be solved in closed form, but its qualitative behaviour can be read off in the same spirit as the phase line, now in the $RW$-plane.

:::proposition[Equilibria of the Lotka–Volterra system]{#lotka-volterra-equilibria}
The system has exactly two equilibrium points: $(R, W) = (0, 0)$, and

$$R = \frac{r}{b}, \qquad W = \frac{k}{a}.$$
:::

:::proof
$dR/dt = R(k - aW) = 0$ requires $R = 0$ or $W = k/a$; $dW/dt = W(-r + bR) = 0$ requires $W = 0$ or $R = r/b$. The combinations $R = 0, W = 0$ and $W = k/a, R = r/b$ satisfy both; the mixed combinations ($R = 0$ with $R = r/b$, or $W = 0$ with $W = k/a$) are impossible.
:::

Away from the equilibria the two signs tell the story. With $W < k/a$ the prey increase and with $W > k/a$ they decrease; with $R > r/b$ the predators increase and with $R < r/b$ they decrease. Starting with few predators and many prey, prey rise, predators then rise, prey fall, predators fall, and the cycle repeats: the trajectories in the phase plane circle the equilibrium $(r/b, k/a)$ counterclockwise (with $R$ on the horizontal axis), and both populations oscillate, the predators' peak lagging the prey's.

:::example[A rabbit–wolf model]
For $\dfrac{dR}{dt} = 0.08R - 0.001RW$ and $\dfrac{dW}{dt} = -0.02W + 0.00002RW$, find the nonzero equilibrium, and determine which populations are increasing when $R = 2000$ and $W = 50$.
:::

:::solution
Here $k = 0.08$, $a = 0.001$, $r = 0.02$, $b = 0.00002$, so the equilibrium is $R = r/b = 1000$ rabbits and $W = k/a = 80$ wolves. At $(2000, 50)$: $dR/dt = 2000(0.08 - 0.05) = 60 > 0$ and $dW/dt = 50(-0.02 + 0.04) = 1 > 0$. Both populations are growing — the phase point is moving up and to the right, on its way around the cycle. Left alone, the rabbits will peak when $W$ reaches $80$, after which the wolves keep growing while the rabbits decline.
:::

Although the system has no explicit solution, the *trajectories* do. Dividing the two equations,

$$\frac{dW}{dR} = \frac{W(-r + bR)}{R(k - aW)},$$

a separable equation. Separating gives $\dfrac{k - aW}{W}\,dW = \dfrac{-r + bR}{R}\,dR$, and integrating,

$$k\ln W - aW = -r\ln R + bR + C, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad k\ln W - aW + r\ln R - bR = C.$$

Each trajectory is a level curve of the function on the left, and those level curves are closed loops around the equilibrium. This is why the populations cycle forever rather than spiralling in or out: the model has a conserved quantity.

:::example[The trajectory through a point]
For the rabbit–wolf model above, write the equation of the trajectory through $(R, W) = (1000, 40)$.
:::

:::solution
Substituting $k = 0.08$, $a = 0.001$, $r = 0.02$, $b = 0.00002$:

$$0.08\ln W - 0.001W + 0.02\ln R - 0.00002R = C.$$

At $(1000, 40)$: $C = 0.08\ln 40 - 0.04 + 0.02\ln 1000 - 0.02 = 0.08\ln 40 + 0.02\ln 1000 - 0.06 \approx 0.2951 + 0.1382 - 0.06 = 0.3733$. The trajectory is the closed curve on which the left side equals $0.3733$. The point $(1000, 40)$ lies directly below the equilibrium $(1000, 80)$: there $dW/dt = 40(-0.02 + 0.02) = 0$ while $dR/dt = 1000(0.08 - 0.04) = 40 > 0$, so it is the point of the cycle at which the wolf population is at its minimum and the rabbits are still increasing.
:::

:::intuition
Think of a pendulum: it never settles, because energy is conserved. The Lotka–Volterra populations behave the same way — the expression $k\ln W - aW + r\ln R - bR$ plays the role of energy. Real ecosystems have friction (limited food for the prey, for instance, which adds a logistic term), and then the loops spiral in to a steady coexistence.
:::

## Second-order linear homogeneous equations

<!-- section: secondorder -->

:::definition[Second-order linear equation]{#second-order-linear}
A second-order linear equation has the form

$$P(x)\,y'' + Q(x)\,y' + R(x)\,y = G(x).$$

It is homogeneous if $G(x) = 0$ for all $x$, and nonhomogeneous otherwise. It has constant coefficients if $P, Q, R$ are constants, in which case it is written $ay'' + by' + cy = G(x)$ with $a \neq 0$.
:::

The first thing to know about a homogeneous linear equation is that its solutions can be combined.

:::theorem[Superposition]{#superposition}
If $y_1$ and $y_2$ are solutions of the homogeneous equation $P y'' + Q y' + R y = 0$, then so is $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ for any constants $c_1, c_2$.
:::

:::proof
Differentiation is linear, so with $y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$,

$$P y'' + Q y' + R y = c_1\left(P y_1'' + Q y_1' + R y_1\right) + c_2\left(P y_2'' + Q y_2' + R y_2\right) = c_1 \cdot 0 + c_2 \cdot 0 = 0.$$
:::

Superposition fails for nonhomogeneous and for nonlinear equations: the sum of two solutions of $y'' + y = 1$ satisfies $y'' + y = 2$, and the sum of two solutions of $y' = y^2$ is generally not a solution. It is the property that makes the theory of linear equations tractable.

To get *all* solutions, two solutions are enough, provided they are genuinely different — not one a multiple of the other. The test for that is a determinant.

:::definition[Wronskian, linear independence]{#wronskian}
The Wronskian of two differentiable functions $y_1, y_2$ is

$$W(y_1, y_2)(x) = \begin{vmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \\ y_1' & y_2' \end{vmatrix} = y_1 y_2' - y_1' y_2.$$

Two solutions of a homogeneous linear equation are linearly independent on an interval if neither is a constant multiple of the other; for solutions, this holds exactly when $W(y_1, y_2) \neq 0$ at some (equivalently every) point of the interval.
:::

:::theorem[General solution of the homogeneous equation]{#homogeneous-general-solution}
Let $P, Q, R$ be continuous on an interval with $P \neq 0$ there, and let $y_1, y_2$ be linearly independent solutions of $P y'' + Q y' + R y = 0$ on that interval. Then every solution is of the form

$$y = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2.$$
:::

The proof uses the existence–uniqueness theorem for second-order linear equations (the same statement as for first order, with two initial conditions). Given any solution $y$ and a point $x_0$, the two equations $c_1 y_1(x_0) + c_2 y_2(x_0) = y(x_0)$, $c_1 y_1'(x_0) + c_2 y_2'(x_0) = y'(x_0)$ have a unique solution $(c_1, c_2)$ because their determinant is the nonzero Wronskian; then $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ and $y$ solve the same initial-value problem, so they are equal.

Now specialise to constant coefficients: $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. The exponential trial solution of the introductory section is the key. Substituting $y = e^{rx}$ gives $e^{rx}(ar^2 + br + c) = 0$, so $e^{rx}$ is a solution exactly when $r$ is a root of the characteristic equation

$$ar^2 + br + c = 0, \qquad r = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$

The three possibilities for the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ give three shapes of general solution.

:::theorem[Solutions by characteristic roots]{#characteristic-roots}
For $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$ with characteristic roots $r_1, r_2$:

1. If $b^2 - 4ac > 0$, the roots are real and distinct, and $y = c_1 e^{r_1 x} + c_2 e^{r_2 x}$.
2. If $b^2 - 4ac = 0$, there is one repeated real root $r = -b/(2a)$, and $y = (c_1 + c_2 x)\,e^{rx}$.
3. If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, the roots are complex conjugates $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$ with $\alpha = -b/(2a)$ and $\beta = \sqrt{4ac - b^2}/(2a)$, and $y = e^{\alpha x}\left(c_1\cos\beta x + c_2\sin\beta x\right)$.
:::

:::proof
Case 1. $e^{r_1 x}$ and $e^{r_2 x}$ are solutions, and their Wronskian is $e^{r_1 x}\cdot r_2 e^{r_2 x} - r_1 e^{r_1 x}\cdot e^{r_2 x} = (r_2 - r_1)e^{(r_1 + r_2)x} \neq 0$, so by the general-solution theorem every solution is a combination of them.

Case 2. $e^{rx}$ is one solution; we need a second. Try $y = xe^{rx}$: then $y' = (1 + rx)e^{rx}$ and $y'' = (2r + r^2 x)e^{rx}$, so

$$ay'' + by' + cy = e^{rx}\left[x\left(ar^2 + br + c\right) + (2ar + b)\right].$$

The first bracket vanishes because $r$ is a root, and $2ar + b = 0$ because $r = -b/(2a)$. So $xe^{rx}$ is a solution. The Wronskian of $e^{rx}$ and $xe^{rx}$ is $e^{rx}(1 + rx)e^{rx} - re^{rx}\cdot xe^{rx} = e^{2rx} \neq 0$.

Case 3. Formally $e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x}$ and $e^{(\alpha - i\beta)x}$ are solutions, and the computation that showed $e^{rx}$ solves the equation is valid for complex $r$. By Euler's formula, $e^{(\alpha \pm i\beta)x} = e^{\alpha x}(\cos\beta x \pm i\sin\beta x)$. Superposition (which allows complex constants) shows that half their sum, $e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x$, and their difference over $2i$, $e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x$, are solutions — and these are real. Their Wronskian is $\beta e^{2\alpha x} \neq 0$ (the terms in $\alpha$ cancel and $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ remains).
:::

In case 3 the real part $\alpha$ of the roots controls growth or decay of the amplitude and the imaginary part $\beta$ is the angular frequency of the oscillation. In case 1 with $r_1, r_2 < 0$ every solution decays; in case 2 the factor $x$ makes the solution rise briefly before $e^{rx}$ wins (if $r < 0$). The three cases are the overdamped, critically damped and underdamped motions of a spring, treated later.

:::example[Complex roots: pure oscillation]
Solve $y'' + 4y = 0$.
:::

:::solution
The characteristic equation $r^2 + 4 = 0$ has roots $r = \pm 2i$, so $\alpha = 0$ and $\beta = 2$:

$$y = c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x.$$

No exponential factor, so the oscillation neither grows nor decays; the period is $2\pi/2 = \pi$. Check: $y'' = -4c_1\cos 2x - 4c_2\sin 2x = -4y$.
:::

:::example[Distinct real roots with initial conditions]
Solve $y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0$ with $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$.
:::

:::solution
$r^2 - 5r + 6 = (r - 2)(r - 3) = 0$, so $y = c_1 e^{2x} + c_2 e^{3x}$ and $y' = 2c_1 e^{2x} + 3c_2 e^{3x}$. The conditions give $c_1 + c_2 = 1$ and $2c_1 + 3c_2 = 0$. From the second, $c_1 = -\tfrac32 c_2$; substituting, $-\tfrac12 c_2 = 1$, so $c_2 = -2$ and $c_1 = 3$:

$$y = 3e^{2x} - 2e^{3x}.$$

Check: $y(0) = 3 - 2 = 1$ and $y'(0) = 6 - 6 = 0$.
:::

:::example[A repeated root with initial conditions]
Solve $y'' + 6y' + 9y = 0$ with $y(0) = 2$, $y'(0) = 1$.
:::

:::solution
$r^2 + 6r + 9 = (r + 3)^2$, a repeated root $r = -3$, so $y = (c_1 + c_2 x)e^{-3x}$. Then $y(0) = c_1 = 2$. Differentiating, $y' = c_2 e^{-3x} - 3(c_1 + c_2 x)e^{-3x}$, so $y'(0) = c_2 - 3c_1 = c_2 - 6 = 1$ and $c_2 = 7$:

$$y = (2 + 7x)e^{-3x}.$$

Check: $y(0) = 2$; $y'(0) = 7 - 6 = 1$. The solution rises at first (positive initial velocity) and then decays to zero, as the factor $e^{-3x}$ takes over.
:::

:::example[Damped oscillation]
Solve $y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0$ with $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$.
:::

:::solution
$r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$ gives $r = \dfrac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$, so $y = e^{-x}(c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x)$. Then $y(0) = c_1 = 1$ and, since

$$y' = e^{-x}\left[(-c_1 + 2c_2)\cos 2x + (-c_2 - 2c_1)\sin 2x\right],$$

$y'(0) = -c_1 + 2c_2 = 0$ gives $c_2 = \tfrac12$:

$$y = e^{-x}\left(\cos 2x + \tfrac12\sin 2x\right).$$

Check: $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = -1 + 1 = 0$. The amplitude decays like $e^{-x}$ while the solution oscillates with angular frequency $2$.
:::

A boundary-value problem imposes the two conditions at different points, typically $y(x_0) = y_0$ and $y(x_1) = y_1$. Unlike an initial-value problem, it may have one solution, none, or infinitely many.

:::example[Boundary-value problems]
Solve $y'' + 4y = 0$ with (a) $y(0) = 1$, $y(\pi/4) = 2$; (b) $y(0) = 1$, $y(\pi/2) = 3$; (c) $y(0) = 1$, $y(\pi/2) = -1$.
:::

:::solution
The general solution is $y = c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x$, and $y(0) = 1$ gives $c_1 = 1$ in every case.

(a) $y(\pi/4) = \cos(\pi/2) + c_2\sin(\pi/2) = c_2 = 2$. Unique solution $y = \cos 2x + 2\sin 2x$.

(b) $y(\pi/2) = \cos\pi + c_2\sin\pi = -1$, regardless of $c_2$. The requirement $y(\pi/2) = 3$ cannot be met: no solution.

(c) $y(\pi/2) = -1$ holds for every $c_2$: infinitely many solutions $y = \cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x$.

The existence–uniqueness theorem is silent here because the conditions are at two different points.
:::

:::intuition
The characteristic equation is the equation's fingerprint. Because $e^{rx}$ reproduces itself under differentiation, feeding it into a constant-coefficient equation just multiplies it by a polynomial in $r$; the equation is satisfied precisely when that polynomial vanishes. A quadratic has two roots, a second-order equation has two constants, and the three ways a quadratic can have roots (two, one, or a complex pair) are the three shapes of solution.
:::

:::pitfall
In the repeated-root case the second solution is $xe^{rx}$, not $e^{rx}$ again; writing $y = c_1 e^{rx} + c_2 e^{rx}$ gives a one-parameter family and cannot satisfy two initial conditions. In the complex case the solution is written with real functions $e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x$ and $e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x$ — do not leave $e^{(\alpha + i\beta)x}$ in a final answer.
:::

## Nonhomogeneous equations: undetermined coefficients

<!-- section: nonhomog -->

:::theorem[Structure of the general solution]{#nonhomogeneous-structure}
Let $y_p$ be any one solution of the nonhomogeneous equation $ay'' + by' + cy = G(x)$, and let $y_c = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ be the general solution of the complementary equation $ay'' + by' + cy = 0$. Then the general solution of the nonhomogeneous equation is

$$y = y_p + y_c = y_p + c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2.$$
:::

:::proof
If $y$ is any solution of the nonhomogeneous equation, then $y - y_p$ satisfies $a(y - y_p)'' + b(y - y_p)' + c(y - y_p) = G - G = 0$, so it solves the complementary equation and equals $c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ for some constants. Conversely $y_p + c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ satisfies the nonhomogeneous equation, by linearity.
:::

So the work splits: find $y_c$ from the characteristic equation, and find one particular solution $y_p$ by any means. When $G$ is built from polynomials, exponentials, sines and cosines — which covers most forcing terms in applications — the method of undetermined coefficients finds $y_p$ by guessing its shape and solving for the coefficients. The guess works because differentiating such functions never produces anything outside their family.

:::recipe[Undetermined coefficients]{#undetermined-coefficients}
For $ay'' + by' + cy = G(x)$, choose the trial form for $y_p$ from the table, with all coefficients unknown:

| forcing term $G(x)$ | trial $y_p$ |
|---|---|
| polynomial of degree $n$ | $A_n x^n + \cdots + A_1 x + A_0$ (full polynomial of degree $n$) |
| $Ce^{kx}$ | $Ae^{kx}$ |
| $C\cos\omega x$ or $C\sin\omega x$ (or both) | $A\cos\omega x + B\sin\omega x$ (always both) |
| $e^{kx} \times$ polynomial of degree $n$ | $e^{kx}\,(A_n x^n + \cdots + A_0)$ |
| $e^{kx}\cos\omega x$ or $e^{kx}\sin\omega x$ | $e^{kx}(A\cos\omega x + B\sin\omega x)$ |
| polynomial $\times\ \cos\omega x$ or $\sin\omega x$ | (polynomial)$\cos\omega x$ + (polynomial)$\sin\omega x$, both of degree $n$ |

Modification rule: if any term of the trial form solves the complementary equation, multiply the whole trial form by $x$; if it still does (repeated root), multiply by $x^2$.

Sum rule: if $G = G_1 + G_2$, find particular solutions for $G_1$ and $G_2$ separately and add them.

Substitute the trial form, match coefficients of like terms, and solve.
:::

The sum rule is superposition again: if $y_{p1}$ handles $G_1$ and $y_{p2}$ handles $G_2$, then $y_{p1} + y_{p2}$ handles $G_1 + G_2$. The modification rule is needed because a trial term that solves the complementary equation is annihilated by the left side and can never produce $G$; multiplying by $x$ produces a new function that is not a solution and whose derivatives bring in the needed terms.

:::example[Polynomial forcing]
Find the general solution of $y'' + y' - 2y = x^2$.
:::

:::solution
Complementary: $r^2 + r - 2 = (r + 2)(r - 1)$, so $y_c = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-2x}$. For $G = x^2$, try the full quadratic $y_p = Ax^2 + Bx + C$, so $y_p' = 2Ax + B$ and $y_p'' = 2A$. Substituting,

$$2A + (2Ax + B) - 2(Ax^2 + Bx + C) = -2A x^2 + (2A - 2B)x + (2A + B - 2C) = x^2.$$

Matching coefficients: $-2A = 1$, so $A = -\tfrac12$; $2A - 2B = 0$, so $B = -\tfrac12$; $2A + B - 2C = 0$, so $-1 - \tfrac12 = 2C$ and $C = -\tfrac34$. Hence

$$y = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-2x} - \tfrac12 x^2 - \tfrac12 x - \tfrac34.$$

Check $y_p$: $y_p'' + y_p' - 2y_p = -1 + (-x - \tfrac12) + (x^2 + x + \tfrac32) = x^2$. Note that the full quadratic was needed even though $G$ has no $x$ or constant term.
:::

:::example[Exponential forcing]
Find a particular solution of $y'' + 4y = e^{3x}$.
:::

:::solution
Try $y_p = Ae^{3x}$: $y_p'' = 9Ae^{3x}$, so $9Ae^{3x} + 4Ae^{3x} = 13Ae^{3x} = e^{3x}$ and $A = \tfrac{1}{13}$. Thus $y_p = \tfrac{1}{13}e^{3x}$, and the general solution is $c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x + \tfrac{1}{13}e^{3x}$. No modification was needed because $e^{3x}$ does not solve $y'' + 4y = 0$.
:::

:::example[The modification rule]
Find a particular solution of $y'' - 3y' + 2y = e^{x}$.
:::

:::solution
The characteristic roots are $1$ and $2$, so $y_c = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{2x}$ — and the naive trial $Ae^x$ is a complementary solution. (Substituting it gives $A - 3A + 2A = 0 \neq 1$: no choice of $A$ works.) Multiply by $x$: $y_p = Axe^x$, with $y_p' = A(1 + x)e^x$ and $y_p'' = A(2 + x)e^x$. Then

$$y_p'' - 3y_p' + 2y_p = Ae^x\left[(2 + x) - 3(1 + x) + 2x\right] = -Ae^x = e^x,$$

so $A = -1$ and $y_p = -xe^x$. Check: with $A = -1$ the bracket is $-1$ and the result is $e^x$.
:::

:::example[A repeated root and the modification rule twice]
Find a particular solution of $y'' - 2y' + y = e^x$.
:::

:::solution
$r^2 - 2r + 1 = (r - 1)^2$: the root $1$ is repeated, so both $e^x$ and $xe^x$ are complementary solutions. The trial form must be $y_p = Ax^2 e^x$. Then $y_p' = A(2x + x^2)e^x$ and $y_p'' = A(2 + 4x + x^2)e^x$, so

$$y_p'' - 2y_p' + y_p = Ae^x\left[(2 + 4x + x^2) - 2(2x + x^2) + x^2\right] = 2Ae^x = e^x,$$

giving $A = \tfrac12$ and $y_p = \tfrac12 x^2 e^x$.
:::

:::example[Resonance]
Find the general solution of $y'' + 4y = \cos 2x$ and describe its behaviour.
:::

:::solution
$y_c = c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x$, and the forcing term is itself a complementary solution, so the trial form is $y_p = x(A\cos 2x + B\sin 2x)$. Differentiating twice (the products need care),

$$y_p' = (A + 2Bx)\cos 2x + (B - 2Ax)\sin 2x, \qquad y_p'' = (4B - 4Ax)\cos 2x + (-4A - 4Bx)\sin 2x.$$

Then $y_p'' + 4y_p = 4B\cos 2x - 4A\sin 2x$, since the terms with $x$ cancel. Matching with $\cos 2x$: $B = \tfrac14$, $A = 0$. So

$$y = c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x + \tfrac14 x\sin 2x.$$

The particular solution has amplitude $x/4$, growing without bound: forcing a system at its own natural frequency produces resonance. This is the mathematics behind a pushed swing, a shattered wine glass and a bridge that must not be marched across in step.
:::

:::example[A sum of forcing terms]
Find a particular solution of $y'' - y = 2x + 3e^{2x}$.
:::

:::solution
$y_c = c_1 e^x + c_2 e^{-x}$. Handle the two terms separately. For $2x$: try $Ax + B$, giving $-(Ax + B) = 2x$, so $A = -2$, $B = 0$. For $3e^{2x}$: try $Ce^{2x}$, giving $4C - C = 3$, so $C = 1$. Adding,

$$y_p = -2x + e^{2x}.$$

Check: $y_p'' - y_p = 4e^{2x} + 2x - e^{2x} = 2x + 3e^{2x}$.
:::

:::intuition
A guitar string plucked and left alone rings at its own frequencies — that is $y_c$, the equation's natural behaviour, which always dies away when there is damping. Bow the string instead, and after the transient it vibrates at the frequency you impose — that is $y_p$, the forced response. Undetermined coefficients says: the forced response has the same *shape* as the force (a sine for a sine, an exponential for an exponential), and only its size and phase need to be computed.
:::

:::pitfall
For a forcing term $\cos\omega x$ the trial form must contain *both* $A\cos\omega x$ and $B\sin\omega x$; the first derivative turns cosines into sines, and omitting the sine term makes the equations for $A$ inconsistent whenever there is a $y'$ term. Likewise a polynomial forcing term needs the full polynomial of that degree, not just the terms present in $G$.
:::

## Variation of parameters

<!-- section: varparams -->

Undetermined coefficients fails when $G$ is not of the exponential–polynomial–trigonometric type: for $y'' + y = \tan x$ or $y'' - 2y' + y = e^x/x$ there is no finite family of functions closed under differentiation to guess from. Variation of parameters is a method that always produces a particular solution, given the complementary solutions, at the price of two integrals.

The idea is to look for $y_p$ in the form $u_1(x)y_1(x) + u_2(x)y_2(x)$, replacing the constants of $y_c$ by functions. Two unknown functions give two degrees of freedom, and the equation is only one condition, so a second condition can be imposed for convenience.

:::theorem[Variation of parameters]{#variation-of-parameters}
Let $y_1, y_2$ be linearly independent solutions of $y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = 0$, with Wronskian $W = y_1 y_2' - y_1' y_2$. Then a particular solution of

$$y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = g(x)$$

is $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$, where

$$u_1 = -\int \frac{y_2\, g}{W}\,dx, \qquad u_2 = \int \frac{y_1\, g}{W}\,dx.$$
:::

:::proof
Set $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$. Then $y_p' = u_1 y_1' + u_2 y_2' + (u_1' y_1 + u_2' y_2)$. Impose the convenient condition

$$u_1' y_1 + u_2' y_2 = 0,$$

so that $y_p' = u_1 y_1' + u_2 y_2'$ and hence $y_p'' = u_1 y_1'' + u_2 y_2'' + u_1' y_1' + u_2' y_2'$. Substituting into the equation and grouping,

$$y_p'' + p y_p' + q y_p = u_1\left(y_1'' + p y_1' + q y_1\right) + u_2\left(y_2'' + p y_2' + q y_2\right) + u_1' y_1' + u_2' y_2'.$$

The two brackets vanish because $y_1, y_2$ solve the homogeneous equation, so the equation reduces to

$$u_1' y_1' + u_2' y_2' = g.$$

Together with the imposed condition this is a linear system for $u_1', u_2'$ with determinant $W \neq 0$. Cramer's rule gives $u_1' = -y_2 g/W$ and $u_2' = y_1 g/W$; integrate to get $u_1, u_2$. Constants of integration may be dropped, since they only add multiples of $y_1, y_2$ to $y_p$.
:::

Note the equation must be in standard form, with leading coefficient $1$: for $ay'' + by' + cy = G$, use $g = G/a$.

:::recipe[Variation of parameters]{#variation-of-parameters-recipe}
1. Write the equation as $y'' + py' + qy = g$ (divide by the leading coefficient).
2. Find $y_1, y_2$ and compute $W = y_1 y_2' - y_1' y_2$.
3. Compute $u_1 = -\int y_2 g/W\,dx$ and $u_2 = \int y_1 g/W\,dx$, omitting constants.
4. $y_p = u_1 y_1 + u_2 y_2$; the general solution is $y_p + c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$. Any part of $y_p$ that is a multiple of $y_1$ or $y_2$ may be dropped.
:::

:::example[Forcing by $\tan x$]
Solve $y'' + y = \tan x$ on $-\pi/2 < x < \pi/2$.
:::

:::solution
$y_1 = \cos x$, $y_2 = \sin x$, and $W = \cos x\cos x - (-\sin x)\sin x = 1$. Then

$$u_1' = -\sin x\tan x = -\frac{\sin^2 x}{\cos x} = -\frac{1 - \cos^2 x}{\cos x} = \cos x - \sec x, \qquad u_2' = \cos x\tan x = \sin x.$$

Integrating, $u_1 = \sin x - \ln(\sec x + \tan x)$ and $u_2 = -\cos x$ (the absolute value is unnecessary since $\sec x + \tan x > 0$ on this interval). So

$$y_p = \cos x\left[\sin x - \ln(\sec x + \tan x)\right] - \sin x\cos x = -\cos x\,\ln(\sec x + \tan x),$$

and the general solution is $y = c_1\cos x + c_2\sin x - \cos x\ln(\sec x + \tan x)$. Sanity check at $x = 0$: $y_p(0) = -\ln 1 = 0$, and $y_p$ is odd, as $\tan x$ is.
:::

:::example[Forcing that undetermined coefficients cannot handle]
Solve $y'' - 2y' + y = \dfrac{e^x}{x}$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
The characteristic root $1$ is repeated, so $y_1 = e^x$, $y_2 = xe^x$, and

$$W = e^x(1 + x)e^x - e^x\cdot xe^x = e^{2x}.$$

With $g = e^x/x$:

$$u_1' = -\frac{xe^x\cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}} = -1, \qquad u_2' = \frac{e^x \cdot e^x/x}{e^{2x}} = \frac{1}{x},$$

so $u_1 = -x$ and $u_2 = \ln x$. Then $y_p = -xe^x + xe^x\ln x$; the term $-xe^x$ is a multiple of $y_2$ and can be dropped, leaving $y_p = xe^x\ln x$ and

$$y = c_1 e^x + c_2 xe^x + xe^x\ln x.$$

Check: writing $y_p = e^x v$ with $v = x\ln x$, one finds $y_p'' - 2y_p' + y_p = e^x v'' = e^x/x$, since $v' = \ln x + 1$ and $v'' = 1/x$.
:::

:::example[Comparing the two methods]
Find a particular solution of $y'' + 4y = \sec 2x$ on $-\pi/4 < x < \pi/4$.
:::

:::solution
$y_1 = \cos 2x$, $y_2 = \sin 2x$, $W = 2\cos^2 2x + 2\sin^2 2x = 2$. Then

$$u_1' = -\frac{\sin 2x\sec 2x}{2} = -\frac{\tan 2x}{2}, \qquad u_2' = \frac{\cos 2x\sec 2x}{2} = \frac12,$$

so $u_1 = \tfrac14\ln(\cos 2x)$ and $u_2 = \tfrac{x}{2}$, and

$$y_p = \tfrac14\cos 2x\,\ln(\cos 2x) + \tfrac{x}{2}\sin 2x.$$

Undetermined coefficients could not have started, because $\sec 2x$ has no finite trial family. Variation of parameters simply asks for two integrals.
:::

:::intuition
The constants in $y_c = c_1 y_1 + c_2 y_2$ are the settings of two dials that describe how the system rings on its own. Variation of parameters lets the dials turn continuously as time passes, and asks how they must turn so that the forcing is exactly accounted for. The answer is a pair of integrals of the force against the natural modes — which is why the method never needs a lucky guess.
:::

## Applications: springs and oscillations

<!-- section: springs -->

A mass $m$ on a spring, displaced $x$ from its rest position, feels the restoring force $-kx$ (Hooke's law, $k > 0$ the spring constant), a damping force $-c\,x'$ proportional to velocity (air resistance, a dashpot), and possibly an external force $F(t)$. Newton's second law $m x'' = \text{force}$ gives:

:::definition[The mass–spring equation]{#mass-spring}
$$m\,x'' + c\,x' + k\,x = F(t), \qquad m, k > 0,\ c \ge 0.$$

The motion is free if $F = 0$ and forced otherwise; undamped if $c = 0$ and damped otherwise.
:::

Free undamped motion, $mx'' + kx = 0$, has characteristic equation $mr^2 + k = 0$ with roots $\pm i\omega$ where $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, so

$$x = c_1\cos\omega t + c_2\sin\omega t = R\cos(\omega t - \delta),$$

where $R = \sqrt{c_1^2 + c_2^2}$ is the amplitude and $\delta$ the phase, with $\cos\delta = c_1/R$, $\sin\delta = c_2/R$. (The rewriting is the cosine subtraction formula run backward.) The motion is simple harmonic with angular frequency $\omega$, period $2\pi/\omega$ and frequency $\omega/(2\pi)$; a stiffer spring or lighter mass oscillates faster, and the amplitude is set entirely by the initial conditions.

:::example[Simple harmonic motion]
A spring with natural length $0.5$ m is stretched to $0.7$ m by a force of $25.6$ N. A mass of $2$ kg is attached, pulled to $0.7$ m and released from rest. Find the position at time $t$.
:::

:::solution
Hooke's law gives $k = 25.6/0.2 = 128$ N/m, so the equation is $2x'' + 128x = 0$, or $x'' + 64x = 0$, and $\omega = 8$. The general solution is $x = c_1\cos 8t + c_2\sin 8t$. The initial conditions are $x(0) = 0.2$ (stretched $0.2$ m beyond rest) and $x'(0) = 0$, so $c_1 = 0.2$ and $8c_2 = 0$:

$$x(t) = 0.2\cos 8t.$$

The mass oscillates between $\pm 0.2$ m with period $2\pi/8 = \pi/4 \approx 0.79$ s.
:::

With damping, $mr^2 + cr + k = 0$ has roots $r = \dfrac{-c \pm \sqrt{c^2 - 4mk}}{2m}$, and the sign of the discriminant sorts the motion into three kinds.

:::theorem[The three damping regimes]{#damping-regimes}
For free damped motion $mx'' + cx' + kx = 0$ with $c > 0$:

1. Overdamped, $c^2 - 4mk > 0$: two distinct negative real roots $r_1, r_2$, and $x = c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}$. No oscillation; the mass creeps back to equilibrium, crossing it at most once.
2. Critically damped, $c^2 - 4mk = 0$: repeated root $r = -c/(2m)$, and $x = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-ct/(2m)}$. No oscillation; the fastest return to equilibrium without overshoot.
3. Underdamped, $c^2 - 4mk < 0$: complex roots $-\dfrac{c}{2m} \pm i\omega_d$ with $\omega_d = \dfrac{\sqrt{4mk - c^2}}{2m}$, and $x = e^{-ct/(2m)}\left(c_1\cos\omega_d t + c_2\sin\omega_d t\right)$. Oscillation at the reduced frequency $\omega_d < \omega$, with amplitude decaying like $e^{-ct/(2m)}$.

In every case $x \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$.
:::

:::proof
The three cases are the three cases of the characteristic-roots theorem. In case 1, both roots are negative because $\sqrt{c^2 - 4mk} < c$. In case 2, $r = -c/(2m) < 0$. In case 3 the real part is $-c/(2m) < 0$. In all three the exponentials decay, so $x \to 0$; and a sum $c_1 e^{r_1 t} + c_2 e^{r_2 t}$ with $r_1 \neq r_2$ vanishes for at most one $t$, which is the "at most once" claim in case 1.
:::

:::example[Overdamped motion]
Solve $x'' + 6x' + 5x = 0$ with $x(0) = 1$, $x'(0) = 0$, and show that the mass never crosses the equilibrium.
:::

:::solution
$c^2 - 4mk = 36 - 20 = 16 > 0$: overdamped. $r^2 + 6r + 5 = (r + 1)(r + 5)$, so $x = c_1 e^{-t} + c_2 e^{-5t}$. The conditions give $c_1 + c_2 = 1$ and $-c_1 - 5c_2 = 0$, so $c_1 = -5c_2$, $-4c_2 = 1$, $c_2 = -\tfrac14$, $c_1 = \tfrac54$:

$$x(t) = \tfrac54 e^{-t} - \tfrac14 e^{-5t}.$$

Since $e^{-t} > e^{-5t}$ for $t > 0$, $x(t) > \tfrac54 e^{-5t} - \tfrac14 e^{-5t} > 0$: the mass approaches equilibrium from above and never crosses it. Check: $x(0) = 1$, $x'(0) = -\tfrac54 + \tfrac54 = 0$.
:::

:::example[Critical damping]
A mass $m = 1$ with spring constant $k = 4$ is critically damped. Find $c$, and solve with $x(0) = 0$, $x'(0) = 3$.
:::

:::solution
Critical damping requires $c^2 = 4mk = 16$, so $c = 4$ and the equation is $x'' + 4x' + 4x = 0$ with repeated root $r = -2$: $x = (c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-2t}$. Then $x(0) = c_1 = 0$ and $x'(0) = c_2 - 2c_1 = c_2 = 3$:

$$x(t) = 3te^{-2t}.$$

The mass, struck at equilibrium with velocity $3$, moves out to a maximum at $t = \tfrac12$ (where $x' = 3(1 - 2t)e^{-2t} = 0$), reaching $x = \tfrac32 e^{-1} \approx 0.55$, then returns to equilibrium without crossing it. Door closers and car shock absorbers are tuned to be near this regime.
:::

:::example[Underdamped motion]
Solve $x'' + 2x' + 10x = 0$ with $x(0) = 2$, $x'(0) = 0$, and find the quasi-period.
:::

:::solution
$c^2 - 4mk = 4 - 40 < 0$: underdamped. $r = -1 \pm 3i$, so $x = e^{-t}(c_1\cos 3t + c_2\sin 3t)$ with $\omega_d = 3$, compared with $\omega = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ for the undamped spring. From $x(0) = 2$, $c_1 = 2$; from $x'(0) = -c_1 + 3c_2 = 0$, $c_2 = \tfrac23$:

$$x(t) = e^{-t}\left(2\cos 3t + \tfrac23\sin 3t\right).$$

The oscillation has quasi-period $2\pi/3 \approx 2.09$ s, and each successive peak is smaller by the factor $e^{-2\pi/3} \approx 0.12$.
:::

Now add a periodic external force, $F(t) = F_0\cos\omega_0 t$. For the undamped system $mx'' + kx = F_0\cos\omega_0 t$, undetermined coefficients with the trial $A\cos\omega_0 t$ gives $A(k - m\omega_0^2) = F_0$, so as long as $\omega_0 \neq \omega$,

$$x_p = \frac{F_0}{m\left(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2\right)}\cos\omega_0 t, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{k/m},$$

and the general solution is this plus $c_1\cos\omega t + c_2\sin\omega t$: a superposition of two oscillations at the natural and the forcing frequency. As $\omega_0$ approaches $\omega$ the amplitude of $x_p$ grows without bound, and at $\omega_0 = \omega$ the trial form must be multiplied by $t$; the resonance example gives

$$x_p = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega}\,t\sin\omega t \qquad (\omega_0 = \omega),$$

an oscillation whose amplitude grows linearly forever. This is pure resonance. Near resonance a related phenomenon appears: with $x(0) = x'(0) = 0$ and $\omega_0$ close to $\omega$, the solution is

$$x = \frac{F_0}{m(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)}\left(\cos\omega_0 t - \cos\omega t\right) = \frac{2F_0}{m(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)}\,\sin\frac{(\omega - \omega_0)t}{2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega + \omega_0)t}{2},$$

a fast oscillation at the average frequency whose amplitude is itself slowly oscillating at the small frequency $(\omega - \omega_0)/2$. The slow throbbing is called beats; it is what you hear when two nearly-in-tune strings are struck together.

With damping, $mx'' + cx' + kx = F_0\cos\omega_0 t$, the complementary solution decays whatever the initial conditions — it is the transient — and every solution settles down to the particular solution, the steady state, which oscillates at the forcing frequency with a bounded amplitude. Damping makes true resonance impossible, but the steady-state amplitude still peaks sharply when $\omega_0$ is near $\omega$ and $c$ is small.

:::example[Steady state of a forced damped spring]
Find the steady-state motion of $x'' + 4x' + 4x = 8\cos 2t$.
:::

:::solution
Try $x_p = A\cos 2t + B\sin 2t$: $x_p' = -2A\sin 2t + 2B\cos 2t$, $x_p'' = -4A\cos 2t - 4B\sin 2t$. Substituting,

$$(-4A + 8B + 4A)\cos 2t + (-4B - 8A + 4B)\sin 2t = 8B\cos 2t - 8A\sin 2t = 8\cos 2t,$$

so $B = 1$ and $A = 0$: the steady state is $x_p = \sin 2t$. The transient $(c_1 + c_2 t)e^{-2t}$ (critical damping) dies away, and the mass ends up oscillating with amplitude $1$ at the forcing frequency, a quarter-cycle behind the force.
:::

:::intuition
A child on a swing is the whole section. Left alone with no friction, the swing repeats forever: simple harmonic motion. Rusty chains make each swing a little smaller: underdamped. Swing in thick honey and it does not swing at all, just oozes back to the bottom: overdamped, or if the honey is exactly right, critically damped. And a parent pushing in time with the swing's own rhythm makes it go higher and higher: resonance. Pushing at the wrong rhythm just makes it jerky — that is forcing off resonance, and pushing at nearly the right rhythm produces the slow surge and fade of beats.
:::

:::pitfall
The damping classification depends on all three constants through $c^2 - 4mk$, not on $c$ alone: $c = 4$ is critical for $m = 1$, $k = 4$ but overdamped for $m = 1$, $k = 1$. And the frequency of a damped oscillation is $\omega_d = \sqrt{4mk - c^2}/(2m)$, always less than the undamped $\sqrt{k/m}$.
:::

## Electric circuits

<!-- section: circuits -->

A series circuit with a resistor $R$, inductor $L$, capacitor $C$ and voltage source $E(t)$ obeys Kirchhoff's law: the applied voltage equals the sum of the drops across the three elements, which are $RI$, $L\,dI/dt$ and $Q/C$, where $Q$ is the charge on the capacitor and $I = dQ/dt$ the current. In terms of $Q$,

$$L\,Q'' + R\,Q' + \frac{1}{C}\,Q = E(t),$$

which is the mass–spring equation with $m \leftrightarrow L$, $c \leftrightarrow R$, $k \leftrightarrow 1/C$, $x \leftrightarrow Q$ and $F \leftrightarrow E$. Every result about springs transfers word for word: the circuit's natural frequency is $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, resistance plays the role of damping, and the three damping regimes are decided by $R^2 - 4L/C$. Differentiating the equation gives the same equation for the current, $LI'' + RI' + I/C = E'(t)$.

| mechanical | electrical |
|---|---|
| displacement $x$ | charge $Q$ |
| velocity $x'$ | current $I = Q'$ |
| mass $m$ | inductance $L$ |
| damping $c$ | resistance $R$ |
| spring constant $k$ | $1/C$ (elastance) |
| external force $F(t)$ | voltage $E(t)$ |

:::example[An undamped LC circuit]
A circuit has $L = 1$ H, no resistance, and $C = 0.01$ F. The capacitor initially carries a charge of $0.5$ C and no current flows. Find $Q(t)$ and the frequency of the oscillation.
:::

:::solution
The equation is $Q'' + 100Q = 0$, so $Q = c_1\cos 10t + c_2\sin 10t$. From $Q(0) = 0.5$ and $Q'(0) = 0$: $Q = 0.5\cos 10t$. The charge sloshes back and forth between the capacitor plates at angular frequency $10$ rad/s, that is $10/(2\pi) \approx 1.59$ Hz, forever — the electrical analogue of a frictionless spring. The current is $I = -5\sin 10t$.
:::

:::example[A forced RLC circuit]
A circuit has $L = 1$ H, $R = 2\ \Omega$, $C = 0.2$ F and voltage $E(t) = 10\cos t$ V. Initially the charge and current are zero. Find $Q(t)$ and the steady-state current.
:::

:::solution
The equation is $Q'' + 2Q' + 5Q = 10\cos t$. Complementary: $r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0$ gives $r = -1 \pm 2i$, so $Q_c = e^{-t}(c_1\cos 2t + c_2\sin 2t)$: underdamped, since $R^2 - 4L/C = 4 - 20 < 0$. Particular: try $Q_p = A\cos t + B\sin t$,

$$(-A + 2B + 5A)\cos t + (-B - 2A + 5B)\sin t = (4A + 2B)\cos t + (4B - 2A)\sin t = 10\cos t.$$

So $4B = 2A$, i.e. $A = 2B$, and $8B + 2B = 10$, giving $B = 1$, $A = 2$: $Q_p = 2\cos t + \sin t$. Now the initial conditions: $Q(0) = c_1 + 2 = 0$, so $c_1 = -2$; and since $Q' = e^{-t}[(-c_1 + 2c_2)\cos 2t + (-c_2 - 2c_1)\sin 2t] - 2\sin t + \cos t$, $Q'(0) = -c_1 + 2c_2 + 1 = 0$ gives $2c_2 = -3$, $c_2 = -\tfrac32$:

$$Q(t) = e^{-t}\left(-2\cos 2t - \tfrac32\sin 2t\right) + 2\cos t + \sin t.$$

The first term is the transient; the steady-state charge is $2\cos t + \sin t$ and the steady-state current is its derivative, $I = \cos t - 2\sin t$, with amplitude $\sqrt5$ A. Check: $Q(0) = -2 + 2 = 0$.
:::

:::intuition
An inductor resists changes in current the way mass resists changes in velocity; a capacitor pushes charge back the way a spring pushes a mass back; a resistor dissipates energy the way friction does. A tuned radio is the resonance section in electrical clothing — the circuit's natural frequency $1/\sqrt{LC}$ is adjusted until it matches the station's, and that station's signal is the one that produces a large response.
:::

## Cauchy–Euler equations

<!-- section: cauchyeuler -->

The constant-coefficient theory does not extend to general variable coefficients, but one family of variable-coefficient equations is almost as easy.

:::definition[Cauchy–Euler equation]{#cauchy-euler}
A second-order Cauchy–Euler (or equidimensional) equation has the form

$$x^2 y'' + b\,x\,y' + c\,y = 0, \qquad x > 0,$$

with constants $b, c$: each derivative $y^{(k)}$ is multiplied by $x^k$.
:::

Because $x^k$ times the $k$th derivative of a power $x^r$ is again a multiple of $x^r$, the natural trial solution is $y = x^r$. Substituting $y' = rx^{r-1}$, $y'' = r(r-1)x^{r-2}$:

$$x^2 r(r - 1)x^{r-2} + bx\,rx^{r-1} + cx^r = x^r\left[r(r - 1) + br + c\right] = 0,$$

so $x^r$ is a solution exactly when $r$ satisfies the indicial equation $r^2 + (b - 1)r + c = 0$.

:::theorem[Solutions of the Cauchy–Euler equation]{#cauchy-euler-solutions}
Let $r_1, r_2$ be the roots of $r^2 + (b - 1)r + c = 0$. For $x > 0$:

1. Real distinct roots: $y = c_1 x^{r_1} + c_2 x^{r_2}$.
2. Repeated root $r$: $y = x^r(c_1 + c_2\ln x)$.
3. Complex roots $\alpha \pm i\beta$: $y = x^\alpha\left(c_1\cos(\beta\ln x) + c_2\sin(\beta\ln x)\right)$.
:::

The pattern is the constant-coefficient theorem with $e^{rx}$ replaced by $x^r$ and $x$ replaced by $\ln x$, and the reason is that the substitution $x = e^t$ turns a Cauchy–Euler equation into a constant-coefficient equation in $t$: by the chain rule $x\,dy/dx = dy/dt$ and $x^2\,d^2y/dx^2 = d^2y/dt^2 - dy/dt$, so the equation becomes $\ddot y + (b - 1)\dot y + cy = 0$, whose characteristic equation is the indicial equation. Its solutions $e^{rt}, te^{rt}, e^{\alpha t}\cos\beta t$ become $x^r, x^r\ln x, x^\alpha\cos(\beta\ln x)$.

:::example[Distinct real roots]
Solve $x^2 y'' + 2xy' - 6y = 0$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
The indicial equation is $r(r - 1) + 2r - 6 = r^2 + r - 6 = (r + 3)(r - 2) = 0$, so

$$y = c_1 x^2 + c_2 x^{-3}.$$

Check with $y = x^2$: $x^2 \cdot 2 + 2x \cdot 2x - 6x^2 = 0$.
:::

:::example[A repeated root]
Solve $x^2 y'' - 3xy' + 4y = 0$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
$r(r - 1) - 3r + 4 = r^2 - 4r + 4 = (r - 2)^2$: repeated root $2$, so

$$y = x^2\left(c_1 + c_2\ln x\right).$$

Check with $y = x^2\ln x$: $y' = 2x\ln x + x$, $y'' = 2\ln x + 3$, and $x^2(2\ln x + 3) - 3x(2x\ln x + x) + 4x^2\ln x = (2 - 6 + 4)x^2\ln x + (3 - 3)x^2 = 0$.
:::

:::example[Complex roots]
Solve $x^2 y'' + xy' + y = 0$ for $x > 0$.
:::

:::solution
$r(r - 1) + r + 1 = r^2 + 1 = 0$ gives $r = \pm i$, so $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1$ and

$$y = c_1\cos(\ln x) + c_2\sin(\ln x).$$

The solution oscillates, but ever more slowly as $x$ grows, since $\ln x$ grows slowly; and infinitely fast as $x \to 0^+$.
:::

## Power-series solutions

<!-- section: series -->

When neither the coefficients are constant nor the equation is Cauchy–Euler, there may be no closed-form solution at all — but there is usually a power series. Assume $y = \sum c_n x^n$, substitute, and match coefficients: the equation becomes a recurrence relation for the $c_n$, and the recurrence determines the series.

:::recipe[Power-series solution about $x = 0$]{#power-series-method}
1. Write $y = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n x^n$, so $y' = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_n x^{n-1}$ and $y'' = \displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_n x^{n-2}$.
2. Substitute into the equation and shift indices so that every sum is a sum of multiples of the same power $x^n$.
3. Set the total coefficient of each $x^n$ to zero to obtain a recurrence relation.
4. Solve the recurrence in terms of the free coefficients (for a second-order equation, $c_0$ and $c_1$), and identify the series if possible.
:::

:::example[Sine and cosine from their equation]
Solve $y'' + y = 0$ by power series.
:::

:::solution
Substitute the series. In $y''$, replace $n$ by $n + 2$ so that the power is $x^n$:

$$y'' = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (n + 2)(n + 1)c_{n+2}x^n, \qquad y'' + y = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left[(n + 2)(n + 1)c_{n+2} + c_n\right]x^n = 0.$$

A power series is identically zero only if every coefficient is, so

$$c_{n+2} = -\frac{c_n}{(n + 2)(n + 1)}, \qquad n \ge 0.$$

The even coefficients are determined by $c_0$: $c_2 = -\dfrac{c_0}{2}$, $c_4 = -\dfrac{c_2}{12} = \dfrac{c_0}{24}$, $c_6 = -\dfrac{c_4}{30} = -\dfrac{c_0}{720}$, and in general $c_{2k} = \dfrac{(-1)^k c_0}{(2k)!}$. The odd ones by $c_1$: $c_3 = -\dfrac{c_1}{6}$, $c_5 = \dfrac{c_1}{120}$, and $c_{2k+1} = \dfrac{(-1)^k c_1}{(2k + 1)!}$. So

$$y = c_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} + c_1\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} = c_0\cos x + c_1\sin x,$$

recovering the known general solution, with $c_0 = y(0)$ and $c_1 = y'(0)$ as they should be. Both series converge for all $x$ by the ratio test.
:::

:::example[A variable-coefficient equation]
Solve $y'' + xy' + y = 0$ by power series, and identify the solution with $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$.
:::

:::solution
Here $xy' = \sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_n x^n$, which also equals $\sum_{n=0}^\infty nc_n x^n$ since the $n = 0$ term is zero. Collecting the coefficient of $x^n$:

$$(n + 2)(n + 1)c_{n+2} + nc_n + c_n = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_{n+2} = -\frac{(n + 1)c_n}{(n + 2)(n + 1)} = -\frac{c_n}{n + 2}.$$

With $c_0 = 1$ and $c_1 = 0$ all odd coefficients vanish, and $c_2 = -\dfrac12$, $c_4 = \dfrac{1}{8}$, $c_6 = -\dfrac{1}{48}$, $c_8 = \dfrac{1}{384}$, that is $c_{2k} = \dfrac{(-1)^k}{2^k\,k!}$. So

$$y = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right)^k = e^{-x^2/2}.$$

Check: $y' = -xy$, so $y'' = -y - xy' $, and $y'' + xy' + y = -y - xy' + xy' + y = 0$. The second solution, from $c_1 = 1$, has no elementary closed form — which is exactly the situation the method exists for.
:::

:::intuition
A power series is a polynomial with infinitely many knobs, and a differential equation is a rule relating each knob to the ones before it. Turn the first one or two by hand (the initial conditions) and the rule sets all the rest. The method never asks whether the answer has a name; it simply computes it, one coefficient at a time.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Dropping the constant of integration, or writing one on each side and never combining them. A first-order general solution has exactly one arbitrary constant; a second-order one has two.
- Forgetting the absolute value in $\ln|y|$ after separating, and then losing the negative solutions; or forgetting that the constant $A = \pm e^C$ can also be $0$, which restores the equilibrium solution you divided out.
- Computing the integrating factor from an equation that is not yet in standard form. Divide by the coefficient of $y'$ first, then read off $P(x)$.
- Multiplying only the left side of a linear equation by $\mu$. The right side must be multiplied too: the answer involves $\int \mu Q$, not $\int Q$.
- In Euler's method, using the slope at the wrong point — it is always $f(x_n, y_n)$ with the current approximation, not the exact value and not the next point.
- Concluding that an equilibrium is stable because $f'(c) \le 0$. Only $f'(c) < 0$ decides; when $f'(c) = 0$ look at the sign of $f$ on each side.
- Confusing the carrying capacity $M$ with the initial population, or writing $C = y_0$ instead of $C = (M - y_0)/y_0$ in the logistic solution.
- Getting the characteristic equation wrong by a sign or by dropping a coefficient: $y'' - 5y' + 6y = 0$ gives $r^2 - 5r + 6$, not $r^2 + 5r + 6$.
- Treating a repeated root like two distinct roots and writing $c_1 e^{rx} + c_2 e^{rx}$; the second solution is $xe^{rx}$.
- Leaving out the sine term when the forcing is a pure cosine, or omitting lower-degree terms when the forcing is a polynomial. The trial form is the whole family.
- Forgetting the modification rule: if the trial form solves the homogeneous equation, multiply by $x$ (by $x^2$ for a repeated root). Otherwise the coefficients cannot be solved for.
- Using variation of parameters on an equation whose leading coefficient is not $1$; divide first, or the formula for $u_1', u_2'$ is off by that factor.
- Deciding the damping type from $c$ alone. It is the sign of $c^2 - 4mk$ that matters.
- Not checking the final answer in the original equation and initial conditions. Substitution takes a minute and catches almost every error above.
