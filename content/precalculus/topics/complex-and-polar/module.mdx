# Complex Numbers & Polar Coordinates

Complex arithmetic, the complex plane, polar form, De Moivre's theorem, polar and parametric curves.

## Complex number

<!-- section: complex-numbers -->

Complex numbers extend the reals to solve equations like x²+1=0 and provide algebra for rotations and oscillations.

### Complex Numbers

A **complex number** is an expression z = a + bi where a, b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit satisfying i² = -1.

- Real part Re(z) = a
- Imaginary part Im(z) = b
- Modulus |z| = √(a² + b²)
- Conjugate \bar z = a - bi (note \bar{(z w)} = \bar z \bar w, |z|² = z \bar z)

Arithmetic follows the distributive law, replacing i² with -1 whenever it appears. Addition and subtraction act componentwise (add real parts, add imaginary parts); multiplication uses FOIL and then the rule $i^2 = -1$.

The complex numbers form a field: every nonzero z has a multiplicative inverse $\frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}$. The condition $z \neq 0$ matters — dividing by zero is undefined here just as in the reals — and $|z|^2 = a^2 + b^2$ is a positive real, so the inverse is a genuine complex number.

:::intuition
Complex numbers are "2D numbers": one real direction, one imaginary direction at right angles. Adding them is vector addition; multiplying scales and rotates. The conjugate is the mirror image across the real axis — flipping a number over and multiplying by it always lands you on the real axis (at $a^2 + b^2$), which is the trick that clears $i$ out of a denominator.
:::

:::example[Multiply (2 + 3i)(1 - i) and simplify]
Distribute (FOIL): $2\cdot 1 + 2\cdot(-i) + 3i\cdot 1 + 3i\cdot(-i) = 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i^2$.
:::

:::solution
1. Replace $i^2 = -1$: $-3i^2 = -3(-1) = +3$.
2. Combine real parts and imaginary parts: $(2+3) + (-2 + 3)i = 5 + i$.
3. Sanity check with moduli: $|2+3i| = \sqrt{13}$ and $|1-i| = \sqrt{2}$, so the product should have modulus $\sqrt{13}\cdot\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{26}$. Indeed $|5+i| = \sqrt{25+1} = \sqrt{26}$ ✓.
:::

:::example[Divide $\frac{3 + 2i}{1 - i}$ and write in $a + bi$ form]
Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, $1 + i$:
$$\frac{3+2i}{1-i}\cdot\frac{1+i}{1+i} = \frac{(3+2i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(1+i)}.$$
Denominator becomes real: $(1-i)(1+i) = 1^2 - i^2 = 1 - (-1) = 2$.
Numerator: $(3+2i)(1+i) = 3 + 3i + 2i + 2i^2 = 3 + 5i - 2 = 1 + 5i$.
Divide each part by 2: $\frac{1 + 5i}{2} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{5}{2}i$.
Sanity check: multiply back, $\left(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{5}{2}i\right)(1 - i) = \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}i + \tfrac{5}{2}i - \tfrac{5}{2}i^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{5}{2} + 2i = 3 + 2i$ ✓.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **i² = -1 arithmetic**: Forgetting to replace i² with -1 when multiplying complex numbers leads to wrong real/imaginary parts.
- **Inverse of complex**: 1/(a+bi) requires multiplying numerator and denominator by conjugate; forgetting yields non-real denominator.
- **Modulus of product**: |z w| = |z| |w| (magnitudes multiply); not |z + w|.
- **Conjugate of sum vs product**: \bar (z+w) = \bar z + \bar w always; same for product.
- **Writing j instead of i**: In some engineering contexts j is used, but in math i is standard.
- **Forgetting complex include the reals**: When b=0, z=a is real.

## Complex plane

<!-- section: complex-plane -->

Plotting complex numbers as points in the plane turns algebra into geometry and reveals multiplication as rotation+scaling.

### The Complex Plane

Plot z = a + bi as the point (a, b) in the Cartesian plane. The horizontal axis is the real axis; vertical is the imaginary axis.

- |z| is the distance from origin to the point.
- Argument arg(z) is the angle from positive real axis to the ray from origin through z (principal value in (-π, π]).

Addition is the parallelogram law (vector sum). Multiplication by a fixed complex number $w$ is a similarity transformation: it scales every point's distance from the origin by $|w|$ and rotates it by $\arg(w)$. This is why "$i$ is a $90°$ turn" — multiplying by $i = 1\cdot(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ keeps the length the same ($|i| = 1$) and rotates by $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$.

:::intuition
The complex plane turns algebra into geometry: every complex number is a point or an arrow from the origin. Pure imaginaries live on the vertical axis, reals on the horizontal. Multiplying by a number is like "grab the whole plane, spin it by that number's angle, and stretch it by that number's length."
:::

:::example[Plot  -1 + 2i , compute its modulus and principal argument]
Point $(-1, 2)$ lives in quadrant II.
:::

:::solution
1. $|z| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}$.
2. The reference angle is $\arctan\left(\frac{|{\rm Im}|}{|{\rm Re}|}\right) = \arctan\frac{2}{1}$. In quadrant II the principal argument is $\pi$ minus this: $\arg(z) = \pi - \arctan 2 \approx 3.1416 - 1.107 = 2.034$ rad.
3. Sanity check the quadrant: $2.034$ rad is between $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\approx 1.571$ and $\pi\approx 3.1416$, i.e. in quadrant II, matching the plot ✓.
:::

:::example[Multiply $(2 + i)$ by $i$ and confirm it is a $90°$ rotation]
Algebraically: $i(2 + i) = 2i + i^2 = -1 + 2i$.
:::

:::solution
1. Geometrically, multiplying by $i$ (which has $|i|=1$, $\arg(i)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$) should keep the length and rotate by $90°$ counterclockwise.
2. Check length: $|2+i| = \sqrt{5}$ and $|-1+2i| = \sqrt{5}$ — unchanged ✓.
3. Check the turn: the point $(2,1)$ rotated $90°$ counterclockwise about the origin goes to $(-1, 2)$, which is exactly $-1 + 2i$ ✓.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Principal argument range**: arg usually taken in (-π, π]; using (0,2π] or other intervals can cause sign errors in polar form.
- **Plotting imag as x**: Real is horizontal x, imag vertical y. 3-2i is (3,-2) not (-2,3).
- **arg of negative real**: arg(-1) = π (or -π boundary), not 0.
- **Modulus vs argument mixup**: |z| is length (always ≥0), arg is angle.
- **Forgetting conjugate is reflection over real axis**: a-bi is (a,-b).
- **Vector addition vs complex**: Same, but remember i direction is perpendicular.

## Polar form of complex numbers

<!-- section: polar-form -->

Polar form turns multiplication and powers into arithmetic on the modulus r and argument θ. De Moivre follows immediately.

### Polar Form of Complex Numbers

Any nonzero complex z can be written z = r (cos θ + i sin θ) where r = |z| ≥ 0 and θ = arg(z).

This is also written $r\,\text{cis}\,\theta$ (shorthand for $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$) or, via Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, as $r e^{i\theta}$.

Conversion:
- $r = |z|$, $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(\text{Im}, \text{Re})$
- $a = r\cos\theta$, $b = r\sin\theta$

Here $\operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$ is the two-argument arctangent that returns the angle in the correct quadrant (unlike $\arctan(b/a)$, which cannot tell quadrant I from III or II from IV). Polar form makes multiplication, division, and powers trivial, because it converts them into arithmetic on $r$ and $\theta$: to multiply, **multiply the moduli and add the arguments**; to divide, divide the moduli and subtract the arguments.

:::intuition
Instead of (x,y) coordinates, describe the number by "how far from origin" and "which direction." Multiplication then becomes "multiply the distances and add the angles" — like two dancers whose spins add up.
:::

:::example[Write -2 - 2i in polar form]
$r = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{4+4} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$.
:::

:::solution
1. The point $(-2,-2)$ is in quadrant III. Its reference angle is $\arctan\frac{2}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4}$, so the principal argument (in $(-\pi,\pi]$) is $\theta = -\pi + \frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{3\pi}{4}$ (equivalently $\frac{5\pi}{4}$ if you prefer $[0,2\pi)$).
2. So $-2 - 2i = 2\sqrt{2}\left(\cos\left(-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right) + i\sin\left(-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right)$.
3. Sanity check by converting back: $\cos(-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}) = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, $\sin(-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}) = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, so $2\sqrt2\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}i\right) = -2 - 2i$ ✓.
:::

:::example[Multiply $z = 2\,\text{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $w = 3\,\text{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{4}$ using polar arithmetic]
Multiply the moduli: $|zw| = 2\cdot 3 = 6$.
:::

:::solution
1. Add the arguments: $\arg(zw) = \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{2\pi}{12} + \frac{3\pi}{12} = \frac{5\pi}{12}$.
2. So $zw = 6\,\text{cis}\,\frac{5\pi}{12}$.
3. Sanity check the modulus rule against $|zw| = |z||w|$: $|z| = 2$, $|w| = 3$, product $6$ ✓, and $\frac{5\pi}{12} = 75°$ lies in quadrant I, as expected for two quadrant-I factors.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Principal argument range**: arg usually taken in (-π, π]; using (0,2π] or other intervals can cause sign errors in polar form.
- **Polar to Cartesian conversion sign**: cos( quadrant II ) is negative; students often drop the sign when converting back.
- **r negative in polar plots**: Forgetting that negative r means go opposite direction produces points in the wrong half-plane.
- **Forgetting r=0 is allowed**: z=0 has r=0, θ arbitrary (but usually 0).
- **cis or e form not recognized**: Equivalent to the cos + i sin.
- **θ in degrees when using formulas**: The trig functions in the form expect radians for calculus, but identities hold either.

## De Moivre's theorem

<!-- section: de-moivre -->

De Moivre turns powers of complex in polar form into simple scaling of r and adding to the angle. It is the key to roots of unity and polynomial roots.

### De Moivre's Theorem

**De Moivre's theorem**: $[r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n(\cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta))$ for integer $n$ (with $r \geq 0$ real). It follows from the polar multiplication rule applied $n$ times, and extends to negative integers via the reciprocal.

Consequences:
- Powers are easy: just raise $r$ to the $n$ and multiply the angle by $n$.
- Roots: the $n$-th roots of $re^{i\theta}$ (with $r > 0$, $n$ a positive integer) are $\sqrt[n]{r}\;\exp\!\left(i\,\frac{\theta + 2\pi k}{n}\right)$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$. Here $\sqrt[n]{r}$ is the ordinary real positive $n$-th root. The roots all have the same modulus $\sqrt[n]{r}$, so they lie on a circle, equally spaced every $\frac{2\pi}{n}$.

This is the key to finding roots of polynomials and understanding roots of unity. Note $k$ runs over exactly $n$ consecutive integers because $k$ and $k+n$ give the same root (their angles differ by $2\pi$), so there are exactly $n$ distinct $n$-th roots.

:::intuition
Raising a complex number to a power is "stretch by r each time and turn by θ each time." After n times you have turned nθ and scaled r^n. The n-th roots are the n numbers that, when turned and stretched n times, land back on the starting number — like asking "which n equally-spaced spokes on a wheel, spun n times, all point back home."
:::

:::example[Compute $(1 + i)^4$ with De Moivre]
Put $1 + i$ in polar form: $r = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}$, and $\theta = \arctan\frac{1}{1} = \frac{\pi}{4}$ (quadrant I). So $1 + i = \sqrt{2}\,\text{cis}\,\frac{\pi}{4}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Apply De Moivre with $n = 4$: $(1+i)^4 = (\sqrt{2})^4\,\text{cis}\!\left(4\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 4\,\text{cis}\,\pi$.
2. Evaluate: $4(\cos\pi + i\sin\pi) = 4(-1 + 0i) = -4$.
3. Sanity check by squaring twice: $(1+i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 2i$, and $(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$ ✓.
:::

:::example[Find the three cube roots of 8 (i.e., solutions to z^3 = 8 = 8 + 0i)]
Write $8 = 8\,\text{cis}\,0$ (here $r = 8$, $\theta = 0$).
:::

:::solution
1. Roots: $\sqrt[3]{8}\,\text{cis}\!\left(\frac{0 + 2\pi k}{3}\right) = 2\,\text{cis}\,\frac{2\pi k}{3}$ for $k = 0, 1, 2$.
2. $k=0$: $2\,\text{cis}\,0 = 2$.
3. $k=1$: $2\,\text{cis}\,\frac{2\pi}{3} = 2\left(-\tfrac{1}{2} + i\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -1 + i\sqrt{3}$.
4. $k=2$: $2\,\text{cis}\,\frac{4\pi}{3} = 2\left(-\tfrac{1}{2} - i\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -1 - i\sqrt{3}$.
5. Sanity check one root: $(-1 + i\sqrt3)^3$. First $(-1 + i\sqrt3)^2 = 1 - 2i\sqrt3 + 3i^2 = 1 - 2i\sqrt3 - 3 = -2 - 2i\sqrt3$. Then $(-2 - 2i\sqrt3)(-1 + i\sqrt3) = 2 - 2i\sqrt3 + 2i\sqrt3 - 2i^2\cdot 3 = 2 + 6 = 8$ ✓. The three roots are equally spaced $\frac{2\pi}{3} = 120°$ apart on the circle of radius $2$.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **De Moivre for fractional powers**: n must be integer for the basic statement; roots require the k=0 to n-1 branches.
- **Forgetting the n distinct roots**: There are always n roots counting multiplicity in complex; the formula gives all.
- **Arg not adding multiples of 2π**: The +2π k /n generates the different branches; k mod n.
- **r negative**: Take |r| and adjust arg by π.
- **Leaving roots in cis form when a+bi wanted**: Convert back using cos sin of the angles.
- **n=0 or negative**: Powers defined for negative integer via inverse; roots for positive integer n.

## Polar coordinates

<!-- section: polar-coordinates -->

Polar coordinates locate points by distance and direction from origin. They simplify many curves with rotational symmetry.

### Polar Coordinates

In the plane, a point can be located by (r, θ) instead of (x,y):

x = r cos θ

y = r sin θ

r² = x² + y²

θ = atan2(y, x)

Polar equations often simplify circles, cardioids, roses, and spirals that have ugly Cartesian forms.

Unlike Cartesian coordinates, polar coordinates are **not unique**: a single point has infinitely many representations. Negative $r$ and $\theta$ outside $[0,2\pi)$ are allowed and converted via the equivalences $(r,\theta) \equiv (-r,\ \theta+\pi) \equiv (r,\ \theta + 2\pi k)$ for any integer $k$. (The origin is special: it is $(0, \theta)$ for *every* $\theta$.)

:::intuition
Polar is "go distance r in direction θ from the origin." Perfect for anything with a center and rotational symmetry (wheels, flowers, radar). A negative r means "face direction θ, then walk backwards."
:::

:::example[Convert the Cartesian point (-3, 4) to polar (r>0, principal θ)]
$r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. The point is in quadrant II ($x<0$, $y>0$). Reference angle is $\arctan\frac{4}{3}$, so the principal angle is $\theta = \pi - \arctan\frac{4}{3}$.
2. Exact answer: $\left(5,\ \pi - \arctan\tfrac{4}{3}\right)$.
3. Sanity check: $\arctan\frac{4}{3} \approx 0.927$, so $\theta \approx 2.214$ rad $\approx 127°$, which is in quadrant II ✓, and $5\cos(2.214) \approx -3$, $5\sin(2.214) \approx 4$ ✓.
:::

:::example[Convert the polar point $(2,\ \tfrac{3\pi}{4})$ to Cartesian $(x, y)$]
$x = r\cos\theta = 2\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = 2\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = -\sqrt{2}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $y = r\sin\theta = 2\sin\frac{3\pi}{4} = 2\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = \sqrt{2}$.
2. So the point is $(-\sqrt{2},\ \sqrt{2})$.
3. Sanity check: $r = \sqrt{(-\sqrt2)^2 + (\sqrt2)^2} = \sqrt{2 + 2} = 2$ ✓, and the point is in quadrant II, matching $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4} = 135°$ ✓.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **r negative in polar plots**: Forgetting that negative r means go opposite direction produces points in the wrong half-plane.
- **Polar to Cartesian conversion sign**: cos( quadrant II ) is negative; students often drop the sign when converting back.
- **θ in degrees vs rad**: atan2 usually radians; convert if problem in degrees.
- **Forgetting r² = x² + y² for conversion**: Always.
- **atan(y/x) instead of atan2**: Loses quadrant info.
- **Assuming r≥0 always**: Negative r valid and useful.

## Parametric equation

<!-- section: parametric-equations -->

Parametric equations describe a curve by letting a parameter t vary, giving x and y as functions of t. They carry direction and timing information and can trace non-functions.

### Parametric Equations

A curve can be described by a pair of functions x = f(t), y = g(t) where t is a parameter (often time or angle).

Advantages:
- Easy to plot direction of travel as t increases.
- Can represent curves that fail the vertical line test (circles, loops).
- Derivatives dx/dt, dy/dt give velocity components.

Eliminating the parameter $t$ sometimes recovers a Cartesian relation, but the parametric form keeps the timing information (where you are *when*, and in which direction you travel).

Classic examples: circle $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$; line $x = t$, $y = mt + b$; cycloid $x = r(t - \sin t)$, $y = r(1 - \cos t)$; helix in 3D, etc.

:::intuition
Parametric equations let the point "walk" according to a clock t. You describe where it is horizontally and vertically at each moment, without needing a single equation relating x and y directly. It is the difference between a photo of a road (the Cartesian curve) and a GPS track that also knows *when* you were at each spot and which way you were headed.
:::

:::example[Parametrize the line segment from (1,2) to (4,8)]
Use the "start plus $t$ times displacement" template with $t$ going from $0$ to $1$. The displacement is $(4-1,\ 8-2) = (3, 6)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $x = 1 + 3t$ (at $t=0$ gives $1$, at $t=1$ gives $4$).
2. $y = 2 + 6t$ (at $t=0$ gives $2$, at $t=1$ gives $8$).
3. Check the endpoints: $t=0 \Rightarrow (1,2)$ ✓ and $t=1 \Rightarrow (4,8)$ ✓. The direction vector $(3,6) = 3(1,2)$, confirming the path stays on the line through the two points.
:::

:::example[Eliminate the parameter from $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$ ($0 \le t < 2\pi$)]
Solve each for the trig value: $\cos t = x$, $\sin t = y$.
:::

:::solution
1. Use the Pythagorean identity $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$: substitute to get $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.
2. So the Cartesian curve is the unit circle. Note what was lost: the Cartesian equation does not record that the point starts at $(1,0)$ and travels counterclockwise once — that direction and timing live only in the parametrization.
3. Sanity check a point: at $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $(x,y) = (0,1)$, and indeed $0^2 + 1^2 = 1$ ✓.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Parametric vs implicit**: A parametric curve may trace only part of the algebraic curve obtained by eliminating t, or may trace it multiple times.
- **Forgetting the range of t**: The interval for t determines which portion of the curve is drawn.
- **Eliminating t incorrectly**: Solve for t from one eq and sub; may introduce extraneous or lose parts.
- **Velocity vs speed**: (dx/dt, dy/dt) is velocity vector; speed is its magnitude.
- **Assuming t must be time**: Can be angle or any parameter.
- **Not checking orientation**: As t increases, may go clockwise or counter.
