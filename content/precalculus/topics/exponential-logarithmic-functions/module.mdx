# Exponential & Logarithmic Functions

Exponential and logarithmic rules, the natural log, logarithmic scales, and growth models.

## Exponential function

<!-- section: exponential-functions -->

Exponential functions grow (or decay) at a rate proportional to their current value. They appear everywhere in nature and finance.

### Exponential Functions

:::definition[Exponential function]{#exponential-definition}
An **exponential function** has the form

$$f(x) = a \cdot b^x, \qquad b > 0,\ b \ne 1,\ a \ne 0.$$

The **base** $b$ (the number being repeatedly multiplied) determines the growth or decay rate; the coefficient $a$ is a vertical stretch or reflection.
:::

The two restrictions on $b$ are not fussiness. We require $b > 0$ so that $b^x$ is real for every real $x$ (e.g. $(-2)^{1/2}$ is not real), and $b \ne 1$ because $1^x = 1$ is just a constant, not a genuine exponential.

:::theorem[Laws of exponents]{#exponent-laws}
For any base $b > 0$ and all real $x$ and $y$,

$$b^{x+y} = b^x \cdot b^y, \qquad (b^x)^y = b^{xy}, \qquad b^0 = 1.$$

Moreover $b^x$ is increasing when $b > 1$ and decreasing when $0 < b < 1$.
:::

The first law is the whole character of the family: adding on the exponent side is multiplying on the value side. The graph always passes through $(0, a)$ (since $b^0 = 1$), has horizontal asymptote $y = 0$, and is defined for all real $x$.

:::intuition
Exponential is "repeated multiplication by the same factor." Start with $a$, multiply by $b$, then by $b$ again, forever. Think of folding a piece of paper: one fold = 2 layers, two folds = 4, ten folds = 1024. The layers don't grow by a fixed amount each fold — they *double*, so small changes in the exponent produce huge changes in the result.
:::

:::example[Solving a simple exponential equation]
Solve $3^{2x-1} = 81$.
:::

:::solution
Rewrite both sides with the **same base**. Since $81 = 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 3^4$, the equation becomes $3^{2x-1} = 3^4$.
Because $b^x$ is one-to-one (for $b > 0,\ b \ne 1$), equal powers of the same base force equal exponents: $2x - 1 = 4$.
Solve the linear equation: $2x = 5$, so $x = \tfrac{5}{2}$.
**Sanity check:** $3^{2(5/2) - 1} = 3^{5 - 1} = 3^4 = 81$. ✓
:::

:::example[Reading off growth from the base]
A colony starts at $200$ cells and triples every hour. Write the model and find the count after $4$ hours.
:::

:::solution
"Triples every hour" means the base is $b = 3$ and the starting value is $a = 200$, so $P(t) = 200 \cdot 3^{\,t}$ with $t$ in hours.
Evaluate at $t = 4$: $P(4) = 200 \cdot 3^4 = 200 \cdot 81 = 16200$ cells.
**Sanity check** by stepping hour-by-hour: $200 \to 600 \to 1800 \to 5400 \to 16200$. ✓ Each step multiplies by $3$, as required.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Base confusion**: Writing b^x as x^b (power vs exponential).
- **Exponent rules with negatives**: (-2)^3 = -8 but (-2)^4 = +16; fractional exponents with even roots of negative bases are not real.
- **Growth vs decay**: For 0 < b < 1 the exponential b^x is decay (decreasing), not growth.
- **Forgetting b^0=1**: Always the y-intercept for a* b^x is a.
- **Domain of exponential**: Always all reals; students sometimes think like logs.
- **Continuous vs discrete**: e^{rt} is continuous limit; (1+r)^t discrete. They are close but not identical.

## Logarithm

<!-- section: logarithms -->

Logarithms are the inverse operation to exponentiation. They turn multiplication into addition and are essential for solving exponential equations and handling scales.

### Logarithms

:::definition[Logarithm]{#logarithm-definition}
The **logarithm** base $b$ of a positive number $a$ is the exponent to which $b$ must be raised to produce $a$:

$$\log_b a = c \iff b^c = a.$$
:::

In words: a logarithm *asks a question* — "what power turns the base into this number?" Logarithms are defined only for a **positive argument** $a > 0$ and a **positive base** $b > 0$ with $b \ne 1$. (There is no real power of a positive base that produces $0$ or a negative number, which is why the argument must be positive.)

:::theorem[Laws of logarithms]{#logarithm-laws}
For a base $b > 0$ with $b \ne 1$ and all $x, y > 0$,

$$\log_b (xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y, \qquad \log_b \frac{x}{y} = \log_b x - \log_b y,$$

$$\log_b (x^y) = y \log_b x, \qquad \log_b b = 1, \qquad \log_b 1 = 0.$$

Products become sums, quotients become differences, and exponents come down in front.
:::

:::proof
Each rule is just an exponent rule read backwards. Write $x = b^m$ and $y = b^n$, so that $m = \log_b x$ and $n = \log_b y$. Because $b^{m}\cdot b^{n} = b^{m+n}$, taking logs turns the product on the left into the sum $m + n$ on the right, which is the first rule; the other two come the same way from $b^m/b^n = b^{m-n}$ and $(b^m)^y = b^{my}$.
:::

:::proposition[Change of base]{#change-of-base}
For $a > 0$ and admissible bases,

$$\log_b a = \frac{\ln a}{\ln b}.$$

Any consistent base works in the numerator and denominator, e.g. $\dfrac{\log_{10} a}{\log_{10} b}$.
:::

:::intuition
A log is the "how many times did I multiply" counter. If you kept multiplying by $2$ until you reached $32$, you multiplied $5$ times: $\log_2 32 = 5$. And because the counters just *add up* when you multiply totals together, a log turns awkward "power towers" back into ordinary numbers you can add and subtract.
:::

:::example[Using log properties to simplify]
Simplify $\log_3 9 + \log_3 27 - \log_3 3$.
:::

:::solution
**Direct approach** — evaluate each term. Since $9 = 3^2$, $\log_3 9 = 2$. Since $27 = 3^3$, $\log_3 27 = 3$. And $\log_3 3 = 1$.
Combine: $2 + 3 - 1 = 4$.
**Check with the log rules** — a sum minus a log is a product over a quotient: $\log_3 9 + \log_3 27 - \log_3 3 = \log_3\!\left(\dfrac{9 \cdot 27}{3}\right) = \log_3\!\left(\dfrac{243}{3}\right) = \log_3 81$.
Since $81 = 3^4$, $\log_3 81 = 4$. ✓ Both methods agree.
:::

:::example[Solving a logarithmic equation]
Solve $\log_2(x - 3) = 4$.
:::

:::solution
Rewrite in exponential form using $\log_b a = c \iff b^c = a$: here $2^4 = x - 3$.
Since $2^4 = 16$, we get $x - 3 = 16$, so $x = 19$.
**Check the domain:** the argument must be positive, and $x - 3 = 16 > 0$. ✓ So $x = 19$ is valid (a solution that made the argument $\le 0$ would have to be discarded).
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Base confusion**: log_b a means "b to what power = a", not "a to what power = b". The base is always on the bottom of the log notation.
- **Domain of logs**: Students forget that log arguments must be positive; domain of log_b is (0, ∞).
- **Change of base arithmetic**: \log_b a = \ln a / \ln b , not \ln a - \ln b or other misapplications.
- **Log of sum**: There is no simple log(x + y) rule; logs turn products/quotients/powers into sums, not sums into anything simpler.
- **Forgetting log_b b =1 and log_b 1=0**: Basic values that simplify many expressions.
- **Negative base or arg**: Logs not defined for negative or zero in reals; b must >0 ≠1.

## Natural logarithm

<!-- section: natural-log -->

The natural log is the logarithm with base e, the special number that makes calculus clean: its derivative is itself, and ln's derivative is 1/x.

### The Natural Logarithm

:::definition[Natural logarithm]{#natural-log-definition}
The **natural logarithm** $\ln x = \log_e x$ uses the famous base $e \approx 2.718281828\ldots$, which can be defined as the limit

$$e = \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^n.$$
:::

:::proposition[$\ln$ and $e^x$ are inverses]
The natural log and the exponential base $e$ undo one another:

$$\ln(e^x) = x \quad \text{for all real } x, \qquad e^{\ln x} = x \quad \text{for } x > 0.$$
:::

In calculus $e$ and $\ln$ are special because $\dfrac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$ (the exponential is its own derivative) and $\dfrac{d}{dx} \ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}$.

Every other logarithm is a constant multiple of the natural log: by change of base, $\log_b x = \dfrac{\ln x}{\ln b}$, so $\log_b x$ is just $\ln x$ scaled by the fixed factor $1/\ln b$.

:::intuition
Money in a bank that pays interest *every instant* grows by exactly $e^{rt}$. The natural log is the tool that runs that continuous growth backwards — it answers "how long did this need to compound to reach that size?" $e$ is the one special growth rate at which the curve's steepness always equals its own height, so $\ln$ and $e^x$ fit together perfectly like a lock and key.
:::

:::example[Solving with natural logs]
Solve $e^{2x} = 7$ for $x$.
:::

:::solution
Take the natural log of both sides: $\ln\!\left(e^{2x}\right) = \ln 7$.
Apply $\ln(e^{u}) = u$ on the left: $2x = \ln 7$.
Divide by $2$: $x = \dfrac{\ln 7}{2}$.
Numerically $\ln 7 \approx 1.94591$, so $x \approx 0.97295$.
**Sanity check:** $e^{2(0.97295)} = e^{1.9459} \approx 7.00$. ✓
:::

:::example[Isolating before taking the log]
Solve $5\,e^{-0.2t} = 2$ for $t$.
:::

:::solution
First **isolate the exponential** by dividing both sides by $5$: $e^{-0.2t} = \dfrac{2}{5} = 0.4$.
Take $\ln$ of both sides: $-0.2t = \ln 0.4$.
Since $\ln 0.4 \approx -0.91629$, we get $-0.2t \approx -0.91629$, so $t \approx \dfrac{-0.91629}{-0.2} \approx 4.581$.
**Sanity check:** $5\,e^{-0.2(4.581)} = 5\,e^{-0.9162} \approx 5(0.40) = 2.00$. ✓ (Note we could not take the log until the exponential was alone — $\ln(5\,e^{-0.2t}) \ne \ln 5 \cdot (-0.2t)$.)
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Base confusion with e**: ln means log_e , not log10 or log2; always natural in advanced math.
- **Domain of ln**: Only positive reals; ln of negative or zero undefined in reals.
- **Forgetting ln(e^x) =x exactly**: Simplifies many expressions.
- **Mixing ln with log in software**: Some languages log is ln, others log10; check docs.
- **Change of base with ln vs log10**: Either works; ln often preferred in calc because of derivative.
- **Approximating e or ln without calc**: e≈2.718, but use exact ln when possible.

## Logarithmic scale

<!-- section: logarithmic-scales -->

Log scales compress huge ranges into manageable visual space by making distance proportional to ratio, not difference.

### Logarithmic Scales

:::definition[Logarithmic scale]{#log-scale-definition}
A **logarithmic scale** spaces numbers so that equal distances correspond to equal *ratios* (multiplicative factors) rather than additive differences.
:::

Classic examples:
- Richter scale for earthquake magnitude (each whole number is 10× energy).
- Decibel scale for sound intensity (10× power per 10 dB).
- pH scale (each unit is 10× change in H+ concentration).
- Astronomical magnitude and stellar brightness.

:::proposition[Exponentials are straight on a semi-log plot]
If $y = a\,b^x$ then

$$\log y = \log a + x\log b,$$

a straight line in the variables $x$ and $\log y$, with slope $\log b$ and intercept $\log a$.
:::

:::proof
Take the log of both sides of $y = a\,b^x$ and use the product and power rules: $\log y = \log a + \log(b^x) = \log a + x \log b$.
:::

This is why semi-log paper is useful for spotting exponential behavior in data: the eye is far better at judging straightness than at judging curvature.

:::intuition
Log scales let your eyes handle numbers that differ by factors of millions without the big numbers squishing the small ones off the page. A jump from 1 to 10 looks the same size as 1,000,000 to 10,000,000.
:::

:::example[Interpreting a decibel reading]
A sound at $60$ dB is how many times more intense than one at $30$ dB? (Assume the power scale where $10$ dB $= 10\times$ intensity.)
:::

:::solution
Because the scale is *logarithmic*, subtract to find the gap in dB: $60 - 30 = 30$ dB.
Convert that gap to factors of $10$: $\dfrac{30\text{ dB}}{10\text{ dB per factor}} = 3$ factors of $10$.
Therefore the intensity ratio is $10^3 = 1000$ times.
**Sanity check** — do *not* divide the readings directly: $60/30 = 2$ is wrong, because dB values are exponents, not the intensities themselves.
:::

:::example[Comparing earthquakes on the Richter scale]
Each whole-number step on the Richter scale means $10\times$ the amplitude. How much larger is a magnitude $7.0$ quake than a magnitude $5.5$ quake in amplitude?
:::

:::solution
Find the difference in magnitude: $7.0 - 5.5 = 1.5$.
Each unit is a factor of $10$, so the amplitude ratio is $10^{1.5}$.
Compute $10^{1.5} = 10^{1} \cdot 10^{0.5} = 10 \cdot \sqrt{10} \approx 10 \cdot 3.162 \approx 31.6$.
So the magnitude $7.0$ quake has roughly $32$ times the amplitude — a "small-looking" $1.5$ gap hides a large multiplicative jump, which is the whole point of a log scale.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Thinking log scale is just 'log of the numbers on axes'**: The spacing between ticks is logarithmic.
- **Confusing additive vs multiplicative**: +1 on Richter is *10 energy, not +1.
- **Forgetting that 0 may not appear or negative not shown**: Many log scales start at small positive.
- **Misreading pH**: pH7 is 10× less acidic than pH6 (lower pH more acidic).
- **Calculator log vs ln in scale calcs**: Use consistent base; the factor is the same.
- **Assuming all 'log plots' are log-log**: Semi-log (one axis log) straightens exponentials; log-log straightens power laws.

## Exponential growth

<!-- section: exponential-growth -->

When a quantity's rate of change is proportional to its size, the result is exponential growth or decay. This leads to the differential equation dP/dt = k P whose solution is P = P0 e^{kt}.

### Exponential Growth and Decay

:::definition[Exponential growth and decay]{#exponential-growth-definition}
**Exponential growth** occurs when a quantity changes at a rate proportional to its current size,

$$\frac{dP}{dt} = k\,P \qquad \text{with solution} \qquad P(t) = P_0\, e^{kt},$$

where $P_0 = P(0)$ is the starting amount and $k > 0$. **Exponential decay** is the same equation with $k < 0$ (the quantity shrinks toward $0$ instead of blowing up).
:::

:::proposition[Discrete and continuous compounding]
Compounding over $t$ years at rate $r$ with $n$ compoundings per year gives

$$A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt},$$

and taking the continuous limit $n \to \infty$ gives

$$A = P\,e^{rt}.$$
:::

:::proposition[Half-life]{#half-life}
For decay ($k < 0$), the time for the quantity to halve is

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|k|}.$$

It depends only on $k$, not on the starting amount $P_0$ — a key feature of exponential decay.
:::

These models appear in finance, biology, physics, and technology adoption curves.

:::intuition
Exponential growth is "the bigger you are, the faster you grow." Each step multiplies by the same factor >1, so the increases themselves get larger and larger — the opposite of linear "add the same amount every time."
:::

:::example[Continuous compounding and doubling time]
A principal of $1000$ dollars is invested at $4\%$ annual interest compounded continuously.
:::

:::solution
Set up the model with $P = 1000$ and $r = 0.04$: $A(t) = 1000\,e^{0.04t}$.
To find the doubling time, set $A = 2000$: $2000 = 1000\,e^{0.04t} \implies 2 = e^{0.04t}$.
Take $\ln$ of both sides: $\ln 2 = 0.04t$, so $t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{0.04} \approx \dfrac{0.693147}{0.04} \approx 17.33$ years.
**Sanity check** with the Rule of 72: $\dfrac{72}{4} = 18$ years — close to $17.33$, as expected for a quick estimate.
:::

:::example[Radioactive decay and half-life]
A $50$ mg sample decays with rate constant $k = -0.10$ per year. Find its half-life and the amount left after $10$ years.
:::

:::solution
Half-life uses only $|k|$: $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{|k|} = \dfrac{0.693147}{0.10} \approx 6.93$ years.
Amount remaining after $10$ years: $P(10) = 50\,e^{(-0.10)(10)} = 50\,e^{-1}$.
Since $e^{-1} \approx 0.36788$, we get $P(10) \approx 50 \cdot 0.36788 \approx 18.39$ mg.
**Sanity check** against the half-life: after one half-life ($\approx 6.93$ yr) about $25$ mg remain; $10$ years is a bit more than one half-life, so a value somewhat below $25$ mg — namely $18.4$ mg — is exactly what we expect. ✓
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Growth vs decay**: For 0 < b < 1 the exponential b^x is decay (decreasing), not growth.
- **Continuous vs discrete**: e^{rt} is the continuous limit; (1+r)^t is discrete annual compounding. They are close but not identical.
- **Half life depends on k not on P0**: Time to halve is independent of starting amount.
- **Rule of 72 is approximation**: 72/r% gives rough doubling years; exact is ln2 *100 / r .
- **Forgetting the e in continuous model**: Many default to (1+r)^t even when problem says continuous.
- **Sign of k**: k>0 growth, k<0 decay; |k| in half life.
