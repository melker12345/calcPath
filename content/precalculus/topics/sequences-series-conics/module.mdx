# Sequences, Series & Conics

Sequences, arithmetic and geometric progressions, the binomial theorem, and conic sections.

## Sequence

<!-- section: sequences -->

A sequence is an ordered list indexed by the natural numbers. It may converge to a limit or diverge.

### Sequences

A **sequence** is a function whose domain is the positive integers (or nonnegative integers), written as an ordered list a₁, a₂, a₃, … .

The $n$th term is denoted $a_n$. Sequences may be defined explicitly (closed form) or recursively ($a_{n+1}$ in terms of previous terms).

Intuitively, a sequence **converges** to a number $L$ if its terms eventually get and stay as close to $L$ as you like. Made precise: a sequence converges to $L$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $n > N$, $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$. Here $\varepsilon$ is the "how close" tolerance you demand, and $N$ is how far out you must go before the tail stays within that tolerance. Otherwise the sequence **diverges**.

:::intuition
A sequence is just a list of numbers indexed by 1,2,3,… like the daily temperature readings forever. Convergence is like a dart that keeps landing closer and closer to the bullseye: no matter how tiny a circle you draw around the target, eventually every dart lands inside it. We care about whether the numbers settle down to a single value as we go further out in the list.
:::

:::example[The sequence defined by $a_n = \dfrac{n}{n+1}$. Does it converge? To what?]
Write the first terms: $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{4}{5}, \dots$ — they are climbing, but each stays below $1$.
:::

:::solution
1. Rewrite in a form where the behavior is obvious: $a_n = \dfrac{n+1-1}{n+1} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{n+1}$.
2. As $n \to \infty$, the leftover $\dfrac{1}{n+1} \to 0$, so $a_n \to 1$.
3. Therefore $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = 1$; the sequence converges to $1$.
4. Sanity check: $a_{99} = \dfrac{99}{100} = 0.99$, already within $0.01$ of $1$, and every later term is even closer. ✓
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Sequence vs series**: A sequence is the list; a series is the sum of the list. Confusing $a_n$ with $S_n$ is common.
- **Convergence requires eventual closeness**: Early terms can be wild; only the tail matters.
- **Bounded does not imply convergent**: (-1)^n is bounded but diverges.
- **Monotonic + bounded implies convergent**: Important theorem; students forget one or the other.
- **Limit of sequence vs limit of function**: Similar but n integer; use same tools often (e.g. 1/n →0).
- **Recursive without base case**: Need initial term(s) to generate the list.

## Arithmetic progression

<!-- section: arithmetic-progressions -->

Constant difference between terms. Simple closed forms for nth term and sum of first n terms.

### Arithmetic Progressions

An **arithmetic progression** (AP) has a constant **common difference** $d$ between consecutive terms. To reach the $n$th term you start at $a_1$ and add $d$ a total of $n-1$ times, giving the closed form

$$a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d.$$

The sum of the first $n$ terms of an AP, written $S_n = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n$, is

$$S_n = \frac{n}{2}\bigl(2a_1 + (n-1)d\bigr) \quad\text{or equivalently}\quad S_n = \frac{n}{2}(\text{first} + \text{last}).$$

Why these are equal: the last term is $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$, so $\text{first} + \text{last} = a_1 + a_1 + (n-1)d = 2a_1 + (n-1)d$. The formula is derived by pairing first$+$last, second$+$second-last, and so on. Each such pair sums to the same value $2a_1 + (n-1)d$, and there are $\tfrac{n}{2}$ pairs, which is exactly the formula.

:::intuition
An arithmetic sequence is "add the same amount every step" — like saving a fixed \$50 per month, so your balance is \$50, \$100, \$150, … The sum formula is the trick Gauss used as a schoolboy: to add $1+2+\dots+100$, fold the list so $1$ pairs with $100$, $2$ with $99$, …, each pair making $101$; fifty pairs give $50 \times 101 = 5050$ without adding one by one.
:::

:::example[Find the sum of the first 15 terms of $4, 9, 14, \dots$]
Identify $a_1 = 4$ and $d = 9 - 4 = 5$ (check: $14 - 9 = 5$ ✓).
:::

:::solution
1. Find the last term: $a_{15} = a_1 + (15-1)d = 4 + 14\cdot 5 = 4 + 70 = 74$.
2. Apply the sum formula: $S_{15} = \dfrac{15}{2}(\text{first}+\text{last}) = \dfrac{15}{2}(4 + 74) = \dfrac{15}{2}\cdot 78 = 15 \cdot 39 = 585$.
3. Sanity check with the other form: $S_{15} = \dfrac{15}{2}\bigl(2\cdot 4 + 14\cdot 5\bigr) = \dfrac{15}{2}(8 + 70) = \dfrac{15}{2}\cdot 78 = 585$. ✓
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Arithmetic vs geometric sum formulas**: Using n/2 (first+last) for a geometric series or a1(1-r^n)/(1-r) for arithmetic produces wrong answers.
- **n starts at 1**: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$; off-by-one common.
- **Sum to n or to infinity**: AP infinite sum diverges unless d=0.
- **Common difference sign**: d can be negative (decreasing AP).
- **Forgetting the /2 in sum**: n/2 * (first+last).
- **Using last term without computing**: last = a1 + (n-1)d ; don't guess.

## Geometric progression

<!-- section: geometric-progressions -->

Constant ratio between terms. Finite and infinite sum formulas (when |r|<1).

### Geometric Progressions

A **geometric progression** (GP) has a constant **common ratio** $r$: each term is the previous one multiplied by $r$, so $a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{n-1}$.

Sum of the first $n$ terms (finite), valid when $r \ne 1$:

$$S_n = a_1\,\frac{r^n - 1}{r - 1} = a_1\,\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}.$$

(The two forms are the same fraction with numerator and denominator both negated.) This comes from a telescoping trick: writing $S_n$ and $rS_n$ and subtracting cancels every middle term, leaving $S_n - rS_n = a_1 - a_1 r^n$, then divide by $1 - r$.

For an **infinite** geometric series, the condition $|r| < 1$ is what makes it converge: then $r^n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, so $S_n \to \dfrac{a_1}{1-r}$. Hence

$$S_\infty = \frac{a_1}{1 - r} \qquad (\text{only when } |r| < 1).$$

When $|r| \ge 1$ the terms do not shrink, the partial sums grow without bound, and the infinite sum diverges (the only exception being the trivial $r = 1$ case, where all terms equal $a_1$ and the finite sums are just $n\,a_1$).

:::intuition
Geometric is "multiply by the same factor every step" — compound interest, half-life decay, or a bouncing ball that keeps half its height each bounce. The infinite sum formula feels like magic: even though you add forever, the total stays finite when $|r|<1$. Think of walking half the remaining distance to a wall, then half of what's left, then half again — you add infinitely many steps but never travel more than the full distance to the wall.
:::

:::example[Infinite sum of $\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \cdots$]
Identify $a_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $r = \dfrac{1/4}{1/2} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Check convergence: $|r| = \tfrac{1}{2} < 1$, so the infinite sum formula applies.
2. Apply it: $S_\infty = \dfrac{a_1}{1 - r} = \dfrac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = \dfrac{1/2}{1/2} = 1$.
3. Sanity check with partial sums: $\tfrac12, \tfrac34, \tfrac78, \tfrac{15}{16}, \dots$ climb toward $1$ but never reach it. You can also read it as the binary fraction $0.1111\ldots_2 = 1$. ✓
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Arithmetic vs geometric sum formulas**: Using n/2 (first+last) for a geometric series or a1(1-r^n)/(1-r) for arithmetic produces wrong answers.
- **Infinite geometric only when |r|<1**: When |r|≥1 the partial sums grow without bound; the formula a1/(1-r) does not apply.
- **r=1 special case**: All terms equal a1; sum = n a1 (finite) or diverges (infinite).
- **Sign of r**: |r|<1 for convergence; negative r alternates but still converges if |r|<1.
- **First term a1 or a0**: Formula uses a1 the first term.
- **Confusing $S_n$ with $a_n$**: $S_n$ is the sum up to $n$; $a_n$ the $n$th term.

## Binomial theorem

<!-- section: binomial-theorem -->

The binomial theorem gives a direct formula for expanding (x+y)^n without multiplying n times, using binomial coefficients.

### Binomial Theorem

The **binomial theorem** expands $(x + y)^n$ for a nonnegative integer $n$ into a sum of $n+1$ terms:

$$(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}\, x^{n-k} y^k,$$

where the **binomial coefficient** $\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ (read "$n$ choose $k$") counts the number of ways to pick $k$ items from $n$. In each term the exponents of $x$ and $y$ always add up to $n$.

Where do the coefficients come from? Writing $(x+y)^n$ as $n$ identical factors $(x+y)(x+y)\cdots(x+y)$, a term with $y^k$ is formed by choosing the $y$ from exactly $k$ of the factors (and $x$ from the other $n-k$); there are $\binom{n}{k}$ such choices, so that is the coefficient. This combinatorial counting is one proof; the other standard proof is induction on $n$. **Pascal's triangle** gives a quick way to read the coefficients without computing factorials — each entry is the sum of the two above it, since $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$.

:::intuition
Instead of multiplying $(x+y)$ by itself $n$ times, the theorem hands you each term's coefficient directly. Picture $n$ coins in a row; each coin you can flip to "$y$" or leave as "$x$". A term like $x^{n-k}y^k$ is "exactly $k$ coins landed on $y$", and the coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$ is just how many ways that can happen.
:::

:::example[Expand $(x + 2)^4$ and identify the $x^2$ term.]
Here $y = 2$ and $n = 4$, so the term for each $k$ is $\dbinom{4}{k}\,x^{4-k}\,2^k$:
:::

:::solution
$k=0:\ \dbinom{4}{0}x^{4}\cdot 2^{0} = 1\cdot x^4 \cdot 1 = x^4$.
$k=1:\ \dbinom{4}{1}x^{3}\cdot 2^{1} = 4 x^3 \cdot 2 = 8x^3$.
$k=2:\ \dbinom{4}{2}x^{2}\cdot 2^{2} = 6 x^2 \cdot 4 = 24x^2$.
$k=3:\ \dbinom{4}{3}x^{1}\cdot 2^{3} = 4 x \cdot 8 = 32x$.
$k=4:\ \dbinom{4}{4}x^{0}\cdot 2^{4} = 1\cdot 16 = 16$.
Full expansion: $x^4 + 8x^3 + 24x^2 + 32x + 16$; the $x^2$ coefficient is $24$.
Sanity check: the coefficients $1,4,6,4,1$ are exactly row $n=4$ of Pascal's triangle. ✓
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Binomial coefficients**: C(n,k)=C(n,n-k); forgetting this or miscalculating factorials for larger n.
- **Off by one in k**: The term with y^k is C(n,k) x^{n-k} y^k ; power on x is n-k.
- **Forgetting the y^k power**: Even if y=1, the coeff is still C(n,k).
- **Negative or fractional n**: The basic theorem is for nonnegative integer n; generalized binomial for others.
- **Not using Pascal for small n**: Quick to build row by row instead of computing large factorials.
- **Sign errors with (x - y)**: Alternating signs from y^k with negative.

## Conic section

<!-- section: conic-sections -->

Conics unify circles, ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas as quadratic curves or cone slices. Standard forms after completing square/rotation.

### Conic Sections

**Conic sections** are the curves obtained by slicing a double cone with a plane at various angles:

- Circle: plane perpendicular to cone axis.
- Ellipse: plane tilted but cuts only one nappe completely.
- Parabola: plane parallel to a generator line.
- Hyperbola: plane cuts both nappes.

Algebraically they are all degree-2 equations: $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$. For a nondegenerate conic, the **discriminant** $B^2 - 4AC$ tells you which type it is: negative $\Rightarrow$ ellipse (or circle), zero $\Rightarrow$ parabola, positive $\Rightarrow$ hyperbola.

Standard forms (after translation to center/vertex $(h,k)$ and rotation to remove any $xy$ term):

- Circle: $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$
- Parabola: $(x-h)^2 = 4p\,(y-k)$, or the horizontal swap $(y-k)^2 = 4p\,(x-h)$
- Ellipse: $\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1$
- Hyperbola: $\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1$, or the swapped form with the $y$ term positive

Each conic also has an equivalent **distance definition** in terms of foci or a focus and a directrix:

- **Parabola**: the set of points equidistant from a fixed **focus** and a fixed line, the **directrix**. Here $p$ is the distance from the vertex to the focus (and also to the directrix).
- **Ellipse**: the set of points whose distances to two **foci** have a constant **sum**. The foci lie on the major axis at distance $c$ from the center, with $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ (using the larger denominator as $a^2$).
- **Hyperbola**: the set of points whose distances to two foci have a constant **difference**. The foci lie on the transverse axis with $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.

:::intuition
All these curves are "conic" because you can get them by slicing an ice-cream cone (a double cone) with one flat cut at different tilts: straight across gives a circle, a gentle tilt an ellipse, a cut parallel to the side a parabola, and a steep cut through both cones a hyperbola. The algebra unifies all four as quadratic equations.
:::

:::example[1:]
**Identify and give the key features of $9x^2 + 4y^2 - 36 = 0$.**
:::

:::solution
Move the constant and divide through by $36$ to get $1$ on the right: $\dfrac{9x^2}{36} + \dfrac{4y^2}{36} = 1$, i.e. $\dfrac{x^2}{4} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$.
Both squared terms are positive with different denominators, so this is an **ellipse** centered at $(0,0)$.
Compare denominators: $9 > 4$, so the larger denominator sits under $y^2$. Thus $a^2 = 9$ (so $a = 3$) belongs to the $y$-direction — the **major axis is vertical** — and $b^2 = 4$ (so $b = 2$) is the horizontal semi-axis.
Vertices (ends of the major axis) at $(0, \pm 3)$; co-vertices at $(\pm 2, 0)$.
Foci on the major (vertical) axis at $(0, \pm c)$ with $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt{5}$.
Sanity check: plug the vertex $(0,3)$ into the original equation: $9(0) + 4(9) - 36 = 36 - 36 = 0$ ✓.
:::

:::example[2:]
**Rewrite $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 3 = 0$ in standard form and identify it.**
:::

:::solution
Group $x$ and $y$ terms: $(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 4y) = 3$.
Complete the square in $x$: half of $-6$ is $-3$, and $(-3)^2 = 9$, so add $9$. Complete the square in $y$: half of $4$ is $2$, and $2^2 = 4$, so add $4$. Add the same amounts to the right side to keep the equation balanced: $(x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 3 + 9 + 4$.
Factor: $(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 16$.
This matches $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$, a **circle** with center $(3, -2)$ and radius $r = \sqrt{16} = 4$.
Sanity check: the point $(7, -2)$ lies $4$ units right of the center, so it should be on the circle. Plug into the original: $7^2 + (-2)^2 - 6(7) + 4(-2) - 3 = 49 + 4 - 42 - 8 - 3 = 0$ ✓.
:::

### Common Mistakes

- **Conic identification from equation**: The sign of B²-4AC tells parabola/ellipse/hyperbola (degenerate cases aside); students often look only at signs of A and C.
- **Conic standard form completion**: For translated conics, students forget to complete the square or divide correctly after moving constants.
- **a>b or b>a in ellipse**: The larger denominator is the semi-major axis; vertices on that axis.
- **For hyperbola the transverse axis**: The one with the positive term (difference for the other).
- **Foci vs center**: c² = |a² - b²| for ellipse/hyperbola; foci offset from center by c along major/transverse.
- **Parabola p**: Distance from vertex to focus/directrix is p; 4p in the equation.
