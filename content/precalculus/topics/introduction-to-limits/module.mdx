# Introduction to Limits

Limit notation and intuition as a bridge from precalculus into calculus.

## Limit (mathematics)

<!-- section: limits-intro -->

The limit is the central idea that makes calculus work: the value the function approaches as input approaches a point (even if the function is not defined or different there).

### Limits (Introduction)

Before any formulas, here is the picture. Imagine walking along the graph of a function toward a particular $x$-value. The **limit** is the height you are heading toward — the value the graph seems to be aiming at — regardless of whether the graph actually *arrives* at that height when you land on the point. There might be a hole there, or the point might be lifted somewhere else; the limit ignores that and reports the trend.

Written formally, we say

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$

if $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ (with $x \ne a$). Read it as: "the limit of $f(x)$, as $x$ approaches $a$, equals $L$." The phrase "$x \ne a$" is essential — the limit is entirely about the neighborhood *around* $a$, never about the single point $a$ itself. This is exactly why a limit can exist at a point where the function is undefined.

**Notation and one-sided limits.** We can approach $a$ from two directions. The **left-hand limit** $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ looks only at inputs slightly *less* than $a$; the **right-hand limit** $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ looks only at inputs slightly *greater* than $a$. The two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists **if and only if** both one-sided limits exist and are equal:

$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L.$$

If the two sides disagree (a jump), the two-sided limit does not exist.

**Infinite limits and limits at infinity** are two different ideas that share the $\infty$ symbol. An *infinite limit* like $\lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{1}{x^2} = \infty$ means the output grows without bound as $x$ nears a finite point. A *limit at infinity* like $\lim_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{1}{x} = 0$ describes the end behavior as $x$ itself runs off to $\pm\infty$. They call for different techniques, so keep them apart.

**Why limits matter.** Once you have limits you can define the rest of calculus rigorously:

- **Continuity** at $a$ means $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$ — the trend, the destination, and the actual value all agree. This requires three things at once: $f(a)$ is defined, the limit exists, and the two are equal.
- **Derivatives** are limits of average rates of change, $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$, giving instantaneous rate.
- **Integrals** are limits of sums of thin rectangles, giving accumulated area.

**How limits are evaluated.** In order of preference:

1. **Direct substitution** — if $f$ is continuous at $a$, just plug in: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.
2. **Factor and cancel** — when substitution gives the indeterminate form $\tfrac{0}{0}$, factor and remove the common factor causing it.
3. **Rationalize** — multiply by a conjugate to clear a $\tfrac{0}{0}$ involving square roots.
4. **Known special limits** — most famously $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ (with $x$ in radians), the engine behind the derivatives of the trig functions.

A form like $\tfrac{0}{0}$ or $\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ is called **indeterminate**: it does *not* mean the limit fails to exist, only that this particular substitution didn't settle the question and more work is needed.

:::intuition
A limit is like reading a car's GPS as it coasts toward a stop sign. The GPS shows where the car is *heading* — the corner — even if the car never quite touches the line, or gets teleported to a different spot at the last instant. You care about the destination the motion points to, not the single frozen snapshot at the sign. A one-sided limit is just checking where the car is heading when it's rolling in from the left versus rolling in from the right; if those two answers disagree, there is no single spot it is aiming for.
:::

**Worked Example:** (factor and cancel)

**Evaluate** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$.

1. Try direct substitution: numerator $= 2^2 - 4 = 0$, denominator $= 2 - 2 = 0$. This gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, an indeterminate form — so substitution alone can't decide it.
2. Factor the numerator using the difference of squares: $x^2 - 4 = (x-2)(x+2)$.
3. For every $x \ne 2$ we may cancel the common factor $(x - 2)$: $\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x + 2$. (We only need $x$ *near* $2$, not equal to $2$, so this cancellation is legal.)
4. Now the limit is of a continuous function, so substitute: $\lim_{x \to 2}(x + 2) = 2 + 2 = 4$.

**Sanity check:** at $x = 1.99$, $\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = \frac{3.9601 - 4}{-0.01} = \frac{-0.0399}{-0.01} = 3.99$, and at $x = 2.01$ it is $\frac{4.0401 - 4}{0.01} = 4.01$ — both hugging $4$ from either side. So $\lim_{x \to 2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2} = 4$, even though $f(2)$ itself is undefined (a hole in the graph).

**Worked Example:** (rationalizing a root)

**Evaluate** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + 9} - 3}{x}$.

1. Substitution gives $\frac{\sqrt{9} - 3}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$, indeterminate.
2. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate $\sqrt{x+9} + 3$ (this equals $1$, so it changes the form but not the value):
$$\frac{\sqrt{x+9} - 3}{x} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x+9} + 3}{\sqrt{x+9} + 3} = \frac{(x + 9) - 9}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9} + 3\right)}.$$
3. The numerator simplifies to $x$, and for $x \ne 0$ we cancel it: $\dfrac{x}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9}+3\right)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}$.
4. This is continuous at $0$, so substitute: $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9} + 3} = \dfrac{1}{6}$.

**Sanity check:** at $x = 0.01$, $\frac{\sqrt{9.01}-3}{0.01} \approx \frac{3.001666 - 3}{0.01} \approx 0.1667 \approx \tfrac{1}{6}$. ✓

**Worked Example:** (a one-sided / jump case)

**Does** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$ **exist for** $f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1 & x < 1 \\ x^2 + 3 & x \ge 1 \end{cases}$?

1. Approach from the left ($x < 1$), where $f(x) = x + 1$: $\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
2. Approach from the right ($x \ge 1$), where $f(x) = x^2 + 3$: $\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 1^2 + 3 = 4$.
3. The one-sided limits disagree ($2 \ne 4$), so there is no single value the function heads toward.

**Conclusion:** $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$ **does not exist** — the graph has a jump of height $4 - 2 = 2$ at $x = 1$. (Note $f(1) = 4$ *is* defined; a value existing at the point does not rescue a two-sided limit when the sides disagree.)

**Worked Example:** (continuity check)

**Is** $g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$ **continuous at** $x = 3$?

1. Check $g(3)$: it is $\tfrac{0}{0}$, so $g$ is **undefined** at $3$. Continuity fails immediately — one of the three requirements (a defined value) is missing.
2. The limit itself still exists: $\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3$ for $x \ne 3$, so $\lim_{x \to 3} g(x) = 6$.
3. Because the limit exists ($6$) but $g(3)$ does not, this is a **removable discontinuity**: defining $g(3) = 6$ would plug the hole and make $g$ continuous there.

**Sanity check:** the trend value $6$ matches $x+3$ at $x=3$, and $g$ is undefined only at the single removable point. ✓

**Worked Example:** (limit at infinity)

**Evaluate** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 1}$.

1. This is a limit *at infinity*, so use the "divide by the highest power" technique rather than substitution. The highest power in the denominator is $x^2$; divide every term by $x^2$:
$$\frac{3x^2 + 2x}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{3 + \frac{2}{x}}{1 - \frac{1}{x^2}}.$$
2. As $x \to \infty$, the shrinking terms $\tfrac{2}{x} \to 0$ and $\tfrac{1}{x^2} \to 0$.
3. So the expression approaches $\dfrac{3 + 0}{1 - 0} = 3$.

**Sanity check:** at $x = 1000$, $\frac{3\,000\,000 + 2000}{1\,000\,000 - 1} \approx \frac{3\,002\,000}{999\,999} \approx 3.002$, closing in on $3$. This also matches the rule of thumb for rational functions with equal top/bottom degrees: the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients, $\tfrac{3}{1} = 3$. ✓

**Worked Example:** (the special trig limit)

**Evaluate** $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 3x}{x}$.

1. Substitution gives $\tfrac{\sin 0}{0} = \tfrac{0}{0}$, indeterminate.
2. We want the argument of $\sin$ to match the denominator so we can use $\lim_{u \to 0}\frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$. Multiply and divide by $3$: $\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x} = 3 \cdot \dfrac{\sin 3x}{3x}$.
3. Let $u = 3x$; as $x \to 0$, $u \to 0$ too, so $\dfrac{\sin 3x}{3x} \to 1$.
4. Therefore $\lim_{x \to 0}\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x} = 3 \cdot 1 = 3$.

**Sanity check** (radians): at $x = 0.001$, $\frac{\sin(0.003)}{0.001} \approx \frac{0.0029999955}{0.001} \approx 3.0000$. ✓

### Common Mistakes

- **Limit vs function value**: The limit can exist even when $f(a)$ is undefined or different (removable discontinuity). Always distinguish $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ from $f(a)$.
- **One-sided limits**: For limits at endpoints or jumps, the left and right limits must agree; otherwise the two-sided limit does not exist — even if $f(a)$ happens to be defined.
- **Indeterminate forms**: $\tfrac{0}{0}$ or $\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ require algebraic manipulation or other techniques (L'Hôpital later); do not conclude "does not exist" immediately.
- **Forgetting to check one-sided at discontinuities**: Jump discontinuities have different left/right limits, so always check both sides there.
- **Plugging in when there is a hole**: If substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, simplify (factor, cancel, or rationalize) first — the raw substitution is not the answer.
- **Limits at infinity vs at a point**: Different techniques — divide by the highest power of $x$ for limits at $\infty$, but substitute or factor for limits at a finite point.
- **Degrees instead of radians in $\frac{\sin x}{x}$**: The special limit $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ only holds when $x$ is measured in radians.
