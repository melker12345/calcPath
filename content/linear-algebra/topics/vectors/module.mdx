# Vectors & Euclidean Space

Vectors are the atomic units of linear algebra. Everything that follows — matrices, linear transformations, eigenvalues, the singular value decomposition — is built on top of them. Before any of that makes sense you must be fluent with what a vector is, how vectors combine, and what the dot product measures.

There are two views of a vector, and a first course keeps switching between them on purpose. Geometrically, a vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ is an arrow with a length and a direction. Algebraically, it is an ordered list of real numbers. The geometric view supplies the pictures and the intuition; the algebraic view supplies the computations and, crucially, keeps working in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{1000}$, where there is no picture to draw. A great deal of linear algebra consists of proving that some geometric statement you believe about arrows in the plane is a consequence of algebra alone, and therefore true in every dimension.

The chapter runs in the order that dependency forces. First vectors themselves and their length. Then the two operations — addition and scalar multiplication — with the eight axioms they satisfy, because those axioms are the definition of a vector space that the later chapters generalise. Then the dot product, which is what turns $\mathbb{R}^n$ from a bare list-space into a space with lengths and angles; the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is the hinge on which that whole section turns. Then projection, which is the single most reused construction in the subject. Then the cross product and the scalar triple product in $\mathbb{R}^3$, which measure area and volume. Finally lines and planes, where all of the above is cashed out as concrete geometry.

## Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$

<!-- section: vectors -->

:::definition[Vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$]{#vector-definition}
Let $n$ be a positive integer. A **vector** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an ordered $n$-tuple of real numbers,

$$\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n \rangle,$$

and $\mathbb{R}^n$ denotes the set of all of them. The numbers $v_i$ are the **components** of $\mathbf{v}$. Two vectors are **equal** exactly when they have the same number of components and every corresponding pair of components is equal.
:::

Equality is component-wise and nothing more: $\langle u_1, u_2\rangle = \langle v_1, v_2\rangle$ if and only if $u_1 = v_1$ *and* $u_2 = v_2$. A vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is never equal to a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$, even one that looks like it with a zero tacked on; they are elements of different sets and no operation in this chapter mixes them. The most common first error in the subject is adding or dotting two vectors of different lengths, and the definition is what forbids it.

The geometric picture in low dimensions is an arrow. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, draw $\langle 3, 4\rangle$ as an arrow whose tail sits at the origin and whose head sits at the point $(3,4)$; in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the same with three coordinates. Crucially, what identifies a vector is its *displacement*, not its starting point. An arrow from $(1,1)$ to $(4,5)$ has the same components — three across, four up — as the arrow from the origin to $(3,4)$, so it is the *same vector*. Arrows that are translates of one another are one vector drawn in different places. This is exactly why vectors model velocities, forces and displacements: those quantities have size and direction but no location.

That said, it is often convenient to pin an arrow to the origin, and then a vector and a point become interchangeable. The **position vector** of a point $P = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ is $\langle p_1, \ldots, p_n \rangle$. Whether $\langle 3,4\rangle$ means "the point $(3,4)$" or "the instruction: move three right and four up" is decided by context, and fluency means holding both at once.

:::notation
Bold lower-case letters $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}$ denote vectors and plain letters $a, b, c, s, t$ denote scalars (real numbers). Components are written with subscripts: $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_n\rangle$. The arrow $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ denotes the vector from the point $P$ to the point $Q$. Angle brackets $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle$ and parentheses are both in common use for vectors; this chapter uses angle brackets for vectors and parentheses for points, but no mathematical distinction rests on the choice.
:::

:::definition[Zero vector and negative]{#zero-vector}
The **zero vector** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathbf{0} = \langle 0, 0, \ldots, 0\rangle$. The **negative** of $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_n\rangle$ is $-\mathbf{v} = \langle -v_1, \ldots, -v_n\rangle$.
:::

The zero vector is a genuine vector, not a missing one, and it is the only vector with no direction: every other vector points somewhere. It will turn out to be the additive identity, and a surprising amount of linear algebra is the study of which vectors a given process sends to $\mathbf{0}$.

Two points determine a vector, and the recipe is *head minus tail*.

:::proposition[Displacement between points]{#displacement}
If $P = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ and $Q = (q_1, \ldots, q_n)$, then

$$\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle q_1 - p_1,\; q_2 - p_2,\; \ldots,\; q_n - p_n\rangle.$$
:::

:::proof
Travelling from $P$ to $Q$ changes the $i$-th coordinate from $p_i$ to $q_i$, a change of $q_i - p_i$. The displacement whose components are those changes, applied at $P$, lands exactly at $Q$, and by definition $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ is that displacement.
:::

Subtract in that order and not the other: $\overrightarrow{QP} = -\overrightarrow{PQ}$, and a sign error here propagates into every projection and distance computed afterwards.

Now the length. In the plane, the arrow $\langle v_1, v_2\rangle$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $|v_1|$ and $|v_2|$, so Pythagoras gives its length as $\sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}$. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ a second application of Pythagoras gives $\sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + v_3^2}$. Beyond $n = 3$ there is no triangle to appeal to, so the same formula is promoted from a theorem to a *definition*.

:::definition[Norm]{#norm-definition}
The **norm** (length, magnitude) of $\mathbf{v} = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_n\rangle \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is

$$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}.$$

A vector with $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 1$ is a **unit vector**.
:::

Two facts are immediate from the formula and worth naming, because everything later leans on them. First, $\|\mathbf{v}\| \ge 0$ always, since it is a square root of a sum of squares; and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 0$ forces every $v_i^2 = 0$, hence $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. So the only vector of length zero is the zero vector. Second, squaring removes the root:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2,$$

and in practice you should work with $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ for as long as possible and take the square root only at the end. Almost every proof in this chapter is an identity about squared norms.

This is the right moment to say what geometry in $\mathbb{R}^n$ means for large $n$, because students reasonably suspect a swindle. There is no picture of $\mathbb{R}^7$. What there is, is a *definition* of length that agrees with the visual one when $n \le 3$, and a body of theorems — Cauchy–Schwarz, the triangle inequality, the Pythagorean theorem, the projection formula — proved from that definition by algebra that never mentions $n$ except as an index of summation. So when we later speak of the angle between two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^7$, we are not claiming to see it. We are saying: the number $\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}$ lies in $[-1,1]$ (that is Cauchy–Schwarz, and it needs proof), so it is the cosine of exactly one angle in $[0,\pi]$, and that angle obeys every law that angles obey in the plane. Any two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^7$ span at most a $2$-dimensional plane inside it, and inside that plane the picture is the ordinary one. That is the whole content of "high-dimensional geometry", and it is why the algebraic definitions are the ones to trust.

:::intuition
A vector is a set of walking directions: "go $3$ blocks east and $4$ blocks north." The directions do not say where you start — only what steps to take — which is why the same vector can be drawn anywhere on the map. The norm is the straight-line distance from where you started to where you ended up: $5$ blocks by Pythagoras, even though you walked $7$ blocks of pavement to get there.

In $\mathbb{R}^{100}$ the same sentence still makes sense if you imagine $100$ dials instead of a map: a vector is a list of $100$ adjustments, and its norm is the total size of the adjustment, measured so that one big change and a spread of small ones can be compared on the same scale.
:::

:::example[Displacement and distance]
Let $P = (1, 2, 3)$ and $Q = (4, 6, 3)$. Find $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ and the distance from $P$ to $Q$.
:::

:::solution
Head minus tail, component by component:

$$\overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 4-1,\; 6-2,\; 3-3\rangle = \langle 3, 4, 0\rangle.$$

Its norm is

$$\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5,$$

and the norm of a displacement vector is exactly the straight-line distance between its endpoints, so $P$ and $Q$ are $5$ apart.

Sanity check: the third coordinates of $P$ and $Q$ agree, so the two points are at the same height and the third component of the displacement had to come out $0$ — it did. The answer $5$ is positive, as a distance must be.
:::

:::example[A norm in $\mathbb{R}^4$]
Find $\|\mathbf{v}\|$ for $\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, -1, 2, 4\rangle$, and find the two scalars $c$ for which $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = 15$.
:::

:::solution
Squares first, and note that the minus sign disappears when squared:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 4 + 1 + 4 + 16 = 25, \qquad \|\mathbf{v}\| = 5.$$

Scaling multiplies every component by $c$, so it multiplies $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ by $c^2$ and the norm by $|c|$ — this is proved as a theorem in the next section. Hence $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = 5|c| = 15$ gives $|c| = 3$, so $c = 3$ or $c = -3$.

Sanity check: there must be two answers, because $\mathbf{v}$ and $-\mathbf{v}$ have equal length; a single answer would have signalled a dropped absolute value.
:::

:::example[Equidistant points]
Find all values of $t$ for which the point $A = (t, 1, 0)$ is at distance $3$ from $B = (1, -1, 2)$.
:::

:::solution
The displacement is $\overrightarrow{BA} = \langle t - 1,\; 2,\; -2\rangle$, so

$$\|\overrightarrow{BA}\|^2 = (t-1)^2 + 4 + 4 = (t-1)^2 + 8.$$

Setting this equal to $3^2 = 9$ gives $(t-1)^2 = 1$, so $t - 1 = \pm 1$ and $t \in \{0, 2\}$.

Sanity check: squaring the *distance* rather than solving with a square root in the way is what made this a one-line quadratic. Both answers are real because $3 > \sqrt{8}$: the sphere of radius $3$ about $B$ does reach the line $y = 1, z = 0$, and it must cut it twice.
:::

:::pitfall
$\|\mathbf{v}\|$ is not $|v_1| + |v_2| + \cdots$. The norm squares, sums and then takes a root, in that order. For $\langle 3,4\rangle$ the norm is $5$, not $7$. The "sum of absolute values" is a perfectly good measurement of size — it is the distance a taxi drives on a grid — but it is not the one that matches Pythagoras, and it is not the one the dot product induces.
:::

## Vector addition, scalar multiplication and span

<!-- section: operations -->

:::definition[Vector addition and scalar multiplication]{#vector-operations}
For $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$, both operations act component-wise:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \langle u_1 + v_1,\; \ldots,\; u_n + v_n\rangle, \qquad c\mathbf{v} = \langle cv_1,\; \ldots,\; cv_n\rangle.$$

Subtraction is defined by $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{v})$.
:::

Geometrically, addition is the **triangle rule**: slide the tail of $\mathbf{v}$ to the head of $\mathbf{u}$; the sum is the arrow from the tail of $\mathbf{u}$ to the head of $\mathbf{v}$. Equivalently it is the **parallelogram rule**: draw $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ from a common tail, complete the parallelogram, and $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is the diagonal from that shared corner. The two descriptions give the same arrow because the opposite sides of a parallelogram are translates of each other, and translates are the same vector. The other diagonal is $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$, drawn from the head of $\mathbf{v}$ to the head of $\mathbf{u}$ — which is just the displacement formula again, since $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{VU}$ when $U$ and $V$ are the heads.

Scalar multiplication stretches. If $c > 1$ the arrow gets longer; if $0 < c < 1$ it shrinks; if $c < 0$ it reverses and then scales by $|c|$; if $c = 0$ it collapses to $\mathbf{0}$. In every case the arrow stays on the same line through the origin, which is what "same or opposite direction" means precisely.

:::theorem[Homogeneity of the norm]{#norm-homogeneity}
For every $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and every scalar $c$,

$$\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|.$$
:::

:::proof
Compute directly:

$$\|c\mathbf{v}\|^2 = (cv_1)^2 + \cdots + (cv_n)^2 = c^2\left(v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2\right) = c^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$

Taking non-negative square roots of both sides gives $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{c^2}\,\|\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$, using $\sqrt{c^2} = |c|$ and not $c$.
:::

The absolute value is the whole content of the theorem. Lengths cannot be negative, so doubling-and-reversing a vector of length $4$ produces a vector of length $8$, not $-8$.

Now the axioms. Everything algebraic that you will ever do with vectors in this course follows from eight properties, and they are worth verifying once, concretely, in $\mathbb{R}^n$ — because the later chapter on vector spaces takes exactly this list and *declares* it the definition of the subject. The point of that abstraction is that polynomials, matrices, sequences and functions satisfy the same eight rules, so every theorem proved from them applies to all of those at once. Here we check that $\mathbb{R}^n$ really is the model case.

:::theorem[The eight vector space axioms for $\mathbb{R}^n$]{#vector-axioms}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and all scalars $c, d \in \mathbb{R}$:

1. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$ (addition is commutative);
2. $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$ (addition is associative);
3. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{u}$ (additive identity);
4. $\mathbf{u} + (-\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$ (additive inverse);
5. $c(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = c\mathbf{u} + c\mathbf{v}$ (distributive over vector addition);
6. $(c + d)\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{u} + d\mathbf{u}$ (distributive over scalar addition);
7. $c(d\mathbf{u}) = (cd)\mathbf{u}$ (compatibility of scalar multiplication);
8. $1\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}$ (unit scalar acts trivially).
:::

:::proof
Every operation is defined component-wise, so each axiom reduces to the corresponding law for real numbers applied in each of the $n$ slots. Writing the $i$-th component of each side:

1. $u_i + v_i = v_i + u_i$ — commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{R}$.
2. $(u_i + v_i) + w_i = u_i + (v_i + w_i)$ — associativity in $\mathbb{R}$.
3. $u_i + 0 = u_i$ — $0$ is the additive identity in $\mathbb{R}$.
4. $u_i + (-u_i) = 0$ — additive inverses in $\mathbb{R}$; the resulting vector has every component $0$, hence equals $\mathbf{0}$.
5. $c(u_i + v_i) = cu_i + cv_i$ — distributivity in $\mathbb{R}$.
6. $(c + d)u_i = cu_i + du_i$ — distributivity in $\mathbb{R}$ again, with the sum in the scalar slot.
7. $c(du_i) = (cd)u_i$ — associativity of multiplication in $\mathbb{R}$.
8. $1 \cdot u_i = u_i$ — $1$ is the multiplicative identity in $\mathbb{R}$.

Since two vectors are equal precisely when all $n$ components agree, each line establishes the corresponding vector identity.
:::

The proof is short, but do not mistake shortness for emptiness. Two things are being asserted. The first is that the axioms hold — that is the routine part. The second is that they hold *for the reason given*: because the operations are defined slot by slot and $\mathbb{R}$ is a field. Change either fact and the list can fail. If you defined $\mathbf{u} \ast \mathbf{v} = \langle u_1 v_1, \ldots, u_n v_n \rangle$ as your "addition", axiom 4 breaks immediately: $\langle 1, 0\rangle$ has no inverse. If you took your scalars from $\mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{R}$ you would keep all eight axioms but lose the ability to normalise a vector, which is why the scalars must form a field.

Three familiar consequences are *not* on the list, and it is a good exercise in axiomatics to see that they do not need to be: $0\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, $(-1)\mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u}$, and $c\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.

:::proposition[Consequences of the axioms]{#axiom-consequences}
In $\mathbb{R}^n$, for every vector $\mathbf{u}$ and scalar $c$: $\;0\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, $\;c\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, and $(-1)\mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u}$.
:::

:::proof
By axiom 6, $0\mathbf{u} = (0 + 0)\mathbf{u} = 0\mathbf{u} + 0\mathbf{u}$. Adding $-(0\mathbf{u})$ to both sides and using axioms 2, 4 and 3 gives $\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{u}$. The same argument with axiom 5 and $\mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $c\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$. Finally, by axioms 8, 6 and the first result,

$$\mathbf{u} + (-1)\mathbf{u} = 1\mathbf{u} + (-1)\mathbf{u} = (1 + (-1))\mathbf{u} = 0\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0},$$

so $(-1)\mathbf{u}$ is an additive inverse of $\mathbf{u}$; inverses are unique (if $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are both inverses of $\mathbf{u}$ then $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{a} + (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{u}) + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$), so $(-1)\mathbf{u} = -\mathbf{u}$.
:::

Notice that this proof never looked at a component. That is deliberate: it is the argument that will still work in the abstract chapter, where there are no components to look at.

Any nonzero vector can be rescaled to length $1$.

:::definition[Normalisation]{#normalisation}
For $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$, the **unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$** is

$$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\,\mathbf{v}.$$
:::

It really is a unit vector: by homogeneity, $\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\| = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}\|\mathbf{v}\| = 1$, where the scalar $1/\|\mathbf{v}\|$ is positive so its absolute value is itself. The hypothesis $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ is not decoration: the zero vector has norm $0$, the formula divides by it, and — more to the point — $\mathbf{0}$ has no direction to normalise.

:::definition[Standard basis vectors]{#standard-basis}
In $\mathbb{R}^n$, let $\mathbf{e}_i$ be the vector with a $1$ in slot $i$ and $0$ everywhere else. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ these are also written

$$\mathbf{i} = \langle 1,0,0\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{j} = \langle 0,1,0\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{k} = \langle 0,0,1\rangle.$$
:::

Every vector decomposes along them: $\langle a, b, c\rangle = a\mathbf{i} + b\mathbf{j} + c\mathbf{k}$, and in general $\mathbf{v} = v_1\mathbf{e}_1 + \cdots + v_n\mathbf{e}_n$. That expression is the prototype of the most important construction in the subject.

:::definition[Linear combination and span]{#span-definition}
A **linear combination** of $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is any vector

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k, \qquad c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Their **span**, written $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$, is the set of all such combinations.
:::

Span is the answer to "where can I get to?" and its geometry in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is worth memorising, because it is the picture behind solution sets of linear systems in the next chapter.

The span of the empty collection, or of $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$: a single point, the origin. The span of one nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is $\{t\mathbf{v} : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$, the **line through the origin** in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$. The span of two nonzero, non-parallel vectors is a **plane through the origin**: the parallelogram rule lets you reach every point of it, and no more, since every combination $a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{v}$ lies in the flat sheet they determine. If the two vectors *are* parallel, the second adds nothing and the span collapses back to a line. The span of three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ when they do not lie in a common plane, and is a plane or a line or the origin when they do. Notice that every span contains $\mathbf{0}$ — take all $c_i = 0$ — so spans are flat objects through the origin, never shifted ones. A line that misses the origin is not a span, and that distinction is the whole difference between a subspace and an affine set.

:::definition[Linear independence]{#linear-independence}
A list $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ is **linearly independent** when the only scalars satisfying

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$$

are $c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_k = 0$. Otherwise the list is **linearly dependent**.
:::

:::proposition[Dependence means redundancy]{#dependence-redundancy}
A list $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ with $k \ge 2$ is linearly dependent if and only if at least one $\mathbf{v}_j$ is a linear combination of the others. In that case removing $\mathbf{v}_j$ does not change the span.
:::

:::proof
($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}$ with some $c_j \neq 0$. Solve for that term:

$$\mathbf{v}_j = -\frac{1}{c_j}\sum_{i \neq j} c_i \mathbf{v}_i,$$

which is division by a nonzero scalar and so is legitimate. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\mathbf{v}_j = \sum_{i \neq j} a_i \mathbf{v}_i$, move everything to one side to get $\sum_{i\neq j} a_i\mathbf{v}_i + (-1)\mathbf{v}_j = \mathbf{0}$, a combination whose $j$-th coefficient is $-1 \neq 0$, hence nontrivial.

For the last claim, any combination using $\mathbf{v}_j$ can have that term rewritten via the displayed formula, producing a combination of the remaining vectors only; so the two spans contain each other.
:::

Two special cases are used constantly. Any list containing $\mathbf{0}$ is dependent, since $1\cdot\mathbf{0}$ together with zero coefficients on everything else is a nontrivial combination giving $\mathbf{0}$. And two vectors are dependent exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other — that is, when they are parallel.

:::intuition
Adding vectors is following two sets of directions back to back: "$3$ east, $4$ north" then "$1$ east, $2$ south" leaves you at "$4$ east, $2$ north." Scalar multiplication is changing gear: multiply by $2$ and you cover twice the ground the same way; multiply by $-1$ and you retrace your steps.

Independence is a team with no passengers. If three people each bring a genuinely new move, no one of them can be imitated by combining the other two. A dependent list has a passenger — one vector you could rebuild from the rest — so dropping it costs you nothing, which is exactly what the proposition above says about the span.
:::

:::example[Arithmetic and normalisation]
Let $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, -2, 3\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 1, -1\rangle$. Compute $2\mathbf{u} - 3\mathbf{v}$, and find the unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$.
:::

:::solution
Scale first, then add component-wise:

$$2\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, -4, 6\rangle, \qquad 3\mathbf{v} = \langle 12, 3, -3\rangle,$$

$$2\mathbf{u} - 3\mathbf{v} = \langle 2 - 12,\; -4 - 3,\; 6 + 3\rangle = \langle -10, -7, 9\rangle.$$

For the unit vector, $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 16 + 1 + 1 = 18$, so $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 3\sqrt{2}$ and

$$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}\langle 4, 1, -1\rangle = \left\langle \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3},\; \frac{\sqrt{2}}{6},\; -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{6}\right\rangle.$$

Sanity check: $\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\| = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}}\cdot 3\sqrt{2} = 1$, and the signs of $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ match those of $\mathbf{v}$ because the scalar $1/(3\sqrt{2})$ is positive.
:::

:::example[Membership in a span]
Is $\mathbf{w} = \langle 3, 7, 1\rangle$ in $\operatorname{span}\{\langle 1,2,0\rangle, \langle 0,1,1\rangle\}$?
:::

:::solution
Ask whether scalars $a, b$ exist with $a\langle 1,2,0\rangle + b\langle 0,1,1\rangle = \langle 3,7,1\rangle$. Componentwise this is the system

$$a = 3, \qquad 2a + b = 7, \qquad b = 1.$$

The first equation forces $a = 3$ and the third forces $b = 1$; those are not choices, so the second equation is a *consistency check* rather than another equation to solve. It reads $2(3) + 1 = 7$, which is true.

So $\mathbf{w} = 3\langle 1,2,0\rangle + \langle 0,1,1\rangle$ and the answer is yes. Sanity check: recompute the combination, $\langle 3,6,0\rangle + \langle 0,1,1\rangle = \langle 3,7,1\rangle$. Had the middle equation failed, the honest conclusion would have been that no such $a,b$ exist and $\mathbf{w}$ lies off the plane spanned by the two vectors.
:::

:::example[Testing a list for dependence]
Are $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1, -1, 2\rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 0, 1, 3\rangle$ and $\mathbf{w} = \langle 2, -1, 7\rangle$ independent?
:::

:::solution
By Proposition (Dependence means redundancy) it is enough to ask whether one is a combination of the others, and $\mathbf{w}$ is the natural candidate. Solve $\mathbf{w} = a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{v}$.

The first component gives $2 = a$, since $\mathbf{v}$ contributes nothing there. The third gives $7 = 2a + 3b = 4 + 3b$, so $b = 1$. Check the second component with $a = 2$, $b = 1$: $2(-1) + 1(1) = -1$, and the second component of $\mathbf{w}$ is indeed $-1$.

So $\mathbf{w} = 2\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$, equivalently $2\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ — a nontrivial combination equal to zero. The list is **dependent**, and $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\} = \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\}$, a plane through the origin rather than all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
:::

:::example[A parameter that creates dependence]
For which $k$ is the list $\langle 1, 2, 3\rangle$, $\langle 2, 4, k\rangle$ dependent?
:::

:::solution
Two vectors are dependent exactly when one is a scalar multiple of the other. Comparing first components, the only possible multiplier is $2$; the second components agree with it ($2\cdot 2 = 4$); so dependence requires $k = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.

For $k = 6$ the list is $\langle 1,2,3\rangle, \langle 2,4,6\rangle = 2\langle 1,2,3\rangle$: dependent, spanning a line. For every other $k$ the two vectors are independent and span a plane. Sanity check: at $k = 6$ the combination $2\langle 1,2,3\rangle - \langle 2,4,6\rangle = \mathbf{0}$ is nontrivial, as required.
:::

:::pitfall
"Linearly dependent" does not mean "all parallel". The list $\langle 1,0\rangle, \langle 0,1\rangle, \langle 1,1\rangle$ is dependent even though no two of its members are parallel — the dependence involves all three at once. Whenever $k > n$, a list of $k$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is automatically dependent, a fact proved in the chapter on dimension.
:::

## The dot product, length and angle

<!-- section: dot -->

Addition and scalar multiplication cannot express a single geometric fact: nothing in the eight axioms mentions length or angle. One extra operation supplies both.

:::definition[Dot product]{#dot-product}
The **dot product** of $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the *scalar*

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_iv_i.$$
:::

The output is a number, not a vector. That is worth saying out loud because the cross product later in this chapter takes two vectors and returns a vector, and confusing the two is the single most common slip in the subject. An expression such as $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})\cdot\mathbf{w}$ is meaningless: its left factor is a scalar and scalars have no dot product.

:::theorem[Algebraic properties of the dot product]{#dot-properties}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and all scalars $c$:

1. $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}$ (symmetry);
2. $\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}$ (distributivity);
3. $(c\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot(c\mathbf{v})$ (homogeneity);
4. $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (positive definiteness).
:::

:::proof
All four are sums of scalar identities.

1. $\sum u_iv_i = \sum v_iu_i$, because multiplication in $\mathbb{R}$ is commutative.
2. $\sum u_i(v_i + w_i) = \sum (u_iv_i + u_iw_i) = \sum u_iv_i + \sum u_iw_i$, by distributivity in $\mathbb{R}$ and the fact that a finite sum splits.
3. $\sum (cu_i)v_i = c\sum u_iv_i = \sum u_i(cv_i)$, pulling the constant out of the sum.
4. $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \sum v_i^2$, which is $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ by the definition of the norm. A sum of squares of real numbers is non-negative and vanishes only when every term vanishes, that is, only when every $v_i = 0$.
:::

Properties 1–3 say the dot product is *bilinear and symmetric*: you may expand brackets in dot products exactly as in ordinary algebra, provided you never try to dot a scalar. Property 4 is the bridge to geometry, since it lets every norm be rewritten as a dot product and back:

$$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}.$$

Combining bilinearity with property 4 gives the expansion that the rest of this section runs on. For any $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$,

$$\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = (\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})\cdot(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u} + 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2,$$

and replacing $\mathbf{v}$ by $-\mathbf{v}$,

$$\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$

These are the vector analogues of $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, and the cross-term $2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is where all the geometry hides.

Now the central inequality of the subject.

:::theorem[Cauchy–Schwarz inequality]{#cauchy-schwarz}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \le \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|,$$

with equality if and only if one of the vectors is a scalar multiple of the other.
:::

:::proof
If $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ both sides are $0$ and $\mathbf{0} = 0\mathbf{u}$, so the statement holds; assume $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

For every real $t$, positive definiteness gives $\|\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0$. Expand using bilinearity:

$$q(t) = \|\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 t^2 + 2(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})\,t + \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 \; \ge \; 0 \quad \text{for all } t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Because $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ the leading coefficient $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ is strictly positive, so $q$ is a genuine upward parabola. A parabola that is never negative has at most one real root, so its discriminant is at most zero:

$$4(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 - 4\|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \le 0,$$

that is, $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 \le \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. Taking non-negative square roots gives the inequality.

Equality holds exactly when the discriminant is zero, which happens exactly when $q$ has a (double) real root $t_0$. But $q(t_0) = \|\mathbf{u} + t_0\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 0$ forces $\mathbf{u} + t_0\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, again by positive definiteness, so $\mathbf{u} = -t_0\mathbf{v}$ is a multiple of $\mathbf{v}$. Conversely if $\mathbf{u} = c\mathbf{v}$ then both sides equal $|c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$.
:::

This proof is worth studying rather than memorising, for two reasons. It never mentions angles, so it cannot be accused of assuming the geometry it is about to justify; and it uses nothing about $\mathbb{R}^n$ beyond the four properties of the dot product, so the identical argument proves Cauchy–Schwarz for any inner product space — for integrals of products of functions, for covariances of random variables. In statistics this inequality is why a correlation coefficient never exceeds $1$ in absolute value.

:::corollary[Triangle inequality]{#triangle-inequality}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \le \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|.$$
:::

:::proof
Start from the expansion of $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2$ and bound the cross term using $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} \le |\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \le \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$:

$$\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \left(\|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|\right)^2.$$

Both $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|$ and $\|\mathbf{u}\|+\|\mathbf{v}\|$ are non-negative, and $x \mapsto \sqrt{x}$ is increasing, so taking square roots preserves the inequality.
:::

Geometrically this says the direct route is never longer than a detour: the third side of a triangle is at most the sum of the other two. Equality requires $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$, which by the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz means the vectors are parallel *and pointing the same way* — the "triangle" is flat.

With homogeneity and the triangle inequality in hand, the norm satisfies the three properties that define a norm on any vector space.

:::theorem[The norm axioms]{#norm-axioms}
The function $\mathbf{v} \mapsto \|\mathbf{v}\|$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies, for all $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$ and all scalars $c$:

1. $\|\mathbf{v}\| \ge 0$, and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 0$ if and only if $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ (positivity);
2. $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$ (absolute homogeneity);
3. $\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\| \le \|\mathbf{u}\| + \|\mathbf{v}\|$ (triangle inequality).
:::

:::proof
Property 1 is property 4 of the dot product after a square root; property 2 is the homogeneity theorem of the previous section; property 3 is the corollary just proved.
:::

Any function on a vector space obeying those three rules is called a norm, and the one above — the **Euclidean norm** — is only the most familiar. The point of isolating the axioms is that every consequence of them, in particular every statement about distances, holds for all of them at once.

:::definition[Distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$]{#distance-definition}
The **distance** between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is

$$d(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{(u_1-v_1)^2 + \cdots + (u_n-v_n)^2}.$$
:::

:::theorem[The metric axioms]{#metric-axioms}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

1. $d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}$;
2. $d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})$ (symmetry);
3. $d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}) \le d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) + d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})$ (triangle inequality).
:::

:::proof
1 follows from norm positivity applied to $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}$, which is $\mathbf{0}$ exactly when $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}$. For 2, $\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u} = (-1)(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v})$, so absolute homogeneity with $c = -1$ gives equal norms. For 3, write $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}) + (\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})$ and apply the norm triangle inequality to that sum.
:::

The third axiom is the one with content, and it is the reason "shortest path" means anything in $\mathbb{R}^n$: inserting a waypoint $\mathbf{v}$ can never shorten a journey.

Cauchy–Schwarz now lets us define angles, which is the payoff.

:::definition[Angle between vectors]{#angle-definition}
For nonzero $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the **angle** between them is the unique $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ with

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}.$$
:::

This definition is legitimate precisely because Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees the quotient lies in $[-1, 1]$, and $\cos$ is a bijection from $[0,\pi]$ onto $[-1,1]$. Without the inequality the right-hand side might not be a cosine of anything, and the definition would be empty. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ it agrees with the angle you would measure with a protractor:

:::theorem[Geometric form of the dot product]{#dot-geometric}
If $\theta$ is the angle between nonzero $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, then

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta.$$

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$, $\theta$ is the ordinary geometric angle at the common tail of the two arrows.
:::

:::proof
The displayed identity is the definition of $\theta$ rearranged. For the second claim, place $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ with a common tail and let $\varphi$ be the geometric angle between them. The three arrows $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$ form a triangle with sides $\|\mathbf{u}\|, \|\mathbf{v}\|, \|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|$ and with $\varphi$ opposite the last side, so the law of cosines gives

$$\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\varphi.$$

The algebraic expansion computed earlier gives $\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$. Comparing the two and cancelling, $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\varphi$. Since $\varphi \in [0,\pi]$ and $\cos$ is injective there, $\varphi = \theta$.
:::

Read off the sign rule at once. Because $\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\| > 0$, the sign of $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is the sign of $\cos\theta$: positive means $\theta < 90^\circ$ (the vectors broadly agree), negative means $\theta > 90^\circ$ (they broadly disagree), and zero means $\theta = 90^\circ$ exactly.

:::definition[Orthogonality]{#orthogonality}
Vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are **orthogonal** when $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$. A set of vectors is orthogonal when every pair in it is.
:::

The definition is stated with the dot product rather than with the angle so that it also covers $\mathbf{0}$: the zero vector is orthogonal to everything, even though it makes no angle with anything. That convention is not a dodge — it is what makes statements like "the orthogonal complement is a subspace" true without exceptions.

:::theorem[Pythagorean theorem in $\mathbb{R}^n$]{#pythagoras}
Vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are orthogonal if and only if

$$\|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$
:::

:::proof
The expansion $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ holds always. Subtracting $\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ from both sides, the stated equation is equivalent to $2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$, i.e. to $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$.
:::

Note that this is an "if and only if", which the school version is not: in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Pythagorean identity *characterises* perpendicularity. The same expansion yields another identity worth knowing.

:::proposition[Parallelogram law]{#parallelogram-law}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

$$\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 2\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$
:::

:::proof
Add the two expansions: the cross terms $+2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ and $-2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ cancel, leaving $2\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$.
:::

In words: the sum of the squares of a parallelogram's two diagonals equals the sum of the squares of its four sides. Remarkably, this identity *characterises* norms that come from a dot product; a norm failing it cannot be written as $\sqrt{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}$ for any inner product.

:::intuition
The dot product measures agreement of direction, scaled by both lengths. Point two torches the same way and the beams reinforce: the dot product is as large as the lengths allow. Turn one through a right angle and the beams share nothing: zero. Turn it all the way round and they cancel: maximally negative.

Cauchy–Schwarz is the quantitative version of "agreement cannot exceed total size". With $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 3$ and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 5$, the dot product must lie in $[-15, 15]$, and it hits $15$ only in the one case where the vectors point exactly the same way.
:::

:::example[Angle between two vectors]
Find the angle between $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4,-1,2\rangle$.
:::

:::solution
Dot product first:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (1)(4) + (2)(-1) + (3)(2) = 4 - 2 + 6 = 8.$$

Norms: $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{1+4+9} = \sqrt{14}$ and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{16+1+4} = \sqrt{21}$. Then

$$\cos\theta = \frac{8}{\sqrt{14}\sqrt{21}} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{294}} \approx 0.4665,$$

using $\sqrt{14}\sqrt{21} = \sqrt{294}$, and $\theta = \arccos(0.4665) \approx 62.2^\circ$.

Sanity check: the dot product is positive, so the angle had to come out acute — it did. And $0.4665 \le 1$, as Cauchy–Schwarz demands; a cosine above $1$ would prove an arithmetic slip.
:::

:::example[Forcing orthogonality]
Find all $k$ for which $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 1, -1\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle -1, k, 3\rangle$ are orthogonal.
:::

:::solution
Orthogonality is one scalar equation:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (2)(-1) + (1)(k) + (-1)(3) = k - 5 = 0,$$

so $k = 5$. Sanity check: with $k = 5$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle -1,5,3\rangle$ and $-2 + 5 - 3 = 0$.

Note that the condition was linear in $k$, so there is exactly one solution. Orthogonality to a fixed nonzero vector always cuts a hyperplane out of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and here the unknown moved along a line, which meets that hyperplane once.
:::

:::example[Working with norms without components]
Suppose $\|\mathbf{u}\| = 3$, $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 4$ and the angle between them is $60^\circ$. Compute $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ and $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|$.
:::

:::solution
By the geometric form,

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (3)(4)\cos 60^\circ = 12 \cdot \tfrac12 = 6.$$

Then the expansion of a squared difference gives

$$\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 9 - 12 + 16 = 13,$$

so $\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{13}$.

Sanity check: the triangle inequality predicts $\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\| \le 3 + 4 = 7$, and the reverse triangle inequality predicts it is at least $|4-3| = 1$. Since $1 \le \sqrt{13} \approx 3.6 \le 7$, the answer is in range. No components were ever needed: the identities do all the work.
:::

:::example[A dependence hidden in the numbers]
Show that $\langle 3, -2\rangle$ and $\langle 4, 6\rangle$ are orthogonal, and find the area of the rectangle they span.
:::

:::solution
Dot them: $(3)(4) + (-2)(6) = 12 - 12 = 0$, so they are orthogonal.

Because the sides meet at a right angle, the parallelogram they span is a rectangle with side lengths

$$\|\langle 3,-2\rangle\| = \sqrt{13}, \qquad \|\langle 4,6\rangle\| = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13},$$

so its area is $\sqrt{13}\cdot 2\sqrt{13} = 26$.

Sanity check: the Pythagorean theorem predicts $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 13 + 52 = 65$; directly, $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \langle 7,4\rangle$ and $49 + 16 = 65$. Consistent.
:::

:::pitfall
$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ does *not* mean one of the vectors is $\mathbf{0}$. Unlike real numbers, vectors have genuine "zero divisors" for this operation: $\langle 1,0\rangle\cdot\langle 0,1\rangle = 0$ with neither factor zero. Cancellation fails for the same reason — from $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{w}$ you may conclude only that $\mathbf{u}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}$, not that $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}$.
:::

## Orthogonal projection

<!-- section: projection -->

Here is the question. Given a nonzero direction $\mathbf{v}$, how much of a vector $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\mathbf{v}$? The answer should be a vector $\mathbf{p}$ on the line $\{t\mathbf{v}\}$, and the remainder $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p}$ should contain no trace of the direction $\mathbf{v}$ — meaning it should be orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$. Those two requirements determine $\mathbf{p}$ completely, and the derivation is three lines.

Write $\mathbf{p} = c\mathbf{v}$ for an unknown scalar $c$; this encodes the first requirement. The second requirement says

$$(\mathbf{u} - c\mathbf{v})\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0.$$

Expand by bilinearity: $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} - c\,(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = 0$. Since $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ we have $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{v}\|^2 > 0$ and may divide:

$$c = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|^2}.$$

The formula was not guessed; it was forced. And the derivation simultaneously proves that such a $\mathbf{p}$ exists and that there is only one.

:::definition[Vector and scalar projection]{#projection-definition}
Let $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$. The **vector projection** of $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$ and the **scalar projection** (the signed length of the shadow) are

$$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}.$$
:::

Keep the two straight. The vector projection is a vector parallel to $\mathbf{v}$; the scalar projection is a number, and it is signed — negative when $\mathbf{u}$ leans away from $\mathbf{v}$, which is exactly when the angle exceeds $90^\circ$. They are related by $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = (\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u})\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}$, and consequently $\|\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\| = |\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}|$.

Note also that $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ depends only on the *line* through $\mathbf{v}$, not on $\mathbf{v}$ itself: replacing $\mathbf{v}$ by $5\mathbf{v}$ multiplies the numerator by $5$ and the denominator by $25$, and the extra $\mathbf{v}$ factor restores the balance. The scalar projection does depend on the sign of $\mathbf{v}$ but not on its length. And $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ is indispensable: the zero vector determines no line to project onto, and the formula would divide by zero.

:::theorem[Orthogonal decomposition]{#orthogonal-decomposition}
Let $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$. Every $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ can be written in exactly one way as

$$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_{\parallel} + \mathbf{u}_{\perp}$$

with $\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}$ a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{\perp}$ orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$. Explicitly,

$$\mathbf{u}_{\parallel} = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_{\perp} = \mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}.$$
:::

:::proof
*Existence.* Define $\mathbf{u}_\parallel$ and $\mathbf{u}_\perp$ by the displayed formulas. Their sum is $\mathbf{u}$, and $\mathbf{u}_\parallel$ is a multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ by construction. Orthogonality is the computation that produced the formula in the first place:

$$\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} - \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0.$$

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $\mathbf{u} = a\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = a'\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}'$ with $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}'\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$. Then $(a - a')\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w}$. Dot both sides with $\mathbf{v}$: the right side gives $0$, and the left gives $(a-a')\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. As $\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \neq 0$, we get $a = a'$ and hence $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w}'$.
:::

Because $\mathbf{u}_\parallel$ and $\mathbf{u}_\perp$ are orthogonal, the Pythagorean theorem applies to the decomposition:

$$\|\mathbf{u}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}_{\parallel}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\|^2,$$

which is a useful check on any projection computation — the two pieces must account for the original length, and neither piece can be longer than $\mathbf{u}$ itself.

The projection is also the *closest* point of the line to $\mathbf{u}$, which is what makes it the right notion of approximation.

:::theorem[Best approximation on a line]{#best-approximation}
Let $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{p} = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$. Then for every scalar $t$,

$$\|\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}\| \ge \|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p}\|,$$

with equality only when $t\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{p}$. Consequently the distance from the tip of $\mathbf{u}$ to the line $\{t\mathbf{v}\}$ is $\|\mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|$.
:::

:::proof
Write $\mathbf{p} = c\mathbf{v}$ and split $\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{p}) + (c - t)\mathbf{v}$. The first bracket is $\mathbf{u}_\perp$, orthogonal to $\mathbf{v}$ and hence to $(c-t)\mathbf{v}$, so Pythagoras gives

$$\|\mathbf{u} - t\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\|^2 + (c-t)^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \; \ge \; \|\mathbf{u}_{\perp}\|^2.$$

The extra term is zero exactly when $t = c$.
:::

That "drop a perpendicular and you land on the nearest point" is one line of algebra, and it generalises verbatim from a line to any subspace. It is the engine of least-squares regression, of Fourier series (where the "directions" are sines and cosines), and of the Gram–Schmidt process, which builds an orthogonal set by repeatedly subtracting off projections.

:::intuition
Shine a light straight down on a tilted rod resting against a wall. The shadow on the floor is the projection of the rod onto the floor's direction: it records how much of the rod's length runs horizontally. Stand the rod upright and the shadow shrinks to a point — zero projection, the rod is orthogonal to the floor. Lay it flat and the shadow is the whole rod.

The perpendicular piece $\mathbf{u}_\perp$ is the part of the rod the shadow loses, and its length is exactly the height of the rod's tip above the floor — that is the "distance to the line" claim, seen from the side.
:::

:::example[Projection and decomposition]
Project $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2, 3\rangle$ onto $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4, 1\rangle$, and write $\mathbf{u}$ as a sum of a piece parallel to $\mathbf{v}$ and a piece orthogonal to it.
:::

:::solution
Two dot products:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 8 + 3 = 11, \qquad \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 16 + 1 = 17.$$

Hence

$$\mathbf{u}_\parallel = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \tfrac{11}{17}\langle 4,1\rangle = \left\langle \tfrac{44}{17},\; \tfrac{11}{17}\right\rangle,$$

$$\mathbf{u}_\perp = \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}_\parallel = \left\langle 2 - \tfrac{44}{17},\; 3 - \tfrac{11}{17}\right\rangle = \left\langle -\tfrac{10}{17},\; \tfrac{40}{17}\right\rangle.$$

Check orthogonality: $\mathbf{u}_\perp\cdot\mathbf{v} = -\tfrac{40}{17} + \tfrac{40}{17} = 0$.

Check lengths: $\|\mathbf{u}_\parallel\|^2 = \frac{121}{289}\cdot 17 = \frac{121}{17}$ and $\|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|^2 = \frac{100 + 1600}{289} = \frac{1700}{289} = \frac{100}{17}$. Their sum is $\frac{221}{17} = 13 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2$, as the Pythagorean identity requires.
:::

:::example[Distance from a point to a line through the origin]
Find the distance from the point $P = (2,3)$ to the line through the origin in the direction $\mathbf{v} = \langle 1,1\rangle$.
:::

:::solution
Let $\mathbf{u} = \langle 2,3\rangle$ be the position vector of $P$. By the best-approximation theorem the distance is $\|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|$.

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 5, \quad \mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 2, \quad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \tfrac{5}{2}\langle 1,1\rangle = \left\langle \tfrac52, \tfrac52\right\rangle.$$

$$\mathbf{u}_\perp = \left\langle 2 - \tfrac52,\; 3 - \tfrac52\right\rangle = \left\langle -\tfrac12,\; \tfrac12\right\rangle, \qquad \|\mathbf{u}_\perp\| = \sqrt{\tfrac14 + \tfrac14} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}.$$

Sanity check: the line is $y = x$, and the standard plane-geometry formula for the distance from $(2,3)$ to $x - y = 0$ is $|2-3|/\sqrt{2} = 1/\sqrt2$. Agreement.
:::

:::example[A negative scalar projection]
Find $\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ and $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ for $\mathbf{u} = \langle -3, 0, 0\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 2, 1, 2\rangle$, and interpret the sign.
:::

:::solution
The two ingredients are

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = (-3)(2) + 0 + 0 = -6, \qquad \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3.$$

Hence

$$\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{-6}{3} = -2, \qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{-6}{9}\langle 2,1,2\rangle = \left\langle -\tfrac43,\; -\tfrac23,\; -\tfrac43\right\rangle.$$

The negative sign says the angle between $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ exceeds $90^\circ$: the shadow falls on the ray *opposite* to $\mathbf{v}$, and its length is $2$.

Sanity check: $\|\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}\| = \tfrac23\|\mathbf{v}\| = 2$, which equals $|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}|$ as it must. And $2 \le \|\mathbf{u}\| = 3$, since a projection can never be longer than the vector it came from.
:::

:::pitfall
$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ and $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}$ are different vectors — they do not even point the same way. The first is parallel to $\mathbf{v}$, the second parallel to $\mathbf{u}$. Read the subscript as "onto", and when a problem says "the projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$", the vector you divide by is $\mathbf{a}$.
:::

## The cross product, area and volume

<!-- section: geometric -->

Everything so far worked in every $\mathbb{R}^n$. This section does not: it is special to $\mathbb{R}^3$, where — and essentially only where — there is a product of two vectors that returns a vector.

:::definition[Cross product]{#cross-product}
For $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, the **cross product** is

$$\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3\\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3\end{pmatrix} = \langle\, u_2v_3 - u_3v_2,\;\; u_3v_1 - u_1v_3,\;\; u_1v_2 - u_2v_1 \,\rangle.$$
:::

The determinant in the first expression is a mnemonic — its first row holds vectors, not scalars — but expanding it along the top row produces exactly the three components on the right, and that is how everyone computes it in practice. Note the middle component: the pattern is $u_3v_1 - u_1v_3$, with the indices $3,1$ before $1,3$. Writing $u_1v_3 - u_3v_1$ there is the most frequent computational error with this formula, and it silently flips the sign of one component.

:::theorem[The cross product is orthogonal to both factors]{#cross-orthogonal}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$,

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}) = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}) = 0.$$
:::

:::proof
Compute the first directly:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}) = u_1(u_2v_3 - u_3v_2) + u_2(u_3v_1 - u_1v_3) + u_3(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1).$$

Expanding, the six terms are $u_1u_2v_3, -u_1u_3v_2, u_2u_3v_1, -u_1u_2v_3, u_1u_3v_2, -u_2u_3v_1$, which cancel in pairs. The second identity is the same computation with the roles of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ exchanged, or follows from it together with anticommutativity below.
:::

This is the property that makes the cross product useful: it manufactures a normal direction to a plane out of two vectors lying in it, which is exactly what the last section of this chapter needs.

:::theorem[Algebraic properties of the cross product]{#cross-properties}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and scalars $c$:

1. $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = -(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{u})$ (anticommutativity);
2. $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$;
3. $\mathbf{u}\times(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{w}$ (distributivity);
4. $(c\mathbf{u})\times\mathbf{v} = c(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{u}\times(c\mathbf{v})$;
5. $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.
:::

:::proof
Each component of $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ has the form $u_av_b - u_bv_a$. Swapping $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ turns this into $v_au_b - v_bu_a$, the negative of the original, which is property 1. Setting $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u}$ in property 1 gives $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{u} = -(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{u})$, so $2(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{0}$ and property 2 follows. For 3, the first component of the left side is $u_2(v_3+w_3) - u_3(v_2+w_2)$, which expands to $(u_2v_3 - u_3v_2) + (u_2w_3 - u_3w_2)$, the sum of the first components on the right; the other two components are identical in form. Property 4 follows because each component is linear in the entries of each factor, and 5 because every component has a factor from $\mathbf{0}$.
:::

Two properties are conspicuously missing. The cross product is **not commutative** — property 1 says reversing the order reverses the vector — and it is **not associative**: with $\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{0}$ we get $(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{i})\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0}$, while $\mathbf{i}\times(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}) = \mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}$. So the expression $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}$ is meaningless without brackets.

The length of $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ is where the geometry is, and it comes from an identity that is pure algebra.

:::lemma[Lagrange's identity]{#lagrange-identity}
For all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$,

$$\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|^2 + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2.$$
:::

:::proof
Expand $\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|^2 = (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)^2 + (u_3v_1-u_1v_3)^2 + (u_1v_2-u_2v_1)^2$. Each square contributes two "square" terms and one cross term, giving

$$\sum_{a\neq b} u_a^2v_b^2 \; - \; 2\left(u_1u_2v_1v_2 + u_1u_3v_1v_3 + u_2u_3v_2v_3\right),$$

where the first sum runs over the six ordered pairs with $a \neq b$. Meanwhile

$$(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 = \left(\sum_a u_av_a\right)^2 = \sum_a u_a^2v_a^2 + 2\left(u_1u_2v_1v_2 + u_1u_3v_1v_3 + u_2u_3v_2v_3\right).$$

Adding the two lines, the cross terms cancel and the squares combine into $\sum_{a,b} u_a^2v_b^2$ over *all nine* pairs, which factors as $\left(\sum_a u_a^2\right)\left(\sum_b v_b^2\right) = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$.
:::

:::theorem[Magnitude of the cross product]{#cross-magnitude}
If $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ is the angle between nonzero $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, then

$$\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta.$$

Moreover $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are parallel.
:::

:::proof
By Lagrange's identity and the geometric form of the dot product,

$$\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - (\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2\left(1 - \cos^2\theta\right) = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2\sin^2\theta.$$

Take square roots; since $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ we have $\sin\theta \ge 0$, so no absolute value is needed. The cross product vanishes iff $\sin\theta = 0$ (or a factor is $\mathbf{0}$), i.e. iff $\theta \in \{0, \pi\}$, which is exactly parallelism.
:::

The direction of $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ among the two unit normals is settled by the **right-hand rule**: point the fingers of your right hand along $\mathbf{u}$, curl them towards $\mathbf{v}$, and your thumb points along $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$. Consistently, $\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}$, $\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j}$ — cyclic in $\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}$, with a sign change if you break the cycle.

:::corollary[Area of a parallelogram and a triangle]{#cross-area}
The parallelogram with adjacent sides $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ has area $\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|$, and the triangle with those two sides has area $\tfrac12\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|$.
:::

:::proof
Take $\mathbf{u}$ as the base, of length $\|\mathbf{u}\|$. The height is the distance from the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ to the line through $\mathbf{u}$, which is $\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta$. Base times height is $\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta = \|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|$. The triangle is half the parallelogram.
:::

Multiplying by a third vector recovers volume.

:::definition[Scalar triple product]{#triple-product}
For $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, the **scalar triple product** is the number $\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})$.
:::

:::theorem[Triple product as a determinant]{#triple-determinant}
$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = \det\begin{pmatrix} u_1 & u_2 & u_3 \\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3 \\ w_1 & w_2 & w_3\end{pmatrix}.$$

Consequently the triple product is unchanged by a cyclic shift, $\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{v}\cdot(\mathbf{w}\times\mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{w}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v})$, and changes sign when any two of the three vectors are swapped.
:::

:::proof
Dotting $\mathbf{u}$ with $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}$ gives

$$u_1(v_2w_3 - v_3w_2) - u_2(v_1w_3 - v_3w_1) + u_3(v_1w_2 - v_2w_1),$$

after rewriting the middle component $v_3w_1 - v_1w_3$ as $-(v_1w_3 - v_3w_1)$. That expression is precisely the cofactor expansion of the displayed $3\times 3$ determinant along its first row.

A cyclic shift of $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}$ is two row swaps of the determinant, and each swap changes its sign, so two swaps restore it. A single swap changes the sign once.
:::

:::theorem[Volume of a parallelepiped]{#parallelepiped-volume}
The parallelepiped with adjacent edges $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ has volume

$$V = \left|\,\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})\,\right|.$$
:::

:::proof
Take the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ as the base; by the area corollary its area is $\|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}\|$. The solid's height is the distance from the tip of $\mathbf{u}$ to the plane of the base, measured along the normal direction $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}$; that is the absolute scalar projection

$$h = \left|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{u}\right| = \frac{|\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})|}{\|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}\|}.$$

Volume is base area times height, and the factor $\|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}\|$ cancels.
:::

:::corollary[Coplanarity test]{#coplanarity}
Three vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ lie in a common plane through the origin — equivalently, they are linearly dependent — if and only if

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = 0.$$
:::

:::proof
The parallelepiped they span degenerates to a flat figure exactly when its volume is $0$, and by the volume theorem that happens exactly when the triple product vanishes. Flatness means all three edges lie in one plane through the common corner. If they do, one of them is a combination of the other two (or two of them are already parallel), so the list is dependent; conversely a dependent list has one vector in the span of the others, which is a plane or less.
:::

:::intuition
The cross product answers "which way is up?" given two arrows lying on a table, and how much table they enclose. Parallel arrows enclose no area, so their cross product is the zero vector — that is the same "no new direction" idea as linear dependence, wearing different clothes.

The triple product then asks how far a third arrow lifts off that table. If it stays on the table, the box you build has no height and no volume — and the three arrows are dependent. That is why one determinant answers a geometric question (volume) and an algebraic one (dependence) at the same time.
:::

:::example[Cross product, area, and a normal direction]
For $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v} = \langle 4,5,6\rangle$, compute $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$, the area of the parallelogram they span, and the area of the triangle with those sides.
:::

:::solution
Component by component, watching the middle sign:

$$\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \langle (2)(6)-(3)(5),\;\; (3)(4)-(1)(6),\;\; (1)(5)-(2)(4)\rangle = \langle -3,\; 6,\; -3\rangle.$$

Then

$$\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{9 + 36 + 9} = \sqrt{54} = 3\sqrt{6},$$

so the parallelogram has area $3\sqrt 6$ and the triangle has area $\tfrac{3\sqrt6}{2}$.

Sanity check: dot the result with each factor. $\langle -3,6,-3\rangle\cdot\langle 1,2,3\rangle = -3 + 12 - 9 = 0$ and $\langle -3,6,-3\rangle\cdot\langle 4,5,6\rangle = -12 + 30 - 18 = 0$. Both vanish, as the orthogonality theorem requires.
:::

:::example[Volume of a parallelepiped]
Find the volume of the parallelepiped with edges $\mathbf{u} = \langle 1,2,3\rangle$, $\mathbf{v} = \langle 0,1,4\rangle$, $\mathbf{w} = \langle 5,6,0\rangle$.
:::

:::solution
Expand the determinant along the first row:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = 1\big((1)(0)-(4)(6)\big) - 2\big((0)(0)-(4)(5)\big) + 3\big((0)(6)-(1)(5)\big),$$

$$= 1(-24) - 2(-20) + 3(-5) = -24 + 40 - 15 = 1.$$

The volume is $|1| = 1$.

Sanity check: the value is nonzero, so the three edges are not coplanar and a genuine solid exists — consistent with the fact that no one of the three vectors is visibly a combination of the others. Had the sign come out negative, the volume would still be $1$; the sign only records the handedness of the edge ordering.
:::

:::example[Testing three vectors for coplanarity]
Are $\mathbf{a} = \langle 1,1,0\rangle$, $\mathbf{b} = \langle 0,1,1\rangle$, $\mathbf{c} = \langle 1,2,1\rangle$ coplanar?
:::

:::solution
Compute the triple product as a determinant along the first row:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = 1\big((1)(1)-(1)(2)\big) - 1\big((0)(1)-(1)(1)\big) + 0 = 1(-1) - 1(-1) = 0.$$

The volume is zero, so the three vectors are coplanar and the list is linearly dependent.

Sanity check: find the dependence explicitly. Indeed $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \langle 1,2,1\rangle = \mathbf{c}$, so $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}$ is a nontrivial combination — dependence confirmed without any determinant.
:::

:::example[Area of a triangle from three points]
Find the area of the triangle with vertices $A = (1,0,2)$, $B = (2,1,0)$, $C = (0,3,1)$.
:::

:::solution
Convert points to edge vectors at $A$:

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 1, 1, -2\rangle, \qquad \overrightarrow{AC} = \langle -1, 3, -1\rangle.$$

Cross them:

$$\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle (1)(-1)-(-2)(3),\;\; (-2)(-1)-(1)(-1),\;\; (1)(3)-(1)(-1)\rangle = \langle 5,\; 3,\; 4\rangle.$$

Its norm is $\sqrt{25+9+16} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2$, so the triangle's area is $\tfrac{5\sqrt2}{2}$.

Sanity check: $\langle 5,3,4\rangle\cdot\langle 1,1,-2\rangle = 5 + 3 - 8 = 0$ and $\langle 5,3,4\rangle\cdot\langle -1,3,-1\rangle = -5 + 9 - 4 = 0$, so the computed vector really is normal to the triangle's plane. This normal is used again in the next section to write down the plane's equation.
:::

:::pitfall
The cross product exists only in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (and, in a different guise, $\mathbb{R}^7$). Writing $\langle 1,2\rangle\times\langle 3,4\rangle$ is meaningless. If a plane problem calls for a perpendicular in $\mathbb{R}^2$, use the rotation $\langle a,b\rangle \mapsto \langle -b, a\rangle$ instead, which is orthogonal to $\langle a,b\rangle$ because $-ab + ba = 0$.
:::

## Lines and planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$

<!-- section: lines-planes -->

Everything in this chapter now pays out as concrete geometry. A line is determined by a point and a direction; a plane by a point and a normal. Both descriptions turn straight into equations.

:::definition[Vector and parametric equations of a line]{#line-equations}
The line through the point $P_0$ with position vector $\mathbf{r}_0$ and direction $\mathbf{d} \neq \mathbf{0}$ is the set of points with position vector

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{r}_0 + t\,\mathbf{d}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Writing $\mathbf{r}_0 = \langle x_0,y_0,z_0\rangle$ and $\mathbf{d} = \langle a,b,c\rangle$, the **parametric equations** are

$$x = x_0 + at, \qquad y = y_0 + bt, \qquad z = z_0 + ct.$$
:::

The vector form says exactly what a line is: start at $P_0$ and travel any multiple of $\mathbf{d}$. The parameter $t$ is a coordinate along the line; $t = 0$ gives $P_0$ and negative $t$ goes the other way. Neither $\mathbf{r}_0$ nor $\mathbf{d}$ is unique — any point of the line will do for $\mathbf{r}_0$, and any nonzero multiple of $\mathbf{d}$ for the direction — so two very different-looking parametrisations can describe the same line. To decide whether they do, check that the directions are parallel and that one line's base point satisfies the other's equations.

Eliminating $t$ gives a description with no parameter at all.

:::proposition[Symmetric equations of a line]{#symmetric-equations}
If $a, b, c$ are all nonzero, the line above consists of exactly the points $(x,y,z)$ with

$$\frac{x-x_0}{a} = \frac{y-y_0}{b} = \frac{z-z_0}{c}.$$
:::

:::proof
From the parametric equations, $t = (x-x_0)/a$, $t = (y-y_0)/b$ and $t = (z-z_0)/c$ whenever the denominators are nonzero. A point lies on the line precisely when some single $t$ produces all three coordinates, that is, precisely when the three expressions agree.
:::

If one component of $\mathbf{d}$ is zero — say $c = 0$ — that coordinate is constant along the line and the symmetric form is written as $\frac{x-x_0}{a} = \frac{y-y_0}{b}$, $z = z_0$. Never write a zero denominator.

Now planes. A plane is pinned down not by directions inside it but by the one direction perpendicular to it.

:::definition[Normal vector]{#normal-vector}
A **normal vector** to a plane is a nonzero vector orthogonal to every vector lying in that plane.
:::

:::theorem[Point–normal and general equations of a plane]{#plane-equation}
Let $\mathbf{n} = \langle a,b,c\rangle \neq \mathbf{0}$ and let $P_0 = (x_0,y_0,z_0)$. The plane through $P_0$ with normal $\mathbf{n}$ is the set of points $P$ with position vectors $\mathbf{r}$ satisfying

$$\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0) = 0,$$

equivalently

$$a(x-x_0) + b(y-y_0) + c(z-z_0) = 0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad ax + by + cz = d$$

with $d = ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0$. Conversely, every equation $ax+by+cz = d$ with $\langle a,b,c\rangle \neq \mathbf{0}$ describes a plane with normal $\langle a,b,c\rangle$.
:::

:::proof
A point $P$ lies in the plane exactly when the displacement $\overrightarrow{P_0P} = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_0$ lies in the plane's directions, which by the definition of a normal happens exactly when $\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0) = 0$. Expanding that dot product componentwise gives the second form, and moving the constants to the right gives the third.

For the converse, given $ax+by+cz = d$ with, say, $a \neq 0$, the point $P_0 = (d/a, 0, 0)$ satisfies it, and subtracting $ax_0+by_0+cz_0 = d$ from $ax+by+cz = d$ returns the point–normal form with $\mathbf{n} = \langle a,b,c\rangle$.
:::

The coefficients of $x, y, z$ *are* the normal vector: that single observation answers most plane questions instantly. Two planes are parallel when their normals are parallel; they are perpendicular when their normals are orthogonal; and a line is parallel to a plane when its direction is orthogonal to the plane's normal.

Three non-collinear points determine a plane, and the cross product is how you find its normal.

:::recipe[Plane through three points]{#plane-through-points}
Given non-collinear $A$, $B$, $C$:

1. Form two edge vectors $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$.
2. Set $\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}$; it is nonzero because the points are not collinear.
3. Write $\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\overrightarrow{OA}) = 0$ and expand to $ax+by+cz=d$.
4. Check by substituting all three points.
:::

:::definition[Angle between two planes]{#plane-angle}
The angle between two planes is the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$ with

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{\|\mathbf{n}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{n}_2\|},$$

where $\mathbf{n}_1, \mathbf{n}_2$ are normals to the two planes.
:::

The absolute value is there because a plane has two opposite normals, so without it the answer would depend on an arbitrary sign; taking the modulus reports the acute angle, which is the one meant by "the angle between two planes". The same convention gives the angle between two lines from their direction vectors.

The two distance formulas are both projections in disguise.

:::theorem[Distance from a point to a plane]{#point-plane-distance}
The distance from the point $P_1 = (x_1,y_1,z_1)$ to the plane $ax+by+cz = d$ is

$$D = \frac{|ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 - d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}.$$
:::

:::proof
Let $\mathbf{n} = \langle a,b,c\rangle$ and let $P_0$ be any point of the plane, so $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_0 = d$. The distance from $P_1$ to the plane is measured along the normal direction, so it is the absolute scalar projection of $\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}$ onto $\mathbf{n}$:

$$D = \left|\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{n}}\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}\right| = \frac{\left|\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_0)\right|}{\|\mathbf{n}\|} = \frac{|\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_1 - d|}{\|\mathbf{n}\|},$$

which is the stated formula. That this really is the distance — i.e. that the nearest point of the plane is the foot of the perpendicular — is the best-approximation theorem applied to the decomposition $\overrightarrow{P_0P_1} = \mathbf{w}_\parallel + \mathbf{w}_\perp$ along $\mathbf{n}$: moving within the plane changes only $\mathbf{w}_\perp$, and by Pythagoras any such change can only lengthen the total. Note the answer does not depend on which $P_0$ was chosen, since only $d$ survives.
:::

:::theorem[Distance from a point to a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$]{#point-line-distance}
The distance from a point $P_1$ to the line through $P_0$ with direction $\mathbf{d}\neq\mathbf{0}$ is

$$D = \frac{\left\|\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}\times\mathbf{d}\right\|}{\|\mathbf{d}\|}.$$
:::

:::proof
Put $\mathbf{w} = \overrightarrow{P_0P_1}$ and let $\theta$ be the angle between $\mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{d}$. The perpendicular distance from $P_1$ to the line is the leg opposite $\theta$ in the right triangle with hypotenuse $\mathbf{w}$, namely $\|\mathbf{w}\|\sin\theta$. By the magnitude theorem, $\|\mathbf{w}\times\mathbf{d}\| = \|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{d}\|\sin\theta$, so dividing by $\|\mathbf{d}\|$ gives exactly $\|\mathbf{w}\|\sin\theta$.

Equivalently, $D = \|\mathbf{w} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{d}}\mathbf{w}\|$ by best approximation; the cross-product form is the same number packaged to avoid computing the projection.
:::

:::intuition
A plane's equation $ax+by+cz=d$ is a single sentence: "your position, dotted with the normal, comes out to $d$." Every point of the plane gives the same reading; points on one side read more, points on the other read less, and the distance formula is just that reading's error divided by the length of the measuring stick $\mathbf{n}$ — dividing by $\|\mathbf{n}\|$ converts the reading into honest units of length.
:::

:::example[Line through two points, in three forms]
Find vector, parametric and symmetric equations for the line through $A = (2,-1,3)$ and $B = (4, 1, 7)$.
:::

:::solution
A direction is the displacement: $\mathbf{d} = \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 2, 2, 4\rangle$, which may be simplified to $\langle 1,1,2\rangle$ since only the direction matters.

Vector form, based at $A$:

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2,-1,3\rangle + t\langle 1,1,2\rangle.$$

Parametric: $x = 2+t$, $y = -1+t$, $z = 3+2t$. Symmetric, solving each for $t$:

$$\frac{x-2}{1} = \frac{y+1}{1} = \frac{z-3}{2}.$$

Sanity check: $t = 2$ gives $(4, 1, 7) = B$, and substituting $B$ into the symmetric form gives $2 = 2 = 2$.
:::

:::example[Plane through three points]
Find an equation of the plane through $A = (1,0,2)$, $B = (2,1,0)$ and $C = (0,3,1)$.
:::

:::solution
The normal was computed in the previous section: with $\overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 1,1,-2\rangle$ and $\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle -1,3,-1\rangle$,

$$\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle 5, 3, 4\rangle.$$

Point–normal form at $A$:

$$5(x-1) + 3(y-0) + 4(z-2) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 5x + 3y + 4z = 13.$$

Sanity check: $A$ gives $5+0+8 = 13$; $B$ gives $10+3+0 = 13$; $C$ gives $0+9+4 = 13$. All three lie on it.
:::

:::example[Distance from a point to a plane]
Find the distance from $P = (4,1,-2)$ to the plane $2x - y + 2z = 6$.
:::

:::solution
Here $\mathbf{n} = \langle 2,-1,2\rangle$ with $\|\mathbf{n}\| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3$. Evaluate the left-hand side at $P$:

$$2(4) - (1) + 2(-2) = 8 - 1 - 4 = 3.$$

So

$$D = \frac{|3 - 6|}{3} = \frac{3}{3} = 1.$$

Sanity check: step one unit from $P$ along the unit normal $\tfrac13\langle 2,-1,2\rangle$, reaching $\left(4+\tfrac23,\; 1-\tfrac13,\; -2+\tfrac23\right)$. Substituting: $2\cdot\tfrac{14}{3} - \tfrac23 + 2\cdot\left(-\tfrac43\right) = \tfrac{28 - 2 - 8}{3} = 6$. The point landed on the plane, so the distance is indeed $1$.
:::

:::example[Where a line meets a plane]
Find the point where the line $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1,2,3\rangle + t\langle 1,1,1\rangle$ crosses the plane $2x - y + z = 5$.
:::

:::solution
Substitute the parametric coordinates $x = 1+t$, $y = 2+t$, $z = 3+t$ into the plane equation:

$$2(1+t) - (2+t) + (3+t) = 2 + 2t - 2 - t + 3 + t = 2t + 3.$$

Set $2t + 3 = 5$, so $t = 1$ and the intersection point is $\mathbf{r}(1) = (2,3,4)$.

Sanity check: $2(2) - 3 + 4 = 5$. A unique solution was expected because the direction $\langle 1,1,1\rangle$ is not orthogonal to the normal $\langle 2,-1,1\rangle$ — their dot product is $2$, not $0$ — so the line is not parallel to the plane and must meet it exactly once.
:::

:::example[Angle between two planes]
Find the acute angle between the planes $x + y = 1$ and $x + z = 2$.
:::

:::solution
Read the normals off the coefficients: $\mathbf{n}_1 = \langle 1,1,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf{n}_2 = \langle 1,0,1\rangle$. Then

$$\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2 = 1, \qquad \|\mathbf{n}_1\| = \|\mathbf{n}_2\| = \sqrt2,$$

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|1|}{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2} = \frac12,$$

so $\theta = 60^\circ$.

Sanity check: the planes are not parallel (the normals are not multiples) and not perpendicular (the dot product is not $0$), so an angle strictly between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ was expected.
:::

:::example[Distance from a point to a line in space]
Find the distance from $P_1 = (3,1,0)$ to the line through $P_0 = (1,0,1)$ with direction $\mathbf{d} = \langle 2,1,-2\rangle$.
:::

:::solution
First the displacement: $\mathbf{w} = \overrightarrow{P_0P_1} = \langle 2, 1, -1\rangle$. Cross it with the direction:

$$\mathbf{w}\times\mathbf{d} = \langle (1)(-2)-(-1)(1),\;\; (-1)(2)-(2)(-2),\;\; (2)(1)-(1)(2)\rangle = \langle -1,\; 2,\; 0\rangle.$$

Its norm is $\sqrt{1+4+0} = \sqrt5$, and $\|\mathbf{d}\| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3$, so

$$D = \frac{\sqrt5}{3}.$$

Sanity check: $D$ must not exceed $\|\mathbf{w}\| = \sqrt6 \approx 2.449$, the distance from $P_1$ to the particular point $P_0$ on the line; indeed $\sqrt5/3 \approx 0.745$ is smaller. The two agree only when $\mathbf{w}$ is already perpendicular to $\mathbf{d}$, and here $\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{d} = 4 + 1 + 2 = 7 \neq 0$, so strict inequality was correct.
:::

:::pitfall
The formula $D = |ax_1+by_1+cz_1-d|/\|\mathbf{n}\|$ is only valid with the plane written as $ax+by+cz = d$ and the *same* coefficients used in $\mathbf{n}$. If the plane is given as $4x - 2y + 4z = 12$, you may either use $\mathbf{n} = \langle 4,-2,4\rangle$ with $d = 12$, or divide the whole equation through by $2$ first — but never mix a reduced normal with an unreduced constant. The two consistent choices give the same distance; a mixed one does not.
:::

:::summary[The dot product, the cross product, and the geometry they give]
In $\mathbb{R}^n$ the dot product $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\sum_i u_iv_i$ is symmetric, bilinear and positive definite, with $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. From those four properties alone follow Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}|\le\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|$ with equality exactly when one vector is a multiple of the other; the triangle inequality; the Pythagorean characterisation $\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|^2=\|\mathbf{u}\|^2+\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 \iff \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$; and, because Cauchy–Schwarz puts the quotient in $[-1,1]$, the angle $\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}$. For $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ every $\mathbf{u}$ splits uniquely as $\mathbf{u}=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}_\perp$ with $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}$, and $\|\mathbf{u}_\perp\|$ is the distance from $\mathbf{u}$ to the line $\{t\mathbf{v}\}$ — the projection is the nearest point on it.

Only in $\mathbb{R}^3$: $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ is orthogonal to both factors, anticommutative but neither commutative nor associative, and Lagrange's identity $\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|^2+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2=\|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ gives $\|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta$, the area of the parallelogram on $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$, vanishing exactly when they are parallel. The triple product $\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})$ is the $3\times3$ determinant of the three rows, its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped, and it vanishes exactly when the three vectors are coplanar, that is, dependent. Cashed out: a line is $\mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{d}$ with $\mathbf{d}\neq\mathbf{0}$, a plane is $\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0$, i.e. $ax+by+cz=d$ with $\mathbf{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle$ read off the coefficients, and the two distance formulas $\frac{|ax_1+by_1+cz_1-d|}{\|\mathbf{n}\|}$ and $\frac{\|\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}\times\mathbf{d}\|}{\|\mathbf{d}\|}$ are projections in disguise.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing the dot and cross products.** $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is a scalar and exists in every $\mathbb{R}^n$; $\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}$ is a vector and exists only in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Expressions like $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})\times\mathbf{w}$ are not wrong, they are meaningless.
- **Writing $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = c\|\mathbf{v}\|$.** The correct rule is $\|c\mathbf{v}\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|$; norms are never negative, so with $c = -3$ and $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 4$ the answer is $12$.
- **Reading $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ as "one of them is zero".** It means orthogonal. For the same reason, you cannot cancel a common factor out of a dot product equation.
- **The middle component of the cross product.** It is $u_3v_1 - u_1v_3$, not $u_1v_3 - u_3v_1$. Check every cross product by dotting the answer with both factors; both must give $0$.
- **Projecting the wrong way round.** $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{v}$, and the denominator is $\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}$. Swapping the roles gives a different vector pointing in a different direction.
- **Normalising $\mathbf{0}$, or projecting onto it.** Both divide by zero. Check $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ before either.
- **Losing the absolute value in a distance.** Both the point–plane and the point–line formulas take a modulus; a negative distance always signals a dropped $|\cdot|$.
- **Assuming a span is a plane.** The span of two vectors is a plane only when they are independent; if one is a multiple of the other, the span is a line. Always check for parallelism first.
- **Mixing dimensions.** $\langle 1,2\rangle + \langle 1,2,3\rangle$ and $\langle 1,2\rangle\cdot\langle 1,2,3\rangle$ are both undefined. Vectors must live in the same $\mathbb{R}^n$ before any operation combines them.
- **Treating a line that misses the origin as a span.** Every span contains $\mathbf{0}$. The line $\mathbf{r}_0 + t\mathbf{d}$ is a span only when $\mathbf{r}_0$ is itself a multiple of $\mathbf{d}$.
