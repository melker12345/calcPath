# Determinants

The determinant is a single number that encodes the most essential properties of a square matrix: whether it is invertible, how it scales volumes, and whether it preserves or reverses orientation. Despite appearing as an arcane formula, it has deep geometric meaning.

The central fact: $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det(A)\neq 0$. A zero determinant means the matrix collapses space to a lower dimension — information is permanently lost and the transformation cannot be undone.

We compute determinants by cofactor expansion, exploit transformation properties to simplify calculations, and connect the result to Cramer's Rule and geometry.

## Cofactor expansion {#cofactor}

:::definition[Determinant of a 2×2 matrix]{#det-2x2}
$$\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} = ad-bc,$$

the main diagonal product minus the anti-diagonal product.
:::

:::definition[Cofactor expansion]{#cofactor-expansion}
For an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ and any fixed row $i$,

$$\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\,a_{ij}\,M_{ij},$$

where the $(i,j)$ minor $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. The same formula works along any column.
:::

The factor $(-1)^{i+j}$ is the cofactor sign, and it creates a checkerboard starting with $+$ at $(1,1)$: $\begin{pmatrix}+&-&+\\-&+&-\\+&-&+\end{pmatrix}$. Since the expansion is valid along *any* row or column, always pick the one with the most zeros to minimise computation.

:::proposition[Determinant of a triangular matrix]{#triangular-determinant}
If $A$ is square and triangular (upper or lower), then $\det(A)$ is the product of its diagonal entries.
:::

Row reducing to triangular form is therefore often faster than cofactor expansion for large matrices.

:::intuition
The $2\times 2$ determinant is just 'main diagonal product minus off-diagonal product.' For $3\times 3$, you split the problem into three $2\times 2$ problems, alternating signs. Each step peels off one layer of the calculation.
:::

:::example[3×3 determinant by cofactor expansion]
$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&4&5\\1&0&6\end{pmatrix}$. Expand along row 1 (start with the most zeros, but row 1 works fine here).
:::

:::solution
1. $\det(A) = 1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}4&5\\0&6\end{pmatrix} - 2\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}0&5\\1&6\end{pmatrix} + 3\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
2. $= 1(24-0) - 2(0-5) + 3(0-4)$.
3. $= 24 + 10 - 12 = 22$.
4. Sanity check: expand instead along column 1 (entries $1,0,1$). Only the two $1$'s contribute: $1\cdot(+1)\det\begin{pmatrix}4&5\\0&6\end{pmatrix} + 1\cdot(-1)^{3+1}\det\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\4&5\end{pmatrix} = 24 + (10-12) = 22$. Same answer from a different expansion. ✓
:::

:::example[Triangular shortcut]
$B = \begin{pmatrix}2&7&-3\\0&5&1\\0&0&4\end{pmatrix}$ is upper triangular (all entries below the diagonal are $0$).
:::

:::solution
1. Rather than a full cofactor expansion, use the rule: the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries.
2. $\det(B) = 2\cdot 5\cdot 4 = 40$.
3. Why it works: expanding along column 1, only the top-left $2$ survives, and the remaining $2\times 2$ block $\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\0&4\end{pmatrix}$ is again triangular. Each expansion step just peels off the next diagonal entry.
:::

## Properties of determinants {#properties}

:::proposition[Row operations and the determinant]{#det-row-operations}
Let $A$ be square.

1. Swapping two rows multiplies $\det(A)$ by $-1$.
2. Scaling a row by $c$ multiplies $\det(A)$ by $c$.
3. Adding a scalar multiple of one row to another leaves $\det(A)$ unchanged.
:::

These three rules make row reduction a fast alternative to cofactor expansion.

:::theorem[Product rule for determinants]{#det-product}
For square matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same size,

$$\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B).$$
:::

Composition scales volumes multiplicatively. Two consequences follow at once: $\det(A^{-1})=1/\det(A)$ for invertible $A$, and $\det(A^k)=\det(A)^k$.

Transpose rule: $\det(A^T)=\det(A)$. Rows and columns play symmetric roles in the determinant.

Scaling: $\det(cA) = c^n\det(A)$ for an $n\times n$ matrix, because each of the $n$ rows is scaled by $c$.

A matrix has $\det=0$ whenever: two rows are equal, a row is all zeros, a row is a linear combination of the others (i.e., the rows are linearly dependent). All of these are equivalent to the columns being linearly dependent.

:::intuition
Swapping rows is like flipping a shape — same size, opposite orientation, so the sign flips. Scaling a row scales the 'height' of the shape, proportionally scaling the area. Adding a row multiple is a shear — it slides rows without changing area.
:::

:::example[Determinant by row reduction]
$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&5&3\\1&0&8\end{pmatrix}$. We reduce to upper triangular using only row-replacement operations, which do **not** change the determinant.
:::

:::solution
1. $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ gives new row 2 $= (0,\,1,\,-3)$. Determinant unchanged.
2. $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$ gives new row 3 $= (0,\,-2,\,5)$. Determinant unchanged.
3. $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + 2R_2$ gives new row 3 $= (0,\,0,\,-1)$. Determinant unchanged. The matrix is now $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&1&-3\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
4. Triangular, so $\det(A) = 1\cdot 1\cdot(-1) = -1$.
5. Sanity check by cofactor expansion along row 1: $1(40-0) - 2(16-3) + 3(0-5) = 40 - 26 - 15 = -1$. ✓ (If we had used a row *swap* or *scaling* here, we would have had to divide out the corresponding factor at the end.)
:::

:::example[Using the product and inverse rules]
Suppose $\det(A) = 3$ and $\det(B) = -2$ for two $3\times 3$ matrices. Find $\det(A^2 B^{-1})$.
:::

:::solution
1. Product rule: $\det(A^2 B^{-1}) = \det(A)^2\,\det(B^{-1})$.
2. Inverse rule: $\det(B^{-1}) = 1/\det(B) = 1/(-2) = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ (valid because $\det(B)\neq 0$, so $B$ is invertible).
3. $\det(A^2 B^{-1}) = 3^2 \cdot (-\tfrac{1}{2}) = 9\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{2}) = -\tfrac{9}{2}$.
:::

## Cramer's Rule {#cramer}

:::theorem[Cramer's Rule]{#cramers-rule}
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with $\det(A)\neq 0$, so that $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a unique solution. Then each component of that solution is

$$x_i = \frac{\det(A_i)}{\det(A)},$$

where $A_i$ is the matrix $A$ with its $i$-th column replaced by $\mathbf{b}$.
:::

The hypothesis $\det(A)\neq 0$ is what makes the formula meaningful. If $\det(A)=0$ the rule does not apply — the system has either no solution or infinitely many, and the division by zero signals exactly that failure.

Cramer's Rule is elegant but computationally expensive — computing $n+1$ determinants of $n\times n$ matrices is $O(n!)$ for each determinant via cofactor expansion, far slower than $O(n^3)$ Gaussian elimination.

Its primary value is theoretical. The formula shows that solutions are rational functions of the entries of $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$, which is useful in sensitivity analysis and proofs involving matrix calculus (e.g., the derivative of the inverse).

:::intuition
Cramer's Rule gives you each unknown in one formula. It's like having a direct calculator for the answer, without any elimination steps. The problem is the calculator is very slow for big systems. Gaussian elimination is the fast version for practical computation.
:::

:::example[Cramer's Rule for a 2×2 system]
Solve $3x+y=7$, $2x+5y=4$ using Cramer's Rule.
:::

:::solution
1. $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&5\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}7\\4\end{pmatrix}$, $\det(A)=15-2=13$.
2. $x = \det\begin{pmatrix}7&1\\4&5\end{pmatrix}/13 = (35-4)/13 = 31/13$.
3. $y = \det\begin{pmatrix}3&7\\2&4\end{pmatrix}/13 = (12-14)/13 = -2/13$.
4. Sanity check by substituting back. Equation 1: $3\cdot\tfrac{31}{13} + \left(-\tfrac{2}{13}\right) = \tfrac{93-2}{13} = \tfrac{91}{13} = 7$. ✓ Equation 2: $2\cdot\tfrac{31}{13} + 5\cdot\left(-\tfrac{2}{13}\right) = \tfrac{62-10}{13} = \tfrac{52}{13} = 4$. ✓
:::

## Geometric interpretation {#geometric}

:::theorem[Determinant as area and volume]{#det-volume}
For a $2\times 2$ matrix $A$,

$$|\det(A)| = \text{area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns of } A,$$

and for a $3\times 3$ matrix it is the volume of the parallelepiped they span.
:::

If $\det(A)>0$, the transformation preserves orientation (counterclockwise goes to counterclockwise). If $\det(A)<0$, orientation is reversed (a reflection is involved).

If $\det(A)=0$, the columns are linearly dependent and the transformation collapses the full-dimensional space to a lower-dimensional one — it maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ onto a proper subspace.

:::proposition[Multilinear and alternating]{#det-multilinear}
The determinant is multilinear and alternating in the columns: it is linear in each column separately, holding the others fixed, and swapping two columns flips its sign.
:::

Unpacking that: scaling one column by $c$ scales $\det$ by $c$, and splitting one column into a sum splits the determinant into a sum; "alternating" forces $\det=0$ whenever two columns are equal, since swapping them changes nothing but must flip the sign. The parallelogram area interpretation makes this geometric: scaling a side scales the area; reflecting a side flips orientation.

:::intuition
The determinant tells you how much the matrix squishes or stretches space. A value of $3$ means it triples all areas. A value of $0.5$ means it halves them. A negative value means it flips orientation. Zero means it squashes a whole dimension flat — irreversibly.
:::

:::example[Area and orientation of a parallelogram]
Find the area and orientation of the parallelogram spanned by the columns $\mathbf{u}=\langle 3,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf{v}=\langle 1,2\rangle$.
:::

:::solution
1. Form $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (columns $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$) and compute $\det(A) = 3\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 = 5$.
2. Area $= |\det(A)| = |5| = 5$.
3. Orientation: $\det(A) = 5 > 0$, so the ordered pair $(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})$ has the same (counterclockwise) orientation as the standard basis — no reflection.
4. Sanity check with a shear, which preserves area. Replace $\mathbf{v}$ by $\mathbf{v} - \tfrac{1}{3}\mathbf{u} = \langle 1-1,\ 2-\tfrac{1}{3}\rangle = \langle 0,\tfrac{5}{3}\rangle$. The sheared parallelogram spans $\langle 3,1\rangle$ and $\langle 0,\tfrac{5}{3}\rangle$, with $\det\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\1&\tfrac{5}{3}\end{pmatrix} = 3\cdot\tfrac{5}{3} - 0 = 5$. Same area. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- Applying $\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc$ directly to a $3\times 3$ matrix. You must expand.
- Getting cofactor signs wrong. Entry $(i,j)$ has sign $(-1)^{i+j}$: $(1,1)$ is $+$, $(1,2)$ is $-$, $(2,1)$ is $-$, etc.
- Writing $\det(cA)=c\,\det(A)$ for an $n\times n$ matrix. The correct formula is $\det(cA)=c^n\det(A)$.
- Assuming $\det(A+B)=\det(A)+\det(B)$. The determinant is multilinear in each row separately, not in the whole matrix.
- Expanding along the wrong row and missing the sign pattern. Always recheck the checkerboard before assigning cofactor signs.
