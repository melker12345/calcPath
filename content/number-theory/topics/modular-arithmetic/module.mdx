# Modular Arithmetic

Arithmetic modulo $n$ is what remains of the integers after you agree to stop distinguishing numbers that differ by a multiple of $n$. That single act of forgetting is astonishingly productive. It turns an infinite ring into a finite one, so questions that are open over $\mathbb{Z}$ become finite checks; it turns the multiplicative structure of $\mathbb{Z}$ into a finite abelian group, so Lagrange's theorem applies and exponents start to repeat; and it turns divisibility statements into equations, which can be added, multiplied and solved.

The chapter is built in four movements. The first develops the congruence relation itself: that it is an equivalence relation compatible with addition and multiplication, that a residue is invertible exactly when it is coprime to the modulus, that the inverse is produced by the extended Euclidean algorithm, and that powers can be computed in logarithmically many multiplications. The second is the Chinese Remainder Theorem, proved with existence, uniqueness and an explicit reconstruction formula, and then pushed past its usual hypothesis to say exactly when a system with non-coprime moduli is solvable. The third recasts everything structurally: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a quotient ring, congruence is the congruence relation attached to the ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem becomes an isomorphism of rings.

The fourth movement asks a question the first three cannot answer: *which residues are squares?* Linear equations mod $n$ are settled completely by the Euclidean algorithm, but $x^2 \equiv a \pmod p$ is a genuinely different problem, and the theory that answers it — Euler's criterion, the Legendre symbol, quadratic reciprocity and its computational cousin the Jacobi symbol — is the first place in elementary number theory where the answer is deep rather than merely careful.

Throughout, $n$ and $m$ denote positive integers, $p$ denotes a prime, and $\gcd$ is always taken to be non-negative. Divisibility and the Euclidean algorithm are assumed, as developed in *Divisibility and Primes*; the language of rings and ideals is used from the third movement onward, but the arithmetic never depends on it.

## Congruences and residue classes

<!-- section: congruences-residues -->

The definition is a piece of deliberate economy: instead of recording a number, record only what it leaves behind after division by $n$.

:::definition[Congruence modulo $n$]{#congruence}
Let $n$ be a positive integer. For integers $a$ and $b$ we write

$$a \equiv b \pmod{n}$$

and say $a$ is **congruent to $b$ modulo $n$**, when $n \mid a - b$, that is, when $a - b = nk$ for some integer $k$. The integer $n$ is the **modulus**.
:::

Everything in this chapter follows from the fact that this relation is an equivalence relation *and* that it respects the ring operations. Neither half is automatic: the relation "$a$ and $b$ have the same number of digits" is an equivalence relation that respects nothing, and the relation "$a \le b$" respects addition but is not an equivalence relation.

:::theorem[Congruence is an equivalence relation]{#congruence-equivalence}
For each fixed $n \ge 1$, congruence modulo $n$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive on $\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::proof
*Reflexive:* $a - a = 0 = n \cdot 0$, so $n \mid a - a$.

*Symmetric:* if $a - b = nk$ then $b - a = n(-k)$, and $-k$ is an integer.

*Transitive:* if $a - b = nk$ and $b - c = n\ell$, then adding gives $a - c = n(k + \ell)$, and $k + \ell$ is an integer.

Each step used only that the multiples of $n$ form a set containing $0$, closed under negation and closed under addition.
:::

:::definition[Residue class]{#residue-class}
The equivalence class of $a$ under congruence modulo $n$ is the **residue class**

$$[a]_n = \{\, a + nk : k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\},$$

written $[a]$ or $\bar a$ when $n$ is clear. The set of all residue classes modulo $n$ is denoted $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::proposition[Least residues classify the classes]{#least-residues}
Every integer is congruent modulo $n$ to exactly one of $0, 1, \dots, n-1$. Consequently $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly $n$ elements, and $a \equiv b \pmod n$ if and only if $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder on division by $n$.
:::

:::proof
Division with remainder gives unique integers $q, r$ with $a = qn + r$ and $0 \le r < n$. Then $a - r = qn$, so $a \equiv r$, which proves that at least one such representative exists.

For uniqueness, suppose $0 \le r < s < n$ with $r \equiv s \pmod n$. Then $n \mid s - r$ while $0 < s - r < n$, and no positive multiple of $n$ is smaller than $n$. So $s - r$ cannot be a multiple of $n$, a contradiction.

Finally, if $a = q_1 n + r_1$ and $b = q_2 n + r_2$ with $0 \le r_1, r_2 < n$, then $a - b = (q_1-q_2)n + (r_1 - r_2)$, so $n \mid a-b$ exactly when $n \mid r_1 - r_2$, which by the uniqueness just proved happens exactly when $r_1 = r_2$.
:::

The number $r$ produced here is the **least nonnegative residue** of $a$ modulo $n$, written $a \bmod n$. Note the typographical distinction that will be kept throughout: $a \bmod n$ is a *number*, the remainder; $a \equiv b \pmod n$ is a *statement* about two numbers.

:::intuition
A clock face is the whole of the definition. The hour hand at $15$ o'clock and at $3$ o'clock points at the same place, because the two readings differ by one full revolution. Arithmetic mod $12$ is what a clock does: you may add and multiply hour-readings and the answer is again a reading, because a whole number of revolutions added anywhere along the way changes nothing about where the hand ends up.
:::

The reason congruence is worth a symbol as heavy as $\equiv$, rather than being phrased as a divisibility statement each time, is that it can be manipulated like an equation.

:::theorem[Congruence is compatible with the ring operations]{#congruence-compatible}
Fix $n \ge 1$ and suppose $a \equiv a' \pmod n$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod n$. Then

$$a + b \equiv a' + b', \qquad a - b \equiv a' - b', \qquad ab \equiv a'b' \pmod n .$$
:::

:::proof
Write $a - a' = nk$ and $b - b' = n\ell$.

For the sum, $(a+b) - (a'+b') = (a - a') + (b - b') = n(k + \ell)$. For the difference, replace $\ell$ by $-\ell$.

For the product, the trick is to change one factor at a time:

$$ab - a'b' = ab - a'b + a'b - a'b' = b(a - a') + a'(b - b') = n\big(bk + a'\ell\big),$$

and $bk + a'\ell$ is an integer. Hence $n \mid ab - a'b'$.
:::

:::corollary[Polynomials respect congruence]{#polynomial-congruence}
Let $f$ be a polynomial with integer coefficients. If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ then $f(a) \equiv f(b) \pmod n$.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Congruence is compatible with the ring operations* and induction on $j$, $a \equiv b$ implies $a^j \equiv b^j$ for every $j \ge 0$: the case $j = 0$ is $1 \equiv 1$, and $a^{j+1} = a^j \cdot a \equiv b^j \cdot b = b^{j+1}$. Multiplying by the integer coefficient $c_j$ gives $c_j a^j \equiv c_j b^j$, and summing the finitely many terms gives $f(a) \equiv f(b)$.
:::

This corollary is the engine behind every elementary divisibility test. Since $10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9$, any integer written as $\sum c_j 10^j$ satisfies $\sum c_j 10^j \equiv \sum c_j \pmod 9$: a number is congruent to its digit sum mod $9$. Since $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, the same argument gives the alternating digit sum mod $11$.

:::example[A divisibility test from a congruence]
Determine $8\,675\,309 \bmod 9$ and $8\,675\,309 \bmod 11$ without dividing.
:::

:::solution
1. Mod $9$: since $10 \equiv 1$, the number is congruent to its digit sum, $8+6+7+5+3+0+9 = 38$.
2. Repeat on $38$: $3 + 8 = 11$, then $1 + 1 = 2$. So $8\,675\,309 \equiv 2 \pmod 9$.
3. Mod $11$: since $10 \equiv -1$, the number is congruent to the alternating sum of its digits taken from the units end, $9 - 0 + 3 - 5 + 7 - 6 + 8 = 16$.
4. And $16 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$. So the two remainders are $2$ and $5$.
:::

:::example[Ruling out a solution by choosing a modulus]
Show that $x^2 + y^2 = 2\,023$ has no solution in integers.
:::

:::solution
1. Work modulo $4$. By the corollary *Polynomials respect congruence* it is enough to know the squares of the four residue classes: $0^2 \equiv 0$, $1^2 \equiv 1$, $2^2 \equiv 0$, $3^2 \equiv 1$.
2. So every square is $\equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 4$, and a sum of two squares is $\equiv 0, 1$ or $2 \pmod 4$ — never $3$.
3. But $2\,023 = 4 \cdot 505 + 3$, so $2\,023 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
4. No solution exists. The whole argument is a finite check on four residue classes, replacing a search over infinitely many integers.
:::

:::definition[Complete residue system]{#complete-residue-system}
A set of integers $\{r_1, \dots, r_n\}$ is a **complete residue system modulo $n$** if every integer is congruent modulo $n$ to exactly one $r_i$.
:::

:::proposition[Recognising a complete residue system]{#crs-test}
A set of $n$ integers is a complete residue system modulo $n$ if and only if its elements are pairwise incongruent modulo $n$. Moreover, if $\{r_1,\dots,r_n\}$ is a complete residue system and $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then so is $\{ar_1 + b, \dots, ar_n + b\}$ for any integer $b$.
:::

:::proof
If the $n$ elements are pairwise incongruent they meet $n$ distinct classes, and there are only $n$ classes by the proposition *Least residues classify the classes*, so they meet every class exactly once. The converse is immediate from "exactly one".

For the second claim, suppose $ar_i + b \equiv ar_j + b \pmod n$. Subtracting $b$ and using compatibility, $ar_i \equiv ar_j$, so $n \mid a(r_i - r_j)$. Since $\gcd(a,n) = 1$, Euclid's lemma gives $n \mid r_i - r_j$, hence $i = j$. So the $n$ new elements are pairwise incongruent, and the first claim applies.
:::

## Modular inverses and linear congruences

<!-- section: inverses-congruences -->

Addition, subtraction and multiplication descend to residue classes. Division does not, and the question of exactly when it does is the first genuinely arithmetic question of the chapter.

:::definition[Modular inverse]{#modular-inverse}
An integer $a$ is **invertible modulo $n$** (a **unit** mod $n$) if there is an integer $x$ with

$$ax \equiv 1 \pmod n .$$

Such an $x$ is an **inverse of $a$ modulo $n$**, written $a^{-1}$.
:::

:::theorem[Invertibility criterion]{#inverse-criterion}
Let $n \ge 1$. An integer $a$ is invertible modulo $n$ if and only if $\gcd(a, n) = 1$. When it is, an inverse is obtained from any Bézout identity $au + nv = 1$ by taking $x = u$.
:::

:::proof
($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. By Bézout's identity there are integers $u, v$ with $au + nv = 1$. Reducing modulo $n$ kills the term $nv$, leaving $au \equiv 1 \pmod n$, so $u$ is an inverse.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $ax \equiv 1 \pmod n$, so $ax - 1 = nk$ for some $k$, that is $ax - nk = 1$. Let $d = \gcd(a,n)$. Then $d$ divides both $ax$ and $nk$, hence divides their difference $1$, so $d = 1$.
:::

The proof is not merely an existence statement: the extended Euclidean algorithm computes the Bézout coefficients $u, v$ explicitly and in $O(\log n)$ steps, so the inverse is as cheap as the gcd.

:::corollary[Inverses are unique modulo $n$]{#inverse-unique}
If $a$ is invertible modulo $n$, its inverse is unique modulo $n$.
:::

:::proof
Suppose $ax \equiv 1$ and $ay \equiv 1 \pmod n$. Then

$$x \equiv x(ay) = (xa)y \equiv 1 \cdot y = y \pmod n,$$

where each congruence used compatibility of multiplication with congruence.
:::

:::recipe[Inverting $a$ modulo $n$ by the extended Euclidean algorithm]{#invert-recipe}
1. Run the Euclidean algorithm on $(n, a)$, recording each quotient.
2. If the last nonzero remainder is not $1$, then $\gcd(a,n) > 1$ and no inverse exists; stop.
3. Otherwise back-substitute to write $1 = au + nv$.
4. Reduce $u$ modulo $n$ to the least nonnegative residue. That is $a^{-1}$.
5. Check: multiply out $a \cdot u$ and confirm the remainder is $1$.
:::

:::example[An inverse by back-substitution]
Compute $17^{-1} \bmod 101$.
:::

:::solution
1. Euclid: $101 = 5 \cdot 17 + 16$, then $17 = 1 \cdot 16 + 1$, then $16 = 16 \cdot 1 + 0$. The gcd is $1$, so the inverse exists.
2. Back-substitute from the second-to-last line: $1 = 17 - 1 \cdot 16$.
3. Substitute $16 = 101 - 5 \cdot 17$: $1 = 17 - (101 - 5\cdot 17) = 6 \cdot 17 - 1 \cdot 101$.
4. So $6 \cdot 17 \equiv 1 \pmod{101}$, giving $17^{-1} \equiv 6$.
5. Check: $6 \cdot 17 = 102 = 101 + 1 \equiv 1$. ✓
:::

:::example[When the inverse does not exist]
Does $14$ have an inverse modulo $35$?
:::

:::solution
1. $\gcd(14, 35) = 7 \neq 1$, so by the theorem *Invertibility criterion* there is no inverse.
2. To see the failure concretely, note $14 \cdot x$ is always a multiple of $7$ modulo $35$, since $35$ is too: every value of $14x \bmod 35$ lies in $\{0, 7, 14, 21, 28\}$, and $1$ is not among them.
:::

With inverses in hand, the general linear congruence can be solved completely — including the case where no inverse exists.

:::theorem[Linear congruence]{#linear-congruence}
Let $a, b$ be integers, $n \ge 1$, and $d = \gcd(a,n)$. The congruence

$$ax \equiv b \pmod n$$

has a solution if and only if $d \mid b$. In that case the solutions form exactly $d$ residue classes modulo $n$: if $x_0$ is one solution, they are

$$x_0, \; x_0 + \tfrac{n}{d}, \; x_0 + 2\tfrac{n}{d}, \; \dots, \; x_0 + (d-1)\tfrac{n}{d} \pmod n,$$

equivalently a single residue class modulo $n/d$.
:::

:::proof
The congruence says $ax - b = nk$ for some $k$, i.e. that $b = ax - nk$ lies in the set $\{ax + ny\}$ of integer combinations of $a$ and $n$. That set is exactly $d\mathbb{Z}$, so a solution exists if and only if $d \mid b$.

Assume $d \mid b$ and write $a = da'$, $b = db'$, $n = dn'$ with $\gcd(a', n') = 1$. Dividing the relation $ax - b = nk$ through by $d$ shows that $ax \equiv b \pmod n$ is equivalent to

$$a'x \equiv b' \pmod{n'} .$$

Since $\gcd(a', n') = 1$, the theorem *Invertibility criterion* gives $(a')^{-1}$ mod $n'$, and multiplying by it shows the solution set is the single class $x \equiv (a')^{-1}b' \pmod{n'}$ — in particular non-empty, and a class modulo $n' = n/d$.

Finally, one class modulo $n/d$ splits into exactly $d$ classes modulo $n$, namely those listed, because two integers congruent mod $n/d$ differ by a multiple of $n/d$, and the multiples of $n/d$ fall into exactly $d$ classes mod $n$, represented by $0, n/d, \dots, (d-1)n/d$.
:::

:::example[A linear congruence with several solutions]
Solve $14x \equiv 30 \pmod{100}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $d = \gcd(14, 100) = 2$ and $2 \mid 30$, so by the theorem *Linear congruence* there are exactly $2$ solution classes mod $100$.
2. Divide through by $2$: $7x \equiv 15 \pmod{50}$.
3. Invert $7$ mod $50$: $7 \cdot 43 = 301 = 6 \cdot 50 + 1$, so $7^{-1} \equiv 43$.
4. Then $x \equiv 43 \cdot 15 = 645 \equiv 645 - 12\cdot 50 = 45 \pmod{50}$.
5. Lifting to modulus $100$: $x \equiv 45$ or $x \equiv 95 \pmod{100}$.
6. Check: $14 \cdot 45 = 630 \equiv 30$, and $14 \cdot 95 = 1330 \equiv 30 \pmod{100}$. ✓
:::

:::pitfall
You may not "cancel" a common factor from both sides of a congruence while leaving the modulus alone. From $6 \equiv 16 \pmod{10}$, cancelling $2$ gives the false $3 \equiv 8 \pmod{10}$. The correct cancellation law is: if $ca \equiv cb \pmod n$ then $a \equiv b \pmod{n/\gcd(c,n)}$. Cancelling $c$ from the modulus as well is only free when $\gcd(c,n) = 1$.
:::

## Powers, orders and fast exponentiation

<!-- section: modular-powers -->

A quantity like $7^{1000} \bmod 13$ is hopeless to compute by first forming $7^{1000}$, an integer with over eight hundred digits. Two ideas make it trivial: reduce after every multiplication, and never perform more than $O(\log e)$ multiplications.

:::theorem[Correctness of square-and-multiply]{#fast-exponentiation}
Let $e \ge 1$ have binary expansion $e = \sum_{i=0}^{k} b_i 2^i$ with $b_i \in \{0,1\}$ and $b_k = 1$. Define $r_k = a$ and, for $i = k-1$ down to $0$,

$$r_i = \begin{cases} r_{i+1}^2 \bmod n, & b_i = 0,\\ a \cdot r_{i+1}^2 \bmod n, & b_i = 1.\end{cases}$$

Then $r_0 \equiv a^e \pmod n$, and the computation uses at most $2k \le 2\log_2 e$ modular multiplications of numbers below $n^2$.
:::

:::proof
Let $e_i = \sum_{j=i}^{k} b_j 2^{j-i}$ be the integer whose binary digits are the leading bits $b_k \dots b_i$ of $e$. Then $e_k = 1$ and $e_i = 2e_{i+1} + b_i$, and $e_0 = e$.

We show by downward induction that $r_i \equiv a^{e_i}$. The base case is $r_k = a = a^{e_k}$. If $r_{i+1} \equiv a^{e_{i+1}}$, then since congruence is compatible with multiplication, $r_{i+1}^2 \equiv a^{2e_{i+1}}$ and $a \cdot r_{i+1}^2 \equiv a^{2e_{i+1}+1}$; in both cases the recursion yields $r_i \equiv a^{2e_{i+1} + b_i} = a^{e_i}$. Taking $i = 0$ gives $r_0 \equiv a^e$.

Each of the $k$ steps costs one squaring and at most one further multiplication, so at most $2k$ multiplications; and since each $r_i$ is reduced below $n$, every product formed is below $n^2$.
:::

Reducing at every step is the whole point: it is what keeps the operands small, and by the theorem *Congruence is compatible with the ring operations* it changes no answer.

:::example[Square-and-multiply in practice]
Compute $7^{100} \bmod 13$.
:::

:::solution
1. Write $100 = 1100100_2$, so the bit string is $1,1,0,0,1,0,0$.
2. Start $r = 7$. Bit $1$: $r \gets 7^2 \cdot 7 = 343 \equiv 5 \pmod{13}$.
3. Bit $0$: $r \gets 5^2 = 25 \equiv 12$. Bit $0$: $r \gets 12^2 = 144 \equiv 1$.
4. Bit $1$: $r \gets 1^2 \cdot 7 = 7$. Bit $0$: $r \gets 49 \equiv 10$. Bit $0$: $r \gets 100 \equiv 9$.
5. So $7^{100} \equiv 9 \pmod{13}$. Six squarings and two extra multiplications replaced ninety-nine.
:::

Once exponents are cheap, the question becomes which exponents matter. Powers of a fixed unit must eventually repeat, because there are only finitely many residues, and the first repeat happens at $1$.

:::definition[Multiplicative order]{#order}
Let $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. The **order** of $a$ modulo $n$, written $\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$, is the least positive integer $d$ with $a^d \equiv 1 \pmod n$.
:::

:::proposition[The order divides every exponent that works]{#order-divides}
Let $\gcd(a,n)=1$ and $d = \operatorname{ord}_n(a)$. Then for a positive integer $e$,

$$a^e \equiv 1 \pmod n \iff d \mid e .$$

In particular $a^i \equiv a^j \pmod n$ if and only if $i \equiv j \pmod d$.
:::

:::proof
If $d \mid e$, say $e = dm$, then $a^e = (a^d)^m \equiv 1^m = 1$.

Conversely suppose $a^e \equiv 1$. Divide: $e = qd + r$ with $0 \le r < d$. Then

$$1 \equiv a^e = (a^d)^q a^r \equiv a^r \pmod n .$$

If $r > 0$ this contradicts minimality of $d$, so $r = 0$ and $d \mid e$. For the last claim, assume $i \ge j$ and multiply $a^i \equiv a^j$ by $(a^{-1})^j$, which exists because $a$ is a unit, to get $a^{i-j} \equiv 1$.
:::

:::theorem[Fermat's little theorem]{#fermat-little}
Let $p$ be prime and $p \nmid a$. Then

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p, \qquad \text{and hence } a^p \equiv a \pmod p \text{ for every } a .$$
:::

:::proof
Since $p \nmid a$ we have $\gcd(a,p)=1$, so by the proposition *Recognising a complete residue system* the list $a\cdot 1, a \cdot 2, \dots, a(p-1)$ is pairwise incongruent mod $p$, and none is $\equiv 0$. Hence it is a permutation of $1, 2, \dots, p-1$ modulo $p$. Multiplying the two lists together,

$$a^{p-1}(p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod p .$$

Each of $1, \dots, p-1$ is coprime to $p$, so $(p-1)!$ is a unit mod $p$ and may be cancelled, leaving $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$. Multiplying by $a$ gives $a^p \equiv a$, which also holds when $p \mid a$ since then both sides are $\equiv 0$.
:::

:::corollary[Euler's theorem]{#euler-theorem}
If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$ then $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, where $\varphi(n)$ counts the integers in $\{1,\dots,n\}$ coprime to $n$. Consequently $\operatorname{ord}_n(a) \mid \varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
Let $u_1, \dots, u_{\varphi(n)}$ be the least residues coprime to $n$. Multiplication by $a$ permutes them: each $au_i$ is coprime to $n$ (a product of two units is a unit), and $au_i \equiv au_j$ forces $u_i \equiv u_j$ after multiplying by $a^{-1}$. Multiplying all of them,

$$a^{\varphi(n)} \prod_i u_i \equiv \prod_i u_i \pmod n,$$

and the product of units is a unit, so it cancels. The divisibility statement is then the proposition *The order divides every exponent that works*.
:::

:::example[Reducing an exponent with the order]
Compute $2^{1000} \bmod 11$ and the order of $2$ modulo $11$.
:::

:::solution
1. The powers of $2$ mod $11$ are $2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{11}(2) = 10 = \varphi(11)$.
2. By the proposition *The order divides every exponent that works*, only $1000 \bmod 10$ matters, and $1000 \equiv 0$.
3. Hence $2^{1000} \equiv 2^{0} = 1 \pmod{11}$.
:::

:::pitfall
Euler's theorem reduces the exponent modulo $\varphi(n)$, not modulo $n$, and only when $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Computing $2^{10} \bmod 10$ as $2^{10 \bmod 4} = 2^2 = 4$ is wrong twice over: $\gcd(2,10)\ne 1$, and the true value is $1024 \equiv 4$ only by coincidence — the same false step gives $2^{12} \equiv 2^0 = 1$, whereas $2^{12} = 4096 \equiv 6 \pmod{10}$.
:::

## The ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$

<!-- section: ring-structure -->

The theorem *Congruence is compatible with the ring operations* says precisely that the operations of $\mathbb{Z}$ descend to residue classes. Naming the resulting object is what lets the rest of algebra be applied to it.

:::definition[The ring of residues]{#znz-ring}
On the set $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ of residue classes modulo $n$, define

$$[a] + [b] = [a+b], \qquad [a]\cdot[b] = [ab] .$$
:::

:::theorem[$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a commutative ring]{#znz-is-ring}
The operations above are well defined, and with them $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a commutative ring with zero $[0]$ and identity $[1]$.
:::

:::proof
*Well defined:* the definitions refer to representatives, so we must check the answer does not depend on which ones. If $[a]=[a']$ and $[b]=[b']$ then $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b'$, so by the theorem *Congruence is compatible with the ring operations* $a + b \equiv a'+b'$ and $ab \equiv a'b'$, i.e. $[a+b]=[a'+b']$ and $[ab]=[a'b']$.

*Axioms:* each is inherited. For instance associativity of multiplication reads $([a][b])[c] = [(ab)c] = [a(bc)] = [a]([b][c])$, where the middle equality is associativity in $\mathbb{Z}$. Commutativity, distributivity, the additive identity $[0]$, additive inverses $-[a]=[-a]$ and the multiplicative identity $[1]$ all transfer by the same one-line argument, because every axiom is an identity between polynomial expressions in the elements.
:::

:::theorem[The group of units]{#unit-group}
The invertible elements of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly the classes $[a]$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$. They form a group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ under multiplication, of order $\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
The description of the units is the theorem *Invertibility criterion* restated, noting that $\gcd(a,n)$ depends only on $[a]$: if $a \equiv a' \pmod n$ then $a' = a + nk$, and any common divisor of $a$ and $n$ divides $a'$, and conversely.

The units of any commutative ring form a group: $1$ is a unit; if $a, b$ are units then $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = 1$, so $ab$ is; and $a^{-1}$ is a unit with inverse $a$. Associativity is inherited. Counting: the classes coprime to $n$ are represented by the integers in $\{1, \dots, n\}$ coprime to $n$, and there are $\varphi(n)$ of those.
:::

:::theorem[$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime]{#field-iff-prime}
For $n \ge 2$, the following are equivalent: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field; $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain; $n$ is prime.
:::

:::proof
*Prime $\Rightarrow$ field.* If $n = p$ is prime, every $a$ with $[a] \neq [0]$ satisfies $p \nmid a$, hence $\gcd(a,p)=1$, hence $[a]$ is a unit by the theorem *The group of units*. So every nonzero element is invertible and $[1] \neq [0]$ because $p \ge 2$.

*Field $\Rightarrow$ domain.* In any field, if $[a][b]=[0]$ with $[a]\neq[0]$, multiplying by $[a]^{-1}$ gives $[b]=[0]$.

*Domain $\Rightarrow$ prime.* If $n$ is composite, write $n = st$ with $1 < s, t < n$. Then $[s]$ and $[t]$ are nonzero — neither $s$ nor $t$ is divisible by $n$, being smaller and positive — while $[s][t] = [n] = [0]$. So $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has zero divisors and is not a domain.
:::

:::notation
When $n = p$ is prime the field $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is written $\mathbb{F}_p$. Its multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ has order $p-1$, which is the group-theoretic content of Fermat's little theorem: the order of an element divides the order of the group.
:::

:::example[Zero divisors and units modulo $12$]
List the units and the zero divisors of $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, and solve $x^2 \equiv 4 \pmod{12}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Units: the $a \in \{1,\dots,12\}$ with $\gcd(a,12)=1$, namely $1, 5, 7, 11$ — four of them, and indeed $\varphi(12)=4$.
2. Each is its own inverse here: $5^2 = 25 \equiv 1$, $7^2 = 49 \equiv 1$, $11^2 = 121 \equiv 1$.
3. Zero divisors: the nonzero non-units $2,3,4,6,8,9,10$; for instance $3 \cdot 4 \equiv 0$ and $6 \cdot 2 \equiv 0$.
4. Testing all twelve classes, $x^2 \equiv 4$ holds for $x \equiv 2, 4, 8, 10$: **four** solutions to a quadratic. Over a field that is impossible, and the theorem *$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime* explains why it is possible here.
:::

## The Chinese Remainder Theorem

<!-- section: crt-statement -->

A congruence modulo $n$ is a piece of partial information about an integer. The Chinese Remainder Theorem says that when the moduli are pairwise coprime, several such pieces fit together in exactly one way — no consistency conditions to check, and no ambiguity beyond the obvious one.

:::theorem[Chinese Remainder Theorem]{#crt}
Let $n_1, \dots, n_k$ be pairwise coprime positive integers, that is $\gcd(n_i, n_j) = 1$ whenever $i \neq j$, and set $N = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$. For any integers $a_1, \dots, a_k$ the system

$$x \equiv a_1 \pmod{n_1}, \quad x \equiv a_2 \pmod{n_2}, \quad \dots, \quad x \equiv a_k \pmod{n_k}$$

has a solution, and the solution is unique modulo $N$.
:::

:::proof
*Uniqueness.* Suppose $x$ and $y$ both satisfy the system. Then $n_i \mid x - y$ for every $i$. Since the $n_i$ are pairwise coprime, their product divides $x-y$: inductively, if $n_1 \cdots n_{j}$ and $n_{j+1}$ both divide $x-y$ and are coprime — $\gcd(n_1\cdots n_j,\, n_{j+1}) = 1$ because $n_{j+1}$ is coprime to each factor — then their product divides $x - y$. Hence $N \mid x-y$, i.e. $x \equiv y \pmod N$.

*Existence.* Consider the map

$$\Phi: \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/n_1\mathbb{Z} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}/n_k\mathbb{Z}, \qquad [x]_N \mapsto ([x]_{n_1}, \dots, [x]_{n_k}),$$

which is well defined because $n_i \mid N$, so $x \equiv y \pmod N$ implies $x \equiv y \pmod{n_i}$. Uniqueness, just proved, says $\Phi$ is injective. Both sides are finite sets of the same size $N = n_1\cdots n_k$, so an injective map between them is surjective. Surjectivity is exactly the assertion that every tuple $(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ is achieved, i.e. that the system has a solution.
:::

The counting argument is short but gives no number. The next section repairs that with an explicit formula; the pigeonhole version is worth keeping because it is the one that generalises, and because it makes clear that existence and uniqueness are not two facts but one.

:::example[Two congruences by inspection]
Solve $x \equiv 2 \pmod 5$ and $x \equiv 3 \pmod 7$.
:::

:::solution
1. The moduli are coprime, so by the theorem *Chinese Remainder Theorem* there is exactly one solution class mod $35$.
2. List the integers $\equiv 3 \pmod 7$ below $35$: $3, 10, 17, 24, 31$.
3. Reduce each mod $5$: $3, 0, 2, 4, 1$. The value $2$ occurs at $17$.
4. So $x \equiv 17 \pmod{35}$. Searching the five candidates rather than all thirty-five is the small-scale version of the constructive method below.
:::

## The constructive formula

<!-- section: constructive-crt -->

To solve the system rather than merely know it is solvable, build a residue that is $1$ at one coordinate and $0$ at all the others, then take the obvious combination.

:::theorem[Explicit CRT reconstruction]{#crt-formula}
With $n_1,\dots,n_k$ pairwise coprime and $N = n_1\cdots n_k$, put

$$N_i = \frac{N}{n_i}, \qquad M_i \equiv N_i^{-1} \pmod{n_i} .$$

Each $M_i$ exists, and

$$x \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i N_i M_i \pmod N$$

is the unique solution of the system $x \equiv a_i \pmod{n_i}$.
:::

:::proof
First, $M_i$ exists: $N_i$ is the product of the $n_j$ with $j \neq i$, each coprime to $n_i$, so $\gcd(N_i, n_i) = 1$ and the theorem *Invertibility criterion* applies.

Now fix $j$ and reduce the sum modulo $n_j$. For $i \neq j$ the factor $N_i$ contains $n_j$ as a divisor, so $a_i N_i M_i \equiv 0 \pmod {n_j}$ and every such term drops out. Only the term $i = j$ survives, and there $N_j M_j \equiv 1 \pmod{n_j}$ by the choice of $M_j$. Hence

$$\sum_i a_i N_i M_i \equiv a_j \cdot 1 = a_j \pmod{n_j},$$

so the stated $x$ solves the system. By the theorem *Chinese Remainder Theorem* it is the only solution mod $N$.
:::

The elements $e_i = N_i M_i$ deserve a name: they satisfy $e_i \equiv 1 \pmod{n_i}$ and $e_i \equiv 0 \pmod{n_j}$ for $j \neq i$, so they behave like a basis of indicator functions, and the formula $x = \sum a_i e_i$ reads as "assemble $x$ from its coordinates". They depend only on the moduli, so they are computed once and reused for every right-hand side.

:::recipe[Solving a CRT system]{#crt-recipe}
1. Check the moduli are pairwise coprime; if not, use the theorem *Systems with non-coprime moduli* instead.
2. Form $N = \prod n_i$ and each $N_i = N/n_i$.
3. Invert $N_i$ modulo $n_i$ by the extended Euclidean algorithm to get $M_i$.
4. Form $x = \sum a_i N_i M_i$ and reduce modulo $N$.
5. Verify by reducing the answer modulo each $n_i$ in turn.
:::

:::example[Three congruences by the formula]
Solve $x \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, $x \equiv 3 \pmod 5$, $x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$.
:::

:::solution
1. $N = 105$, and $N_1 = 35$, $N_2 = 21$, $N_3 = 15$.
2. Inverses: $35 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ and $2^{-1} \equiv 2$, so $M_1 = 2$. $21 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $M_2 = 1$. $15 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so $M_3 = 1$.
3. Assemble: $x = 2(35)(2) + 3(21)(1) + 2(15)(1) = 140 + 63 + 30 = 233$.
4. Reduce: $233 - 2 \cdot 105 = 23$, so $x \equiv 23 \pmod{105}$.
5. Check: $23 = 7\cdot3+2$, $23 = 4\cdot 5+3$, $23 = 3\cdot 7 + 2$. ✓
:::

## Systems with non-coprime moduli

<!-- section: non-coprime-case -->

Drop the coprimality hypothesis and both halves of the theorem change. Solutions need no longer exist — $x \equiv 1 \pmod 2$ and $x \equiv 2 \pmod 4$ is contradictory, since the second forces $x$ even — and when they do exist, uniqueness is modulo the least common multiple rather than the product.

:::theorem[Two congruences, general moduli]{#two-congruences}
Let $m, n$ be positive integers and $d = \gcd(m,n)$. The system

$$x \equiv a \pmod m, \qquad x \equiv b \pmod n$$

has a solution if and only if $d \mid a - b$. When it does, the solution is unique modulo $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = mn/d$.
:::

:::proof
*Necessity.* If $x$ solves both, then $d \mid m \mid x - a$ and $d \mid n \mid x - b$, so $d$ divides $(x-b)-(x-a) = a - b$.

*Sufficiency.* The first congruence says $x = a + mt$ for some integer $t$. Substituting into the second, we need

$$a + mt \equiv b \pmod n, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad mt \equiv b - a \pmod n .$$

By the theorem *Linear congruence* this is solvable in $t$ exactly when $\gcd(m,n) = d$ divides $b-a$, which is the hypothesis, and then $t$ is determined modulo $n/d$. Writing $t = t_0 + (n/d)s$ gives

$$x = a + mt_0 + \frac{mn}{d}s,$$

so $x$ is determined modulo $mn/d = \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$.
:::

:::corollary[General systems]{#general-crt}
A system $x \equiv a_i \pmod{n_i}$, $i = 1,\dots,k$, has a solution if and only if

$$\gcd(n_i, n_j) \mid a_i - a_j \quad \text{for all } i \neq j,$$

and the solution is then unique modulo $\operatorname{lcm}(n_1,\dots,n_k)$.
:::

:::proof
Necessity is the necessity half of the theorem *Two congruences, general moduli* applied to each pair.

For sufficiency, induct on $k$. The case $k = 1$ is trivial. Suppose the first $k-1$ congruences have been merged into a single congruence $x \equiv c \pmod{L}$ with $L = \operatorname{lcm}(n_1, \dots, n_{k-1})$; note $c \equiv a_i \pmod {n_i}$ for each $i < k$. We must merge it with $x \equiv a_k \pmod{n_k}$, so by the theorem *Two congruences, general moduli* it suffices to show $g := \gcd(L, n_k)$ divides $c - a_k$.

Let $q$ be any prime power dividing $g$ exactly, say $q = p^\alpha \| g$. Then $p^\alpha \mid n_k$ and $p^\alpha \mid L$, and since $L$ is a least common multiple, $p^\alpha \mid n_i$ for some $i < k$. Hence $p^\alpha \mid \gcd(n_i, n_k)$, which divides $a_i - a_k$ by hypothesis; and $p^\alpha \mid n_i \mid c - a_i$. Adding, $p^\alpha \mid c - a_k$. As this holds for every prime power exactly dividing $g$, we get $g \mid c - a_k$. The merged modulus is $\operatorname{lcm}(L, n_k) = \operatorname{lcm}(n_1,\dots,n_k)$.
:::

:::example[A solvable system with overlapping moduli]
Solve $x \equiv 5 \pmod{12}$ and $x \equiv 11 \pmod{18}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $d = \gcd(12,18) = 6$ and $a - b = 5 - 11 = -6$, which is divisible by $6$. So a solution exists, unique mod $\operatorname{lcm}(12,18)=36$.
2. Write $x = 5 + 12t$ and substitute: $5 + 12t \equiv 11 \pmod{18}$, so $12t \equiv 6 \pmod{18}$.
3. Divide through by $6$: $2t \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so $t \equiv 2 \pmod 3$.
4. Take $t = 2$: $x = 5 + 24 = 29$. Hence $x \equiv 29 \pmod{36}$.
5. Check: $29 = 2\cdot 12 + 5$ and $29 = 18 + 11$. ✓
:::

:::example[An unsolvable system]
Show that $x \equiv 3 \pmod{10}$, $x \equiv 8 \pmod{15}$ has no solution.
:::

:::solution
1. $\gcd(10,15) = 5$ and $3 - 8 = -5$, which *is* divisible by $5$ — so this pair alone is fine, and in fact $x \equiv 23 \pmod{30}$ works.
2. Adjoin a third congruence $x \equiv 1 \pmod 6$. Now $\gcd(10,6) = 2$ must divide $3 - 1 = 2$ ✓, but $\gcd(15,6) = 3$ must divide $8 - 1 = 7$, and it does not.
3. By the corollary *General systems* the three-congruence system is unsolvable, even though every pair but one is compatible. Pairwise checking is genuinely necessary, and checking only consecutive pairs is not enough.
:::

## The isomorphism and its applications

<!-- section: crt-applications -->

The Chinese Remainder Theorem is a statement about sets of solutions, but the map in its proof respects addition and multiplication, and that upgrade is what makes the theorem a computational tool rather than a curiosity.

:::theorem[CRT as a ring isomorphism]{#crt-isomorphism}
If $\gcd(m,n)=1$ then the reduction map

$$\Phi : \mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \qquad [x]_{mn} \mapsto ([x]_m, [x]_n)$$

is an isomorphism of rings, where the right-hand side has componentwise operations. More generally $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} \cong \prod_i \mathbb{Z}/n_i\mathbb{Z}$ for pairwise coprime $n_i$ with product $N$.
:::

:::proof
$\Phi$ is well defined and bijective by the theorem *Chinese Remainder Theorem*. It is a ring homomorphism because reduction is: $[x+y]_m = [x]_m + [y]_m$ and $[xy]_m = [x]_m[y]_m$ by the definition of the operations in $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, and the same in the second coordinate, while $[1]_{mn} \mapsto ([1]_m,[1]_n)$ is the identity of the product. A bijective ring homomorphism is an isomorphism, its set-theoretic inverse being automatically a homomorphism.
:::

:::corollary[$\varphi$ is multiplicative]{#phi-multiplicative}
If $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ then $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
An isomorphism of rings carries units to units bijectively, so $(\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$. A pair is invertible componentwise exactly when both components are, so the units of a product are the product of the unit groups. Counting with the theorem *The group of units* gives $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$.
:::

The practical consequence is a divide-and-conquer strategy: to compute something modulo $N$, compute it modulo each prime power dividing $N$ — where the arithmetic is small and the structure is simpler — and reassemble.

:::example[A large power by splitting the modulus]
Compute $2^{100} \bmod 35$.
:::

:::solution
1. $35 = 5 \cdot 7$ with $\gcd(5,7)=1$, so work in $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$.
2. Mod $5$: $\operatorname{ord}_5(2) = 4$ and $100 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$, so $2^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
3. Mod $7$: $\operatorname{ord}_7(2) = 3$ and $100 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so $2^{100} \equiv 2^1 = 2 \pmod 7$.
4. Reassemble: solve $x \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, $x \equiv 2 \pmod 7$. The candidates $\equiv 2 \pmod 7$ are $2,9,16,23,30$, and $16 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.
5. So $2^{100} \equiv 16 \pmod{35}$.
:::

:::example[Counting square roots of $1$]
How many solutions does $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{105}$ have?
:::

:::solution
1. $105 = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7$, so by the theorem *CRT as a ring isomorphism*, $x$ corresponds to a triple and $x^2 \equiv 1$ holds exactly when it holds in each coordinate.
2. Modulo an odd prime $p$, $x^2 \equiv 1$ means $p \mid (x-1)(x+1)$, so $x \equiv \pm 1$: two solutions each.
3. The triples therefore number $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$, so there are $8$ solutions mod $105$.
4. Explicitly they are $1, 29, 34, 41, 64, 71, 76, 104$ — and note $8 \ne 2$, another reminder that a quadratic over a non-field can have many roots.
:::

## Congruence as an equivalence relation

<!-- section: definition-equivalence -->

It is worth pausing over what the theorem *Congruence is an equivalence relation* buys, because the payoff is structural rather than computational: an equivalence relation is the same thing as a partition, and a partition is the same thing as a new set whose points are the old set's classes.

:::theorem[Classes partition the integers]{#partition}
Fix $n \ge 1$. The residue classes modulo $n$ are non-empty, pairwise disjoint, and their union is $\mathbb{Z}$. Moreover $[a] = [b]$ if and only if $a \equiv b \pmod n$.
:::

:::proof
Each class is non-empty since $a \in [a]$ by reflexivity, and the union is all of $\mathbb{Z}$ for the same reason.

Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \varnothing$, say $c$ lies in both. Then $c \equiv a$ and $c \equiv b$, so by symmetry and transitivity $a \equiv b$. Now for any $x \in [a]$ we get $x \equiv a \equiv b$, so $x \in [b]$, and symmetrically; hence $[a] = [b]$. So two classes are either disjoint or equal.

Finally, if $a \equiv b$ then $b \in [a] \cap [b]$, which by the previous paragraph forces $[a]=[b]$; conversely $[a]=[b]$ gives $a \in [b]$, i.e. $a \equiv b$.
:::

:::definition[Quotient set and canonical projection]{#projection}
The set of classes is the **quotient** $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and the **canonical projection** is

$$\pi_n : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \qquad \pi_n(a) = [a]_n .$$
:::

The projection is surjective by construction and is very far from injective: each fibre $\pi_n^{-1}([a])$ is an infinite arithmetic progression of common difference $n$. What the theorem *Least residues classify the classes* adds is a canonical choice of representative in each fibre, which is what makes the quotient a *computable* object and not merely a well-defined one.

:::remark
Choosing representatives is a convention, not a fact. The least nonnegative residues $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ are standard, but the **least absolute residues** $\{-\lfloor (n-1)/2 \rfloor, \dots, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\}$ are often better: modulo $13$ they let one write $12 \equiv -1$, turning $12^{2} \equiv 1$ into a one-line observation. Both are complete residue systems, so nothing mathematical distinguishes them.
:::

:::example[Least absolute residues shorten a computation]
Compute $12^{34} \bmod 13$ and $11^{20} \bmod 13$ using signed representatives.
:::

:::solution
1. $12 \equiv -1 \pmod{13}$, so $12^{34} \equiv (-1)^{34} = 1$.
2. $11 \equiv -2 \pmod{13}$, so $11^{20} \equiv (-2)^{20} = 2^{20}$.
3. By Fermat's little theorem $2^{12} \equiv 1$, so $2^{20} \equiv 2^{8} = 256 = 19\cdot 13 + 9 \equiv 9$.
4. Hence $12^{34} \equiv 1$ and $11^{20} \equiv 9 \pmod{13}$.
:::

## Congruence as a ring congruence

<!-- section: ring-congruence -->

Abstract algebra isolates the property that made $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ possible, and gives it a name.

:::definition[Ring congruence]{#ring-congruence-def}
An equivalence relation $\sim$ on a ring $R$ is a **congruence relation** if it is compatible with the operations: whenever $a \sim a'$ and $b \sim b'$,

$$a + b \sim a' + b' \quad\text{and}\quad ab \sim a'b' .$$
:::

By the theorem *Congruence is compatible with the ring operations*, congruence mod $n$ is a congruence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$. The next result says that on $\mathbb{Z}$ there are no others.

:::theorem[Every congruence on $\mathbb{Z}$ is a congruence mod $n$]{#congruences-classified}
Let $\sim$ be a congruence relation on the ring $\mathbb{Z}$. Then there is a unique integer $n \ge 0$ such that $a \sim b \iff a \equiv b \pmod n$, where $n = 0$ gives equality.
:::

:::proof
Let $I = \{ a \in \mathbb{Z} : a \sim 0 \}$. Then $0 \in I$; if $a, b \in I$ then $a - b \sim 0 - 0 = 0$ by compatibility with addition and negation, so $a - b \in I$; and if $a \in I$ and $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ then $ra \sim r\cdot 0 = 0$, so $ra \in I$. Thus $I$ is closed under subtraction and under multiplication by arbitrary integers.

If $I = \{0\}$ take $n = 0$. Otherwise let $n$ be the least positive element of $I$, which exists by well-ordering since $I$ contains a nonzero element and is closed under negation. For any $a \in I$, divide: $a = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$; then $r = a - qn \in I$, so minimality forces $r = 0$ and $n \mid a$. Conversely every multiple of $n$ is in $I$. Hence $I = n\mathbb{Z}$.

Finally $a \sim b$ if and only if $a - b \sim 0$ — subtract $b \sim b$ from one side, add it back for the converse — if and only if $a - b \in I = n\mathbb{Z}$, which is $a \equiv b \pmod n$. Uniqueness holds because $n$ is recovered from $\sim$ as the least positive element of $I$.
:::

:::remark
The set $I$ built in that proof is exactly an *ideal*, and the correspondence "congruence relations $\leftrightarrow$ ideals" holds in every ring, not just $\mathbb{Z}$. What is special about $\mathbb{Z}$ is the division algorithm, which forced $I$ to be generated by one element.
:::

## Ideals, divisibility and containment

<!-- section: ideals-divisibility -->

:::definition[Ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$]{#ideal}
A subset $I \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ is an **ideal** if $0 \in I$, $I$ is closed under subtraction, and $ra \in I$ for every $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $a \in I$. The **principal ideal** generated by $n$ is $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
:::

:::theorem[$\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain]{#pid}
Every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ equals $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \ge 0$.
:::

:::proof
This is the middle paragraph of the proof of the theorem *Every congruence on $\mathbb{Z}$ is a congruence mod $n$*, which used only the three closure properties now taken as the definition: if $I = \{0\}$ take $n=0$, otherwise take $n$ least positive in $I$ and divide with remainder to show $I = n\mathbb{Z}$. Uniqueness: $n\mathbb{Z} = m\mathbb{Z}$ with $m, n \ge 0$ forces $m \mid n$ and $n \mid m$, hence $m = n$.
:::

:::proposition[Divisibility is containment]{#divides-contains}
For positive integers $m, n$:

$$m \mid n \iff n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq m\mathbb{Z} .$$

Moreover $\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z} = m\mathbb{Z} + n\mathbb{Z}$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\mathbb{Z} = m\mathbb{Z} \cap n\mathbb{Z}$.
:::

:::proof
If $m \mid n$ then every multiple of $n$ is a multiple of $m$, giving containment. Conversely if $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq m\mathbb{Z}$ then $n \in m\mathbb{Z}$, so $m \mid n$.

The set $m\mathbb{Z} + n\mathbb{Z} = \{mu + nv\}$ is an ideal, so by the theorem *$\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain* it equals $d\mathbb{Z}$ for some $d \ge 0$. Since $m$ and $n$ lie in it, $d$ divides both; and $d = mu+nv$ for some $u,v$, so every common divisor of $m$ and $n$ divides $d$. That is the defining property of the greatest common divisor. The intersection $m\mathbb{Z}\cap n\mathbb{Z}$ is likewise an ideal $\ell\mathbb{Z}$, and $\ell$ is a common multiple divided by every common multiple, i.e. the least one.
:::

:::remark
"To contain is to divide" inverts the intuition that a divisor is *smaller*. The bigger ideal belongs to the smaller number: $2\mathbb{Z} \supset 6\mathbb{Z}$ because $2 \mid 6$. Bézout's identity, the Chinese Remainder Theorem and the classification above are all statements about the lattice of these ideals.
:::

## Homomorphisms and induced maps

<!-- section: homomorphisms -->

:::theorem[First isomorphism theorem for $\mathbb{Z}$]{#first-iso}
The projection $\pi_n : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $n\mathbb{Z}$. Conversely, if $f : \mathbb{Z}\to R$ is a ring homomorphism whose kernel contains $n\mathbb{Z}$, then $f$ factors uniquely as $f = \bar f \circ \pi_n$ for a ring homomorphism $\bar f : \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to R$.
:::

:::proof
That $\pi_n$ is a homomorphism is the definition of the operations on classes; it is surjective because every class is $[a]$ for some $a$; and $\pi_n(a) = [0]$ means $n \mid a$, so the kernel is $n\mathbb{Z}$.

For the factorisation, there is no choice about $\bar f$: the requirement $f = \bar f \circ \pi_n$ forces $\bar f([a]) = f(a)$, which gives uniqueness. It is well defined: if $[a]=[b]$ then $a - b \in n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \ker f$, so $f(a) - f(b) = f(a-b) = 0$. And it is a homomorphism because $\bar f([a]+[b]) = \bar f([a+b]) = f(a+b) = f(a)+f(b)$, similarly for products, and $\bar f([1]) = f(1) = 1$.
:::

:::corollary[When reduction descends]{#reduction-descends}
For positive integers $m, n$ there is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ sending $[a]_n \mapsto [a]_m$ if and only if $m \mid n$, and it is then the unique ring homomorphism between them.
:::

:::proof
($\Leftarrow$) If $m \mid n$ then $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq m\mathbb{Z} = \ker \pi_m$ by the proposition *Divisibility is containment*, so the theorem *First isomorphism theorem for $\mathbb{Z}$* applies to $f = \pi_m$ and produces the map.

($\Rightarrow$) If such a map $g$ exists then $g([n]_n) = [n]_m$, but $[n]_n = [0]_n$ and $g([0]_n) = [0]_m$, so $m \mid n$. Uniqueness: any ring homomorphism out of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is determined by the image of $[1]$, which must be the identity, and $[a] = a\cdot[1]$.
:::

:::example[A congruence pulled back along reduction]
Given that $x \equiv 7 \pmod{60}$, what can be said about $x$ modulo $4$, and modulo $8$?
:::

:::solution
1. $4 \mid 60$, so the corollary *When reduction descends* gives a well-defined reduction: $x \equiv 7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
2. $8 \nmid 60$, so no such map exists and the class of $x$ mod $8$ is genuinely not determined: $x = 7$ gives $7 \pmod 8$, while $x = 67$ gives $3 \pmod 8$.
3. What *is* determined is $x$ modulo $\gcd(60, 8) = 4$ — consistent with step 1.
:::

## Quadratic residues

<!-- section: quadratic-residue-def -->

Linear congruences are settled: $ax \equiv b \pmod n$ is solved by the Euclidean algorithm. The quadratic case $x^2 \equiv a \pmod p$ is not settled by anything so mechanical, and deciding solvability without searching is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

:::definition[Quadratic residue]{#qr-def}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $p \nmid a$. Then $a$ is a **quadratic residue** modulo $p$ if $x^2 \equiv a \pmod p$ has a solution, and a **quadratic non-residue** otherwise. The set of residues is written $QR_p$.
:::

The class of $0$ is excluded by convention: $x^2 \equiv 0$ always has the single solution $x \equiv 0$, and including it would break every counting statement below.

:::theorem[Exactly half the nonzero residues are squares]{#half-are-residues}
Let $p$ be an odd prime. Of the $p-1$ nonzero classes mod $p$, exactly $\tfrac{p-1}{2}$ are quadratic residues and exactly $\tfrac{p-1}{2}$ are non-residues. The residues are listed without repetition by $1^2, 2^2, \dots, \big(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\big)^2$.
:::

:::proof
The squaring map $s : \mathbb{F}_p^\times \to \mathbb{F}_p^\times$, $s(x) = x^2$, has image $QR_p$ by definition. Each fibre has exactly two elements: if $s(x) = s(y)$ then $p \mid x^2 - y^2 = (x-y)(x+y)$, and since $\mathbb{F}_p$ is a field by the theorem *$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime*, $x \equiv y$ or $x \equiv -y$. These two possibilities are distinct because $x \equiv -x$ would give $p \mid 2x$, impossible for $p$ odd and $p \nmid x$.

So $s$ is two-to-one onto its image, and $|QR_p| = (p-1)/2$; the remaining $(p-1) - (p-1)/2 = (p-1)/2$ classes are non-residues. Since $k$ and $p-k$ have the same square, the values $1^2,\dots,((p-1)/2)^2$ already cover the image, and they are pairwise distinct because $1 \le j < k \le (p-1)/2$ gives $j \not\equiv \pm k$.
:::

:::example[The residues modulo $11$]
List the quadratic residues mod $11$ and decide whether $x^2 \equiv 5 \pmod{11}$ and $x^2 \equiv 6 \pmod{11}$ are solvable.
:::

:::solution
1. By the theorem *Exactly half the nonzero residues are squares* it suffices to square $1$ through $5$: $1, 4, 9, 16 \equiv 5, 25 \equiv 3$.
2. So $QR_{11} = \{1, 3, 4, 5, 9\}$, five of the ten nonzero classes, and the non-residues are $\{2, 6, 7, 8, 10\}$.
3. $5 \in QR_{11}$, so $x^2 \equiv 5$ is solvable, with $x \equiv \pm 4$.
4. $6 \notin QR_{11}$, so $x^2 \equiv 6$ has no solution.
:::

## Euler's criterion

<!-- section: euler-criterion -->

Listing squares decides membership in $QR_p$ in $O(p)$ work. Euler's criterion decides it in $O(\log p)$ multiplications, by turning a question about solvability into a single modular power.

:::theorem[Euler's criterion]{#euler-criterion-thm}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $p \nmid a$. Then

$$a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv \begin{cases} \;\; 1 \pmod p & \text{if } a \in QR_p,\\ -1 \pmod p & \text{if } a \notin QR_p.\end{cases}$$
:::

:::proof
First, $\big(a^{(p-1)/2}\big)^2 = a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ by Fermat's little theorem, so $p$ divides $(y-1)(y+1)$ where $y = a^{(p-1)/2}$. As $\mathbb{F}_p$ is a field, $y \equiv \pm 1$: the value is always one of the two.

If $a \in QR_p$, write $a \equiv x^2$ with $p \nmid x$. Then

$$a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv x^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p,$$

again by Fermat's little theorem. So every residue gives $+1$.

It remains to show no non-residue gives $+1$. The congruence $y^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1$ is a polynomial equation of degree $(p-1)/2$ over the field $\mathbb{F}_p$, so it has at most $(p-1)/2$ solutions. The theorem *Exactly half the nonzero residues are squares* supplies exactly $(p-1)/2$ solutions already, namely the quadratic residues. There is no room for another, so the non-residues must give $-1$.
:::

:::example[Euler's criterion on a larger prime]
Is $7$ a quadratic residue modulo the prime $p = 37$?
:::

:::solution
1. Compute $7^{18} \bmod 37$ by squaring: $7^2 = 49 \equiv 12$, $7^4 \equiv 12^2 = 144 \equiv 144 - 111 = 33 \equiv -4$.
2. $7^8 \equiv (-4)^2 = 16$, and $7^{16} \equiv 16^2 = 256 \equiv 256 - 222 = 34 \equiv -3$.
3. Then $7^{18} = 7^{16}\cdot 7^2 \equiv (-3)(12) = -36 \equiv 1 \pmod{37}$.
4. By the theorem *Euler's criterion*, $7$ is a quadratic residue mod $37$. Indeed $9^2 = 81 = 2\cdot 37 + 7$.
:::

## Counting solutions, and $-1$ as a residue

<!-- section: number-solutions-minus-one -->

:::proposition[A square congruence has $0$ or $2$ solutions]{#zero-or-two}
Let $p$ be an odd prime and $p \nmid a$. Then $x^2 \equiv a \pmod p$ has exactly two solutions if $a \in QR_p$ and none otherwise; the two solutions are $x_0$ and $p - x_0$.
:::

:::proof
If there is no solution the count is $0$ by definition. If $x_0$ is a solution then so is $-x_0$, and these are incongruent because $x_0 \equiv -x_0$ would give $p \mid 2x_0$, impossible. There are no others: $x^2 \equiv x_0^2$ gives $p \mid (x-x_0)(x+x_0)$, and $\mathbb{F}_p$ being a field forces $x \equiv \pm x_0$.
:::

:::theorem[The first supplement]{#first-supplement}
Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then $-1$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$ if and only if $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Equivalently,

$$(-1)^{(p-1)/2} = \begin{cases} 1 & p \equiv 1 \pmod 4,\\ -1 & p \equiv 3 \pmod 4.\end{cases}$$
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Euler's criterion* with $a = -1$, the class $-1$ is a quadratic residue exactly when $(-1)^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. Since $p$ is odd and $p > 2$, the integers $1$ and $-1$ are incongruent mod $p$, so the congruence holds exactly when the integer $(-1)^{(p-1)/2}$ equals $1$, i.e. when $(p-1)/2$ is even.

Write $p$ as $4k+1$ or $4k+3$, the only odd options. If $p = 4k+1$ then $(p-1)/2 = 2k$ is even; if $p = 4k+3$ then $(p-1)/2 = 2k+1$ is odd. So the condition is precisely $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
:::

:::corollary[Square roots of $-1$ modulo a prime]{#sqrt-minus-one}
For $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ the congruence $x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$ has exactly two solutions, and one of them is $\big(\tfrac{p-1}{2}\big)!$ modulo $p$. For $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ it has none.
:::

:::proof
The counts follow from the theorem *The first supplement* together with the proposition *A square congruence has $0$ or $2$ solutions*.

For the explicit root, put $m = (p-1)/2$ and pair each factor of $(p-1)!$ with its negative: $p - j \equiv -j$, so

$$(p-1)! = \prod_{j=1}^{m} j \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{m}(p-j) \equiv (m!)\cdot(-1)^m (m!) = (-1)^m (m!)^2 \pmod p .$$

Wilson's theorem states $(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$. When $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $m$ is even, so $(m!)^2 \equiv -1$.
:::

:::example[Building a square root of $-1$]
Find a solution of $x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{13}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $13 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so by the theorem *The first supplement* a solution exists.
2. The corollary *Square roots of $-1$ modulo a prime* offers $m! = 6! = 720$.
3. Reduce: $720 = 55 \cdot 13 + 5$, so $x \equiv 5$.
4. Check: $5^2 = 25 = 26 - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod{13}$. ✓ The other solution is $13 - 5 = 8$.
:::

:::remark
The first supplement is the engine of Fermat's two-square theorem: a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares, and the proof begins by choosing $u$ with $u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$ — which exists for exactly these primes. It also settles which primes divide some value of $x^2+1$, a question that looks unrelated to congruence classes until this theorem is available.
:::

## The Legendre symbol

<!-- section: legendre-def -->

Euler's criterion computes a value in $\{1, -1\}$ that records whether $a$ is a square. Packaging that value as a symbol turns quadratic-residue statements into algebraic identities.

:::definition[Legendre symbol]{#legendre}
Let $p$ be an odd prime. For an integer $a$, define

$$\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } p \mid a,\\ 1 & \text{if } a \in QR_p,\\ -1 & \text{if } a \notin QR_p.\end{cases}$$
:::

:::proposition[Euler's criterion in symbol form]{#legendre-euler}
For every integer $a$ and odd prime $p$,

$$\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) \equiv a^{(p-1)/2} \pmod p,$$

and the symbol depends only on $a \bmod p$.
:::

:::proof
If $p \mid a$ both sides are $\equiv 0$. Otherwise the congruence is the theorem *Euler's criterion*, read through the definition of the symbol. Dependence on $a \bmod p$ only: if $a \equiv a'$ then $a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv (a')^{(p-1)/2}$ by the corollary *Polynomials respect congruence*, and two values in $\{0, 1, -1\}$ that are congruent mod an odd prime $p \ge 3$ are equal.
:::

## Multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol

<!-- section: legendre-properties -->

:::theorem[The Legendre symbol is completely multiplicative]{#legendre-multiplicative}
For an odd prime $p$ and all integers $a, b$,

$$\left(\frac{ab}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\left(\frac{b}{p}\right).$$
:::

:::proof
By the proposition *Euler's criterion in symbol form*,

$$\left(\frac{ab}{p}\right) \equiv (ab)^{(p-1)/2} = a^{(p-1)/2}b^{(p-1)/2} \equiv \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\left(\frac{b}{p}\right) \pmod p .$$

Both sides lie in $\{0, 1, -1\}$, and distinct members of that set are incongruent modulo an odd prime $p \ge 3$ — the largest difference is $2 < 3 \le p$. So the congruence is an equality.
:::

:::corollary[Products of residues and non-residues]{#qr-product-rules}
For $p \nmid ab$: residue times residue is a residue, residue times non-residue is a non-residue, and non-residue times non-residue is a **residue**. Also $\left(\frac{a^2}{p}\right) = 1$ whenever $p \nmid a$.
:::

:::proof
Each statement is the multiplication table of $\{1,-1\}$ read through the theorem *The Legendre symbol is completely multiplicative*: $1\cdot 1 = 1$, $1 \cdot (-1) = -1$, $(-1)(-1)=1$. Taking $b = a$ gives $\left(\frac{a^2}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)^2 = 1$.
:::

:::pitfall
That two non-residues multiply to a residue is the standard surprise, and it kills the naive analogy with divisibility. It also means the Legendre symbol cannot be used to *find* a square root: knowing $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = 1$ tells you a root exists and nothing about where it is.
:::

:::example[Factoring a symbol]
Evaluate $\left(\frac{60}{13}\right)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Reduce first: $60 = 4\cdot 13 + 8$, so $\left(\frac{60}{13}\right) = \left(\frac{8}{13}\right)$ by the proposition *Euler's criterion in symbol form*.
2. Factor $8 = 4 \cdot 2$ and split by multiplicativity: $\left(\frac{8}{13}\right)=\left(\frac{4}{13}\right)\left(\frac{2}{13}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{13}\right)$, since $\left(\frac{4}{13}\right)=1$ by the corollary *Products of residues and non-residues*.
3. The squares mod $13$ are $\{1,3,4,9,10,12\}$, and $2$ is not among them, so $\left(\frac{2}{13}\right) = -1$.
4. Hence $\left(\frac{60}{13}\right) = -1$.
:::

## Quadratic reciprocity: a preview

<!-- section: legendre-reciprocity-preview -->

Multiplicativity reduces $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$ to the symbols of the prime factors of $a$. What it does not do is evaluate $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ for a prime $q$; the numerator and denominator are unrelated as far as anything proved so far can tell. Quadratic reciprocity asserts that they are not unrelated at all — it is proved in the following chapter, and stated here because every computation below uses it.

:::theorem[Law of quadratic reciprocity]{#reciprocity}
Let $p \neq q$ be odd primes. Then

$$\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}} .$$

Equivalently, $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ unless $p \equiv q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, in which case $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = -\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$. The two supplements are

$$\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}, \qquad \left(\frac{2}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p^2-1}{8}},$$

so $2$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$ exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$.
:::

The first supplement is already proved above as the theorem *The first supplement*; the second and the reciprocity law itself are not proved here.

:::recipe[Evaluating $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$ by reciprocity]{#reciprocity-recipe}
1. Reduce $a$ modulo $p$ to a least residue, using signed representatives if that shortens the factorisation.
2. Factor the numerator and split the symbol by multiplicativity.
3. Handle $-1$ and $2$ by the supplements; drop any square factor, whose symbol is $1$.
4. For an odd prime $q$ in the numerator, flip $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ to $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)$, inserting a sign $-1$ only when $p \equiv q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
5. Reduce $p$ modulo $q$ and recurse. The numbers shrink, so the process terminates.
:::

:::example[Reciprocity in action]
Evaluate $\left(\frac{43}{97}\right)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $97 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so no sign appears when flipping: $\left(\frac{43}{97}\right) = \left(\frac{97}{43}\right)$.
2. Reduce: $97 = 2\cdot 43 + 11$, so this is $\left(\frac{11}{43}\right)$.
3. Both $11$ and $43$ are $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so flipping costs a sign: $\left(\frac{11}{43}\right) = -\left(\frac{43}{11}\right) = -\left(\frac{10}{11}\right)$.
4. Split: $\left(\frac{10}{11}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{11}\right)\left(\frac{5}{11}\right)$. Now $11 \equiv 3 \pmod 8$ so $\left(\frac{2}{11}\right) = -1$; and $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $\left(\frac{5}{11}\right) = \left(\frac{11}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{5}\right) = 1$.
5. So $\left(\frac{10}{11}\right) = -1$ and $\left(\frac{43}{97}\right) = -(-1) = 1$: $43$ is a quadratic residue mod $97$.
:::

## The Jacobi symbol

<!-- section: jacobi-def -->

The recipe above has an ugly step: reducing $p$ modulo $q$ produces a composite numerator, which must be factored before reciprocity can be applied again, and factoring is expensive. The Jacobi symbol removes the need to factor by extending the definition to composite denominators.

:::definition[Jacobi symbol]{#jacobi}
Let $n$ be an odd positive integer with prime factorisation $n = p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_r^{e_r}$ (repetitions allowed in the exponents). For any integer $a$ define

$$\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = \prod_{i=1}^{r} \left(\frac{a}{p_i}\right)^{e_i},$$

a product of Legendre symbols. By convention $\left(\frac{a}{1}\right) = 1$.
:::

:::pitfall
When $n$ is prime the Jacobi symbol *is* the Legendre symbol, so the notation is consistent. But for composite $n$, $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = 1$ does **not** mean $a$ is a square mod $n$. Modulo $15$, $\left(\frac{2}{15}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\left(\frac{2}{5}\right) = (-1)(-1) = 1$, while the squares mod $15$ are $\{1,4,6,9,10\}$ and $2$ is not among them. Two non-residues cancelled. The implication that survives is one-directional: if $a$ is a square mod $n$ then $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = 1$, so the value $-1$ is a genuine proof of non-residuity.
:::

## Properties of the Jacobi symbol

<!-- section: jacobi-properties -->

Every formal rule of the Legendre symbol survives the extension — which is exactly why the extension is useful, since the rules can now be applied without factoring.

:::theorem[Jacobi symbol rules]{#jacobi-rules}
Let $m, n$ be odd positive integers and $a, b$ integers. Then

$$\left(\frac{ab}{n}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{n}\right)\left(\frac{b}{n}\right), \qquad \left(\frac{a}{mn}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{m}\right)\left(\frac{a}{n}\right),$$

the symbol depends only on $a \bmod n$, and $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = 0$ exactly when $\gcd(a,n) > 1$. Moreover the supplements and reciprocity hold verbatim:

$$\left(\frac{-1}{n}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}, \qquad \left(\frac{2}{n}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{n^2-1}{8}}, \qquad \left(\frac{m}{n}\right)\left(\frac{n}{m}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}\cdot\frac{n-1}{2}}$$

for $\gcd(m,n)=1$.
:::

:::proof
Multiplicativity in the numerator is the theorem *The Legendre symbol is completely multiplicative* applied factor by factor; multiplicativity in the denominator is immediate from the definition, since the prime factorisation of $mn$ is the concatenation of those of $m$ and $n$. Dependence on $a \bmod n$ follows because $p_i \mid n$, so $a \bmod n$ determines $a \bmod p_i$. The value is $0$ exactly when some $p_i \mid a$, i.e. when $\gcd(a,n)>1$.

For the supplements and reciprocity, the point is a parity computation. If $u, v$ are odd then

$$\frac{uv-1}{2} - \frac{u-1}{2} - \frac{v-1}{2} = \frac{(u-1)(v-1)}{2} \equiv 0 \pmod 2,$$

since $(u-1)(v-1)$ is divisible by $4$. So $u \mapsto \frac{u-1}{2} \bmod 2$ is additive over products of odd numbers; the same computation with $\frac{u^2-1}{8}$ shows it too is additive, using $8 \mid (u^2-1)(v^2-1)$. Writing $n = \prod p_i$ as a product of $\sum e_i$ primes with repetition, $\left(\frac{-1}{n}\right) = \prod (-1)^{(p_i-1)/2} = (-1)^{\sum (p_i-1)/2} = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ by additivity, and likewise for $2$. Reciprocity follows by applying the Legendre law to every pair $(p_i, q_j)$ of primes from $m$ and $n$ and collecting the exponent $\sum_{i,j}\frac{p_i-1}{2}\frac{q_j-1}{2} \equiv \frac{m-1}{2}\cdot\frac{n-1}{2} \pmod 2$ by the same additivity.
:::

:::example[A Jacobi computation with no factoring]
Evaluate $\left(\frac{1001}{9907}\right)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $9907 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ and $1001 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so flipping is free: $\left(\frac{1001}{9907}\right) = \left(\frac{9907}{1001}\right)$.
2. Reduce: $9907 = 9\cdot 1001 + 898$, giving $\left(\frac{898}{1001}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{1001}\right)\left(\frac{449}{1001}\right)$.
3. $1001 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so $\left(\frac{2}{1001}\right) = 1$.
4. $449 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so flip freely: $\left(\frac{449}{1001}\right) = \left(\frac{1001}{449}\right) = \left(\frac{103}{449}\right)$, using $1001 = 2\cdot 449 + 103$.
5. $449 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $\left(\frac{103}{449}\right) = \left(\frac{449}{103}\right) = \left(\frac{37}{103}\right)$; then $37 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ gives $\left(\frac{103}{37}\right) = \left(\frac{29}{37}\right) = \left(\frac{37}{29}\right) = \left(\frac{8}{29}\right)$.
6. $\left(\frac{8}{29}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{29}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{2}{29}\right)$, and $29 \equiv 5 \pmod 8$ so this is $-1$.
7. Hence $\left(\frac{1001}{9907}\right) = -1$. At no point was any number factored.
:::

## Jacobi symbols and primality testing

<!-- section: jacobi-primality -->

The Jacobi symbol is computable in $O(\log^2 n)$ bit operations by the recipe above, without factoring $n$. Euler's criterion says that for a *prime* $n$ the symbol agrees with a modular power. Comparing the two quantities therefore tests primality.

:::definition[Euler witness and Euler pseudoprime]{#euler-witness}
Let $n$ be an odd composite number and $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Then $a$ is an **Euler witness** for $n$ if

$$a^{(n-1)/2} \not\equiv \left(\frac{a}{n}\right) \pmod n .$$

If instead the congruence holds, $n$ is an **Euler pseudoprime to base $a$** and $a$ is a liar.
:::

:::proposition[The test is sound]{#solovay-sound}
If $n$ is an odd prime then $a^{(n-1)/2} \equiv \left(\frac{a}{n}\right) \pmod n$ for every $a$. Hence an Euler witness proves $n$ composite.
:::

:::proof
For prime $n$ the Jacobi symbol is the Legendre symbol, and the congruence is the proposition *Euler's criterion in symbol form*. The contrapositive is the second sentence: if some $a$ fails the congruence, $n$ cannot be prime.
:::

:::theorem[At least half the bases are witnesses]{#solovay-strassen}
Let $n$ be odd and composite. Then the set of liars

$$L = \Big\{\, a \in (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times : a^{(n-1)/2} \equiv \left(\tfrac{a}{n}\right) \pmod n \,\Big\}$$

is a proper subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$. Consequently $|L| \le \varphi(n)/2$, so a base chosen uniformly at random from $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ is an Euler witness with probability at least $1/2$.
:::

:::proof
$L$ is a subgroup: the map $a \mapsto a^{(n-1)/2}\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)^{-1}$ is a homomorphism $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times \to (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, because both $a \mapsto a^{(n-1)/2}$ and the Jacobi symbol are multiplicative by the theorem *Jacobi symbol rules*, and $L$ is exactly its kernel.

That $L$ is *proper* is the substantive half, and we show it in the two shapes a composite $n$ can take. If $n$ is divisible by the square of a prime, say $p^2 \mid n$, take $a = 1 + n/p$; then $a^{(n-1)/2}$ has order divisible by $p$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ while $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = \pm 1$ has order dividing $2$, and $p$ is odd, so $a \notin L$. Otherwise $n = p\,m$ with $p$ prime, $m > 1$ and $\gcd(p,m)=1$; choose a non-residue $c$ mod $p$ and, by the theorem *Chinese Remainder Theorem*, an $a$ with $a \equiv c \pmod p$ and $a \equiv 1 \pmod m$. Then $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = \left(\frac{c}{p}\right)\left(\frac{1}{m}\right) = -1$, whereas $a^{(n-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod m$, so $a^{(n-1)/2} \equiv -1 \pmod n$ is impossible. Hence $a \notin L$.

A proper subgroup has index at least $2$ by Lagrange's theorem, so $|L| \le \varphi(n)/2 \le (n-1)/2$, and the bases in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ that are not in $L$ — including every $a$ with $\gcd(a,n)>1$, which fail immediately — number at least $(n-1)/2$.
:::

This is the Solovay–Strassen primality test: pick $k$ random bases, declare $n$ composite the moment a witness appears, and otherwise declare it prime with error probability at most $2^{-k}$. Unlike the Fermat test, it has no analogue of the Carmichael numbers — there is no composite $n$ for which every coprime base lies — and that is exactly what the theorem above rules out.

:::example[Detecting a composite with one base]
Use the base $a = 2$ to show $n = 561$ is composite, given that $561$ passes the Fermat test to base $2$.
:::

:::solution
1. $561 = 3 \cdot 11 \cdot 17$, and indeed $2^{560}\equiv 1 \pmod{561}$, so the Fermat test learns nothing: $561$ is a Carmichael number.
2. Jacobi: $\left(\frac{2}{561}\right) = (-1)^{(561^2-1)/8}$. Since $561 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, this is $+1$.
3. Modular power: $2^{280} \bmod 561$. Squaring gives $2^{10}\equiv 463$, $2^{20}\equiv 463^2 \equiv 67$, $2^{40}\equiv 67^2 \equiv 1$, so the order of $2$ divides $40$; then $280 = 7\cdot 40$ gives $2^{280}\equiv 1^{7} = 1$.
4. Here the two agree, so $2$ is a liar. Try $a = 5$: $\left(\frac{5}{561}\right) = \left(\frac{5}{3}\right)\left(\frac{5}{11}\right)\left(\frac{5}{17}\right) = (-1)(1)(-1) = 1$, while $5^{280} \equiv 67 \pmod{561}$.
5. Since $67 \not\equiv 1$, the base $5$ is an Euler witness and $561$ is composite. The theorem *At least half the bases are witnesses* guarantees such a base is easy to find — unlike for the Fermat test, where for $561$ almost none exists.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Cancelling a factor without touching the modulus.** From $ca \equiv cb \pmod n$ one may only conclude $a \equiv b \pmod{n/\gcd(c,n)}$. Cancelling $2$ from $6 \equiv 16 \pmod{10}$ gives the false $3 \equiv 8 \pmod{10}$; the true conclusion is $3 \equiv 8 \pmod 5$.
- **Inverting when no inverse exists.** Writing $x \equiv b/a$ presumes $\gcd(a,n) = 1$. When $d = \gcd(a,n) > 1$ the congruence $ax\equiv b$ has $d$ solutions or none, and the theorem *Linear congruence* is the correct tool.
- **Reducing an exponent modulo $n$.** Exponents reduce modulo $\varphi(n)$ — or modulo the order of the base — never modulo $n$, and only when the base is coprime to $n$. $2^{12} \bmod 10$ is not $2^{12 \bmod 10}$.
- **Applying the CRT without checking coprimality.** The clean statement needs pairwise coprime moduli. Otherwise use the corollary *General systems*: the system is solvable exactly when $\gcd(n_i,n_j) \mid a_i - a_j$ for every pair, and checking only consecutive pairs is not enough.
- **Expecting two square roots modulo a composite.** $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{105}$ has eight solutions, one for each choice of sign in each of the three CRT coordinates. Only modulo a prime is the count $0$ or $2$.
- **Reading a Jacobi symbol as a residue test.** $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = 1$ for composite $n$ does not make $a$ a square mod $n$ — two hidden non-residues can cancel, as with $\left(\frac{2}{15}\right)=1$. Only the value $-1$ is conclusive.
- **Forgetting that a symbol depends on the numerator modulo $p$, not on its size.** Always reduce first; $\left(\frac{60}{13}\right)$ is a symbol about $8$, and signed residues often shorten the work further.
- **Confusing $a \bmod n$ with $a \equiv b \pmod n$.** The first is a number, the least nonnegative remainder; the second is a relation between two numbers. Writing "$\equiv$" between an integer and a remainder computation is harmless, but writing "$=$" where the classes only agree is not.
