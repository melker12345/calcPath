# Arithmetic Functions

An **arithmetic function** is nothing more than a function $f : \mathbb{Z}^{+} \to \mathbb{C}$. Stated that baldly the definition promises nothing: every sequence is an arithmetic function, and there is no theory of arbitrary sequences. What makes the subject is a single structural observation — the positive integers are not merely ordered, they *factor* — and the functions worth studying are the ones that notice.

The chapter is organised around that observation and around one operation. The operation is **Dirichlet convolution**,

$$(f * g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)\, g(n/d),$$

which sums over the factorisations of $n$ rather than over its predecessors. Under it the arithmetic functions form a commutative ring with identity, invertible elements and all; the multiplicative functions form a group inside that ring; and almost every classical identity in the subject — Euler's $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d) = n$, Möbius inversion, the formula $\sigma_k = \mathrm{id}^k * u$ — is a single line of algebra in that ring once the ring is built. Building it is therefore the first order of business, and the two facts that do the work, associativity and the multiplicativity of a convolution of multiplicative functions, are proved in full.

With the algebra in place the classical functions follow quickly: the totient $\varphi$, the Möbius function $\mu$, the divisor functions $\sigma_k$, the Carmichael function $\lambda$. Each is introduced by what it counts, then computed from the prime factorisation, then related to the others by a convolution identity. Two named theorems get complete proofs — the Euclid–Euler classification of even perfect numbers, and Korselt's criterion for Carmichael numbers — because both are cases where a purely elementary argument settles a question that looks as though it should need machinery.

The last lesson is about size rather than structure. The functions here oscillate violently: $\varphi(n)/n$ comes arbitrarily close to $0$ and infinitely often equals $1 - 1/n$; $d(n)$ is $2$ infinitely often and unbounded. What is stable is their *average* behaviour, and Mertens' three theorems are the sharpest elementary statements about how the primes distribute themselves in that average. Two of the three are proved here from Chebyshev-type estimates; the third needs the prime number theorem for its constant, and where that happens the argument is explicitly labelled a sketch.

Throughout, $p$ always denotes a prime, $d \mid n$ means $d$ runs over the *positive* divisors of $n$, and $n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$ is the prime factorisation of $n > 1$ with the $p_i$ distinct and $a_i \ge 1$. Unique factorisation is assumed throughout, as developed in *Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic*.

## Definitions and basic classes

<!-- section: arithmetic-definitions -->

A warning about terminology before anything else. In number theory "additive" and "multiplicative" do **not** describe behaviour under $m + n$. Both describe behaviour under *multiplication*, and they differ only in whether the values are combined by $+$ or by $\times$. The convention is historical and unhelpful, and it is universal.

:::definition[Arithmetic function]{#arithmetic-function}
An **arithmetic function** is a function $f : \mathbb{Z}^{+} \to \mathbb{C}$. Two arithmetic functions are equal when they agree at every positive integer.
:::

:::definition[Additive and multiplicative]{#arithmetic-classes}
An arithmetic function $f$ is

- **additive** if $f(mn) = f(m) + f(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m,n) = 1$;
- **completely additive** if $f(mn) = f(m) + f(n)$ for all $m, n$;
- **multiplicative** if $f$ is not identically zero and $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m,n) = 1$;
- **completely multiplicative** if $f$ is not identically zero and $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ for all $m,n$.
:::

The coprimality hypothesis is the whole point. It says the value of $f$ at $n$ is determined by its values at the prime powers $p_i^{a_i}$, because those are pairwise coprime and multiply to $n$. A multiplicative function is thus a function on prime powers that has been extended to all integers in the only consistent way, and specifying one means specifying a doubly-indexed table of numbers $f(p^a)$ and nothing else.

:::proposition[Normalisation at $1$]{#normalisation}
If $f$ is multiplicative then $f(1) = 1$. If $f$ is additive then $f(1) = 0$.
:::

:::proof
Since $\gcd(1,1) = 1$, multiplicativity gives $f(1) = f(1 \cdot 1) = f(1)^2$, so $f(1) \in \{0, 1\}$. If $f(1) = 0$ then for every $n$, $\gcd(n,1) = 1$ gives $f(n) = f(n)f(1) = 0$, so $f$ is identically zero, which the definition excludes. Hence $f(1) = 1$. In the additive case the same computation reads $f(1) = 2f(1)$, so $f(1) = 0$; no exclusion is needed because the zero function is additive and harmless.
:::

:::theorem[A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers]{#determined-by-prime-powers}
Let $f$ be multiplicative and let $n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$. Then

$$f(n) = \prod_{i=1}^{r} f\!\left(p_i^{a_i}\right).$$

Conversely, given any values $c_{p,a} \in \mathbb{C}$ indexed by primes $p$ and exponents $a \ge 1$, the formula $f(n) = \prod_i c_{p_i, a_i}$, together with $f(1) = 1$, defines a multiplicative function.
:::

:::proof
For the first claim, induct on $r$. When $r = 0$ the empty product is $1 = f(1)$ by *Normalisation at $1$*, and $r = 1$ is trivial. For $r > 1$ put $m = p_1^{a_1}$ and $n' = p_2^{a_2}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$. No prime divides both $m$ and $n'$, so $\gcd(m, n') = 1$ and multiplicativity gives $f(n) = f(m)f(n')$; the inductive hypothesis expands $f(n')$.

For the converse, let $\gcd(m,n) = 1$. No prime occurs in both factorisations, so the factorisation of $mn$ is the concatenation of those of $m$ and $n$ with the exponents carried over unchanged. The product defining $f(mn)$ therefore splits as the product for $f(m)$ times the product for $f(n)$. Finally $f(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so $f$ is not identically zero.
:::

The converse half is what licenses the standard style of proof in this chapter: to prove two multiplicative functions equal, check them on prime powers. Everything else is bookkeeping done once and for all.

:::example[Recognising the classes]
Classify $\omega(n)$ (the number of distinct primes dividing $n$), $\Omega(n)$ (the number of prime factors counted with multiplicity), $\mathrm{id}(n) = n$, and $d(n)$ (the number of positive divisors).
:::

:::solution
1. $\omega$ is additive: for coprime $m, n$ the prime sets are disjoint, so $\omega(mn) = \omega(m) + \omega(n)$. It is not completely additive, since $\omega(4) = 1$ but $\omega(2) + \omega(2) = 2$.
2. $\Omega$ is completely additive: exponents simply add when any two integers are multiplied, coprime or not.
3. $\mathrm{id}$ is completely multiplicative, since $mn = mn$ with no hypothesis at all.
4. $d$ is multiplicative — this is proved below as *Divisors of a coprime product* — but not completely multiplicative: $d(4) = 3$ while $d(2)d(2) = 4$.
:::

:::proposition[Divisors of a coprime product]{#divisor-bijection}
If $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ then every divisor $e$ of $mn$ factors uniquely as $e = e_1e_2$ with $e_1 \mid m$ and $e_2 \mid n$, and the map $(e_1, e_2) \mapsto e_1e_2$ is a bijection from divisors of $m$ paired with divisors of $n$ onto the divisors of $mn$. Consequently $d$ is multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
Set $e_1 = \gcd(e, m)$ and $e_2 = \gcd(e, n)$. Since $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ we have $\gcd(e_1, e_2) = 1$, and both divide $e$, so $e_1e_2 \mid e$. Conversely write $e = \prod p^{c_p}$ with $c_p \le a_p + b_p$ where $p^{a_p} \| m$ and $p^{b_p} \| n$; coprimality forces $a_p = 0$ or $b_p = 0$ for each $p$, so $c_p \le a_p$ or $c_p \le b_p$, meaning each prime power of $e$ lands entirely in $e_1$ or entirely in $e_2$. Hence $e \mid e_1e_2$ and so $e = e_1e_2$.

The map $(e_1, e_2) \mapsto e_1e_2$ is injective because $e_1 = \gcd(e_1e_2, m)$ recovers $e_1$ from the product, again using $\gcd(e_2, m) = 1$; and it is surjective by the previous paragraph. Counting both sides gives $d(mn) = d(m)d(n)$, and $d(1) = 1$, so $d$ is multiplicative.
:::

:::remark
The bijection above is the reason so many proofs in this chapter look the same. Whenever a quantity is defined as a sum over divisors of $n$, splitting $n$ into coprime parts splits the sum into a product of two sums, because the index set itself splits as a product. That single observation is upgraded into the theorem *Convolution preserves multiplicativity* later in the chapter.
:::

:::definition[The basic functions]{#basic-functions}
Four arithmetic functions recur constantly:

$$\varepsilon(n) = \begin{cases} 1 & n = 1, \\ 0 & n > 1,\end{cases} \qquad u(n) = 1, \qquad \mathrm{id}_k(n) = n^k, \qquad \mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id}_1 .$$

The function $\varepsilon$ is the **delta function** (or unit function); $u$ is the **constant one**.
:::

All four are completely multiplicative. For $\varepsilon$ this is a small surprise worth checking: $\varepsilon(mn) = \varepsilon(m)\varepsilon(n)$ holds for all $m,n$ because both sides are $1$ when $m = n = 1$ and $0$ otherwise. The property that actually matters about $\varepsilon$, however, is not its multiplicativity but the fact — proved in the lesson *Dirichlet convolution* — that it is the identity element for convolution.

## Values, order of magnitude, and the ring structure

<!-- section: arithmetic-ring-ops -->

Before convolution there are the two obvious operations. Arithmetic functions can be added and multiplied pointwise, since their values are complex numbers, and under those operations they form a commutative ring — a completely uninteresting one, isomorphic to a countable product of copies of $\mathbb{C}$, riddled with zero divisors and carrying no arithmetic information whatever. It is worth stating precisely once, because the contrast with the Dirichlet ring is the point.

:::proposition[The pointwise ring]{#pointwise-ring}
Under $(f+g)(n) = f(n) + g(n)$ and $(f \cdot g)(n) = f(n)g(n)$, the set $\mathcal{A}$ of arithmetic functions is a commutative ring with identity $u$. Its zero divisors are exactly the functions vanishing somewhere without vanishing identically, and its units are exactly the nowhere-zero functions.
:::

:::proof
Every axiom is checked at each $n$ separately, where it is an axiom of $\mathbb{C}$; the identity is the function constantly $1$, namely $u$. If $f$ vanishes at some $m$ and $g$ is any function supported at $m$ alone, then $fg = 0$ while neither factor need be zero, giving a zero divisor; and if $f$ is nowhere zero then $n \mapsto f(n)^{-1}$ is a pointwise inverse. Conversely a unit cannot vanish anywhere, since $f(n)g(n) = 1$ forces $f(n) \neq 0$.
:::

:::remark
Pointwise multiplication destroys multiplicativity in no way — a pointwise product of multiplicative functions is multiplicative — but it is useless for identities like $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d) = n$, which relate a function's values at $n$ to its values at the divisors of $n$. Nothing in the pointwise ring looks at divisors. Dirichlet convolution is the operation that does, and it is introduced in the lesson *Dirichlet convolution*.
:::

Two more operations appear often enough to be named: the **summatory function** and the **Dirichlet series**, treated in the next section. The summatory function is the bridge to analysis.

:::definition[Summatory function]{#summatory-function}
The **summatory function** of $f$ is

$$F(x) = \sum_{n \le x} f(n), \qquad x \ge 1,$$

the sum running over positive integers $n$ not exceeding $x$.
:::

Individual values of a multiplicative function jump around: $d(n)$ is $2$ at every prime and exceeds $100$ infinitely often, and no asymptotic formula for $d(n)$ itself can exist. The summatory function smooths that out, and it is where all the asymptotic statements of the subject live. The basic tool for computing one is the exchange of order in a double sum, which deserves to be stated as a lemma because every later estimate uses it.

:::lemma[Dirichlet hyperbola exchange]{#hyperbola-exchange}
For any arithmetic function $f$ and any $x \ge 1$,

$$\sum_{n \le x} \sum_{d \mid n} f(d) = \sum_{d \le x} f(d) \left\lfloor \frac{x}{d} \right\rfloor .$$
:::

:::proof
Both sides count the same set of pairs $(d, q)$ of positive integers with $dq \le x$, weighted by $f(d)$. On the left the outer index is $n = dq$ and the inner index is $d \mid n$; on the right the outer index is $d$ and the inner count is the number of $q$ with $q \le x/d$, which is $\lfloor x/d\rfloor$. Since $f(d)$ does not depend on $q$, the inner sum on the right is $f(d)$ times that count.
:::

:::example[The average number of divisors]
Use the exchange lemma to show $\sum_{n \le x} d(n) = x\ln x + O(x)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Since $d(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} 1$, the lemma with $f = u$ gives $\sum_{n\le x} d(n) = \sum_{d \le x}\lfloor x/d\rfloor$.
2. Write $\lfloor x/d \rfloor = x/d + O(1)$; the error terms contribute $O(x)$ in total, since there are at most $x$ of them.
3. The main term is $x\sum_{d \le x} 1/d = x(\ln x + O(1))$, using the harmonic sum estimate.
4. Combining, $\sum_{n\le x} d(n) = x\ln x + O(x)$, so the *average* number of divisors of an integer near $x$ is about $\ln x$ even though no individual value is predictable.
:::

:::remark
The error term here can be improved a great deal — the hyperbola method gives $x\ln x + (2\gamma - 1)x + O(\sqrt{x})$, and the true exponent in the error is the subject of the unsolved Dirichlet divisor problem. The crude version above is enough for everything in this chapter.
:::

## Generating functions and Dirichlet series

<!-- section: arithmetic-generating -->

An arithmetic function has two natural generating objects. The ordinary power series $\sum f(n)x^n$ is the right tool for additive structure — it turns $n \mapsto n+1$ into multiplication by $x$ — and is almost useless here. The right object is the **Dirichlet series**, which turns the multiplicative structure into an Euler product.

:::definition[Dirichlet series]{#dirichlet-series}
The **Dirichlet series** of an arithmetic function $f$ is the formal series

$$D_f(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{f(n)}{n^{s}} .$$

When $f = u$ this is the **Riemann zeta function** $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n\ge 1} n^{-s}$, convergent for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$.
:::

The reason this is the right transform is one line of algebra: $n^{-s}m^{-s} = (nm)^{-s}$. Multiplying two Dirichlet series therefore collects terms according to *factorisations*, exactly the way convolution does.

:::theorem[Dirichlet series multiply by convolution]{#series-convolution}
For arithmetic functions $f, g$,

$$D_f(s)\, D_g(s) = D_{f*g}(s), \qquad (f*g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)g(n/d),$$

as an identity of formal Dirichlet series, and as an identity of functions wherever both series converge absolutely.
:::

:::proof
Formally, multiply out:

$$\left(\sum_{a \ge 1}\frac{f(a)}{a^s}\right)\left(\sum_{b\ge 1}\frac{g(b)}{b^s}\right) = \sum_{a,b \ge 1} \frac{f(a)g(b)}{(ab)^s} = \sum_{n \ge 1} \frac{1}{n^s}\sum_{ab = n} f(a)g(b),$$

and the inner sum is $\sum_{d \mid n} f(d)g(n/d) = (f*g)(n)$, since the pairs $(a,b)$ with $ab = n$ are exactly the pairs $(d, n/d)$ with $d \mid n$. When both series converge absolutely at $s$, the double series converges absolutely, so the rearrangement collecting terms with $ab = n$ is legitimate by Fubini's theorem for series, and the identity holds as one between complex numbers.
:::

This theorem is the dictionary for the rest of the chapter. Every convolution identity proved later has a Dirichlet series shadow, and it is usually the shadow one remembers: $\sigma_k = \mathrm{id}_k * u$ becomes $\sum \sigma_k(n)n^{-s} = \zeta(s)\zeta(s-k)$, and the Möbius identity $\mu * u = \varepsilon$ becomes $\sum\mu(n)n^{-s} = 1/\zeta(s)$.

:::theorem[Euler product]{#euler-product}
Let $f$ be multiplicative with $\sum_n |f(n)|n^{-\sigma} < \infty$. Then

$$D_f(s) = \prod_{p} \left( 1 + \frac{f(p)}{p^{s}} + \frac{f(p^2)}{p^{2s}} + \cdots \right)$$

for $\mathrm{Re}(s) = \sigma$, the product over all primes. If $f$ is completely multiplicative the inner series is geometric and

$$D_f(s) = \prod_p \left(1 - \frac{f(p)}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1} .$$
:::

:::proof
Let $P$ be a finite set of primes and expand the finite product $\prod_{p \in P}\sum_{a \ge 0} f(p^a)p^{-as}$. By absolute convergence the product of the inner series may be expanded term by term, and a general term is $\prod_{p\in P} f(p^{a_p}) p^{-a_p s} = f(n)n^{-s}$ where $n = \prod_{p\in P}p^{a_p}$, the equality of coefficients being *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers*. Distinct exponent vectors give distinct $n$ by unique factorisation, so the finite product equals $\sum_{n \in S(P)} f(n)n^{-s}$ where $S(P)$ is the set of integers all of whose prime factors lie in $P$.

Now take $P = \{p : p \le N\}$. Every $n \le N$ lies in $S(P)$, so the difference between $D_f(s)$ and the finite product is bounded by $\sum_{n > N}|f(n)|n^{-\sigma}$, the tail of a convergent series, which tends to $0$. Hence the products converge to $D_f(s)$. For completely multiplicative $f$, $f(p^a) = f(p)^a$ and $\sum_a (f(p)p^{-s})^a = (1 - f(p)p^{-s})^{-1}$, the ratio having modulus $|f(p)|p^{-\sigma} < 1$ because the single term $n = p$ of a convergent series of non-negative terms is less than $1$ for all but finitely many $p$, and for the remaining finitely many the hypothesis of absolute convergence forces it too.
:::

:::corollary[Euler's product for zeta]{#zeta-euler}
For $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$,

$$\zeta(s) = \prod_p \left(1 - p^{-s}\right)^{-1} .$$

In particular $\zeta(s) \neq 0$ in that half-plane.
:::

:::proof
Apply *Euler product* to the completely multiplicative function $u$, for which $\sum_n n^{-\sigma}$ converges when $\sigma > 1$. Non-vanishing follows because a convergent infinite product of nonzero factors is nonzero: each factor $(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ is a nonzero complex number, and the partial products converge to $\zeta(s)$ while their reciprocals $\prod_{p \le N}(1-p^{-s})$ converge to a finite limit, so $\zeta(s)$ cannot be $0$.
:::

:::intuition
The Euler product is unique factorisation, written as an equation between infinite sums. Choosing a term from each bracket is choosing an exponent for each prime; multiplying them out and collecting is exactly the statement that each integer arises from exactly one such choice.
:::

:::example[The Dirichlet series of $d$ and of $\sigma$]
Write $\sum_{n\ge1} d(n)n^{-s}$ and $\sum_{n \ge 1}\sigma(n)n^{-s}$ in terms of $\zeta$.
:::

:::solution
1. $d = u * u$, since $\sum_{d\mid n} 1 \cdot 1 = d(n)$. By *Dirichlet series multiply by convolution*, $\sum d(n)n^{-s} = \zeta(s)^2$, valid for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$.
2. $\sigma = \mathrm{id} * u$, since $\sum_{d\mid n} d = \sigma(n)$. The Dirichlet series of $\mathrm{id}$ is $\sum n\cdot n^{-s} = \sum n^{-(s-1)} = \zeta(s-1)$.
3. Hence $\sum\sigma(n)n^{-s} = \zeta(s)\zeta(s-1)$, valid for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 2$ so that both factors converge absolutely.
:::

## Multiplicative functions in practice

<!-- section: multiplicative-def -->

The definition of multiplicativity was given in *Additive and multiplicative*, and the theorem *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers* reduced every multiplicative function to a table of values $f(p^a)$. This section is about using that reduction: how to decide whether a given function is multiplicative, and how to read off its values once it is.

The practical test has two halves. To prove multiplicativity, exhibit the function as a count of something that splits across coprime parts, or as a convolution of functions already known to be multiplicative. To disprove it, one counterexample suffices — and the cheapest counterexample is almost always a comparison of $f(p^2)$ against $f(p)^2$, which tests *complete* multiplicativity, or of $f(6)$ against $f(2)f(3)$, which tests the real thing.

:::example[Which of these are multiplicative?]
Decide whether each of $f(n) = n + 1$, $g(n) = 2^{\omega(n)}$, $h(n) = \gcd(n, 12)$ is multiplicative.
:::

:::solution
1. $f(n) = n+1$: $f(1) = 2 \neq 1$, so it fails *Normalisation at $1$* immediately and is not multiplicative.
2. $g(n) = 2^{\omega(n)}$: $\omega$ is additive, so for coprime $m,n$, $g(mn) = 2^{\omega(m)+\omega(n)} = g(m)g(n)$, and $g(1) = 2^0 = 1$. Multiplicative. It is not completely multiplicative: $g(4) = 2$ but $g(2)^2 = 4$.
3. $h(n) = \gcd(n,12)$: take $m = 4$, $n = 3$. Then $h(12) = 12$ while $h(4)h(3) = 4 \cdot 3 = 12$. That case works; in fact $\gcd(n, N)$ is multiplicative in $n$ for any fixed $N$, because $\gcd(mn, N) = \gcd(m,N)\gcd(n,N)$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$, each prime power of $N$ being captured by exactly one of $m$, $n$.
:::

:::proposition[Exponentiating an additive function]{#exp-additive}
If $g$ is additive then $f(n) = c^{g(n)}$ is multiplicative for any constant $c \neq 0$; if $g$ is completely additive then $f$ is completely multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
For coprime $m,n$, $f(mn) = c^{g(mn)} = c^{g(m)+g(n)} = c^{g(m)}c^{g(n)} = f(m)f(n)$, and $f(1) = c^{g(1)} = c^0 = 1$ by *Normalisation at $1$*, so $f$ is not identically zero. Dropping the coprimality hypothesis on $g$ drops it on $f$, giving the second claim.
:::

:::corollary[The Liouville function]{#liouville}
The **Liouville function** $\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(n) = (-1)^{\Omega(n)}$ is completely multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
$\Omega$ is completely additive, as observed in *Recognising the classes*, so *Exponentiating an additive function* applies with $c = -1$.
:::

:::pitfall
A multiplicative function need not be positive, need not be monotone, and need not be determined by finitely much data. The one thing it *is* determined by is its table on prime powers — and two multiplicative functions agreeing on all primes may still differ, because they can differ at $p^2$. Checking an identity at primes only is the most common error in the subject.
:::

:::proposition[Products and quotients]{#products-quotients}
If $f$ and $g$ are multiplicative then so is the pointwise product $fg$, and so is $f/g$ provided $g$ is nowhere zero. The pointwise *sum* $f+g$ is in general not multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
For coprime $m,n$: $(fg)(mn) = f(mn)g(mn) = f(m)g(m)f(n)g(n) = (fg)(m)(fg)(n)$, and $(fg)(1) = 1$. The same computation with $g(mn)^{-1}$ gives the quotient. For the last claim, $(f+g)(1) = f(1)+g(1) = 2 \neq 1$, so a sum of two multiplicative functions is never multiplicative.
:::

:::example[Reading values off the table]
Let $f$ be multiplicative with $f(p^a) = a + 1$ for every prime $p$ and every $a \ge 1$. Compute $f(360)$ and identify $f$.
:::

:::solution
1. $360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$.
2. By *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers*, $f(360) = f(2^3)f(3^2)f(5) = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$.
3. The number of divisors of $p^a$ is $a+1$, so $f$ agrees with $d$ on all prime powers, and two multiplicative functions agreeing on prime powers are equal. Hence $f = d$, and indeed $d(360) = 24$.
:::

## Convolution preserves multiplicativity

<!-- section: multiplicative-conv-preserve -->

This is the engine of the chapter. Every classical multiplicative function below is built by convolving simpler ones, and the reason each is multiplicative is the single theorem proved here. It deserves a careful proof because the two hypotheses — that $f$ and $g$ are multiplicative, and that the divisors of a coprime product factor — enter at different points, and seeing where is what makes the result usable.

:::theorem[Convolution preserves multiplicativity]{#conv-preserves-mult}
If $f$ and $g$ are multiplicative, then so is $f * g$, where

$$(f*g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)\,g(n/d).$$
:::

:::proof
First, $(f*g)(1) = f(1)g(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so $f*g$ is not identically zero.

Now let $\gcd(m,n) = 1$. By *Divisors of a coprime product*, every divisor $e$ of $mn$ is uniquely $e = e_1e_2$ with $e_1 \mid m$, $e_2 \mid n$, and every such pair arises. Reindexing the sum over $e \mid mn$ as a double sum over the pairs,

$$(f*g)(mn) = \sum_{e_1 \mid m}\ \sum_{e_2 \mid n} f(e_1e_2)\, g\!\left(\frac{mn}{e_1e_2}\right).$$

Two coprimality facts now apply. Since $e_1 \mid m$ and $e_2 \mid n$ and $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, we have $\gcd(e_1, e_2) = 1$, so $f(e_1e_2) = f(e_1)f(e_2)$. Likewise $m/e_1$ divides $m$ and $n/e_2$ divides $n$, so $\gcd(m/e_1,\, n/e_2) = 1$ and

$$g\!\left(\frac{mn}{e_1e_2}\right) = g\!\left(\frac{m}{e_1}\cdot\frac{n}{e_2}\right) = g\!\left(\frac{m}{e_1}\right)g\!\left(\frac{n}{e_2}\right).$$

Substituting both and separating the variables, the double sum factors:

$$(f*g)(mn) = \left(\sum_{e_1 \mid m} f(e_1)g(m/e_1)\right)\left(\sum_{e_2\mid n} f(e_2)g(n/e_2)\right) = (f*g)(m)\,(f*g)(n).$$
:::

:::remark
Note what was *not* assumed: nothing about complete multiplicativity, and nothing about convergence, since every sum is finite. Note also what fails: a convolution of two completely multiplicative functions is usually not completely multiplicative. $u * u = d$ and $d(4) = 3 \neq 4 = d(2)^2$.
:::

:::corollary[Local factors of a convolution]{#local-factors}
If $f, g$ are multiplicative then for every prime power $p^a$,

$$(f*g)(p^a) = \sum_{j=0}^{a} f(p^{j})\, g(p^{a-j}),$$

and $f*g$ is determined by these values.
:::

:::proof
The divisors of $p^a$ are exactly $1, p, \dots, p^a$ by unique factorisation, which is the stated sum. Determination is *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers* applied to $f*g$, which is multiplicative by *Convolution preserves multiplicativity*.
:::

This corollary is the computational form of the theorem. To identify a convolution it is enough to evaluate one finite sum at each prime power, and that sum is usually a geometric series or a telescoping one.

:::example[Identifying $u * \mathrm{id}$ at a prime power]
Compute $(u * \mathrm{id})(p^a)$ and hence give a closed formula for $\sigma(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} d$.
:::

:::solution
1. By *Local factors of a convolution*, $(u*\mathrm{id})(p^a) = \sum_{j=0}^a 1 \cdot p^{j} = 1 + p + \cdots + p^a$.
2. Summing the geometric series, this is $(p^{a+1}-1)/(p-1)$.
3. Since $u*\mathrm{id}$ is multiplicative, $\sigma(n) = \prod_{i} \dfrac{p_i^{a_i+1}-1}{p_i - 1}$.
4. Check: $\sigma(12) = \frac{2^3-1}{1}\cdot\frac{3^2-1}{2} = 7 \cdot 4 = 28$, and indeed $1+2+3+4+6+12 = 28$.
:::

:::example[A convolution that collapses]
Let $f$ be the multiplicative function with $f(p^a) = 1$ for $a = 1$ and $f(p^a) = 0$ for $a \ge 2$; that is, $f(n) = 1$ if $n$ is squarefree and $0$ otherwise. Compute $(f * f)(p^a)$ for $a = 0,1,2,3$.
:::

:::solution
1. $a = 0$: the only term is $f(1)f(1) = 1$.
2. $a = 1$: $f(1)f(p) + f(p)f(1) = 1 + 1 = 2$.
3. $a = 2$: $f(1)f(p^2) + f(p)f(p) + f(p^2)f(1) = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1$.
4. $a = 3$: $f(1)f(p^3) + f(p)f(p^2) + f(p^2)f(p) + f(p^3)f(1) = 0$.
5. So $(f*f)(n)$ counts the ways to write $n$ as an ordered product of two squarefree numbers, and it vanishes as soon as some prime occurs to the third power or higher.
:::

## Euler products for multiplicative functions

<!-- section: multiplicative-euler-products -->

The theorem *Euler product* says that the Dirichlet series of a multiplicative function factors over the primes. In practice one rarely needs the analytic statement; one needs the bookkeeping, which is that a *local factor*

$$E_p(f, s) = \sum_{a \ge 0} \frac{f(p^a)}{p^{as}}$$

can be computed for each prime separately, and the answer for the whole function is the product. The art is recognising a local factor as a ratio of zeta values, because then the whole series is a ratio of zeta functions and its analytic behaviour is known.

:::proposition[The series of $\mu$, $d$ and $\sigma_k$]{#standard-series}
For $\mathrm{Re}(s)$ large enough for absolute convergence,

$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{1}{\zeta(s)}, \qquad \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{d(n)}{n^s} = \zeta(s)^2, \qquad \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sigma_k(n)}{n^s} = \zeta(s)\zeta(s-k).$$
:::

:::proof
Each is *Dirichlet series multiply by convolution* applied to a convolution identity established elsewhere in the chapter. For the first, $\mu * u = \varepsilon$ by *The Möbius sum identity*, and $D_\varepsilon(s) = 1$ since $\varepsilon$ is supported at $n=1$; so $D_\mu(s)\zeta(s) = 1$. For the second, $d = u*u$. For the third, $\sigma_k = \mathrm{id}_k * u$ by *Divisor-power sums as a convolution*, and $D_{\mathrm{id}_k}(s) = \sum n^{k-s} = \zeta(s-k)$. Absolute convergence holds for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$ in the first two cases and $\mathrm{Re}(s) > \max(1, k+1)$ in the third, since $\sigma_k(n) \le n^k d(n) \le n^{k+1}$.
:::

:::example[Counting squarefree integers]
Let $Q(n) = 1$ if $n$ is squarefree and $0$ otherwise. Compute the Euler product and Dirichlet series of $Q$, and deduce the density of squarefree integers.
:::

:::solution
1. $Q$ is multiplicative with $Q(p^0) = Q(p) = 1$ and $Q(p^a) = 0$ for $a \ge 2$, so the local factor is $E_p(Q,s) = 1 + p^{-s}$.
2. Hence $\sum Q(n)n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 + p^{-s})$. Multiplying and dividing by $(1 - p^{-s})$ gives $1 + p^{-s} = (1-p^{-2s})/(1-p^{-s})$, so the product is $\zeta(s)/\zeta(2s)$.
3. The density of squarefree integers is governed by the residue of that series at $s=1$, which is $1/\zeta(2) = 6/\pi^2 \approx 0.6079$.
4. So about $60.8\%$ of integers are squarefree — a figure the local factors deliver with no analysis beyond knowing $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$.
:::

:::proposition[A completely multiplicative function has a one-term local factor]{#cm-local}
$f$ is completely multiplicative if and only if its local factors are all geometric, $E_p(f,s) = (1 - f(p)p^{-s})^{-1}$, equivalently $f(p^a) = f(p)^a$ for every prime power.
:::

:::proof
If $f$ is completely multiplicative, $f(p^a) = f(p)^a$ by induction on $a$, and summing the geometric series gives the stated factor. Conversely, if $f(p^a) = f(p)^a$ for all $p, a$, take any $m, n$ and compare prime by prime: writing $m = \prod p^{a_p}$, $n = \prod p^{b_p}$, both $f(mn)$ and $f(m)f(n)$ equal $\prod_p f(p)^{a_p + b_p}$, using multiplicativity of $f$ across distinct primes. Hence $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ with no coprimality hypothesis.
:::

:::intuition
Reading a Dirichlet series as an Euler product is reading a function "one prime at a time". Every question about a multiplicative function that does not involve how the primes interact — and most do not — becomes a question about a single power series in $p^{-s}$.
:::

## Euler's totient: definition and first values

<!-- section: definition-totient -->

:::definition[Euler's totient function]{#totient-def}
For $n \ge 1$, **Euler's totient** $\varphi(n)$ is the number of integers $k$ with $1 \le k \le n$ and $\gcd(k,n) = 1$:

$$\varphi(n) = \#\{\, k : 1 \le k \le n,\ \gcd(k,n) = 1 \,\}.$$
:::

The convention $\varphi(1) = 1$ falls out of the definition — $k = 1$ is counted, since $\gcd(1,1) = 1$ — and it is the right convention, as every identity below confirms. For a prime $p$ every one of $1, \dots, p-1$ is coprime to $p$ and $p$ itself is not, so $\varphi(p) = p-1$; this is the largest $\varphi(n)$ can be, and it characterises the primes.

:::proposition[The totient characterises primes]{#totient-primes}
For $n > 1$: $\varphi(n) = n - 1$ if and only if $n$ is prime.
:::

:::proof
If $n = p$ is prime the count above gives $p-1$. Conversely suppose $n > 1$ is composite, say $n = ab$ with $1 < a < n$. Then $a$ lies in $\{1, \dots, n\}$, is not $n$, and satisfies $\gcd(a,n) = a > 1$, so at least two of the $n$ integers in the range — namely $a$ and $n$ — are excluded from the count, giving $\varphi(n) \le n - 2$.
:::

:::theorem[Totient of a prime power]{#totient-prime-power}
For a prime $p$ and $a \ge 1$,

$$\varphi(p^a) = p^a - p^{a-1} = p^{a}\left(1 - \frac1p\right).$$
:::

:::proof
An integer $k$ in $\{1, \dots, p^a\}$ fails to be coprime to $p^a$ exactly when $p \mid k$, since $p$ is the only prime dividing $p^a$. The multiples of $p$ in that range are $p, 2p, \dots, p^{a-1}\cdot p$, of which there are $p^{a-1}$. Subtracting from the total $p^a$ gives the formula.
:::

:::example[Small totients]
Compute $\varphi(n)$ for $n = 9, 16, 12, 30$ directly from the definition, and check the prime-power values against the theorem.
:::

:::solution
1. $\varphi(9)$: of $1,\dots,9$ the multiples of $3$ are $3,6,9$, so $\varphi(9) = 9 - 3 = 6$, matching $3^2 - 3^1$.
2. $\varphi(16) = 16 - 8 = 8$, the excluded numbers being the eight even ones.
3. $\varphi(12)$: coprime residues are $1,5,7,11$, so $\varphi(12) = 4$.
4. $\varphi(30)$: coprime residues are $1,7,11,13,17,19,23,29$, so $\varphi(30) = 8$.
:::

## The product formula, via inclusion–exclusion

<!-- section: totient-formula -->

There are two proofs of the closed formula for $\varphi$, and both are worth having. The first counts directly with inclusion–exclusion and needs no structural theory at all; the second, in the next section, deduces it from multiplicativity in two lines. The first proof is the one that explains *why* the answer is a product over the distinct primes and does not see the exponents.

:::theorem[Euler's product formula]{#totient-formula-thm}
For $n \ge 1$ with distinct prime divisors $p_1, \dots, p_r$,

$$\varphi(n) = n \prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_i}\right) = \prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(p_i^{a_i} - p_i^{a_i-1}\right),$$

where $n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$.
:::

:::proof
**By inclusion–exclusion.** For each $i$ let $A_i = \{\,k : 1 \le k \le n,\ p_i \mid k\,\}$. An integer $k$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$ satisfies $\gcd(k,n) > 1$ precisely when some prime dividing $n$ divides $k$, that is, precisely when $k \in A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_r$. Hence

$$\varphi(n) = n - \left| A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_r\right| .$$

For any subset $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,r\}$, the intersection $\bigcap_{i \in S} A_i$ consists of the multiples of $\prod_{i\in S}p_i$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$, because the $p_i$ are distinct primes so divisibility by each is divisibility by the product. Since $\prod_{i \in S}p_i$ divides $n$, the number of such multiples is exactly $n / \prod_{i\in S}p_i$ with no rounding. Inclusion–exclusion gives

$$\varphi(n) = \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,r\}} (-1)^{|S|}\,\frac{n}{\prod_{i \in S}p_i} = n\sum_{S}\prod_{i\in S}\left(\frac{-1}{p_i}\right).$$

The last sum is the expansion of the product $\prod_{i=1}^r\left(1 - 1/p_i\right)$: choosing, from each factor, either the $1$ or the $-1/p_i$ corresponds exactly to choosing the subset $S$ of indices contributing their second term. Therefore $\varphi(n) = n\prod_i (1 - 1/p_i)$. Distributing $n = \prod p_i^{a_i}$ one factor at a time turns each $p_i^{a_i}(1 - 1/p_i)$ into $p_i^{a_i} - p_i^{a_i - 1}$, which is the second form.
:::

:::intuition
Read $\varphi(n)/n$ as a probability: a random $k$ avoids $p_1$ with probability $1 - 1/p_1$, avoids $p_2$ with probability $1 - 1/p_2$, and — because the $p_i$ are distinct primes dividing $n$ — these events are genuinely independent on the range $1$ to $n$. The formula is the product rule for independent events, and the exactness of $n/\prod_{i\in S}p_i$ in the proof is precisely that independence.
:::

:::example[Computing $\varphi$ from the factorisation]
Compute $\varphi(600)$, $\varphi(1001)$ and $\varphi(2^{10})$.
:::

:::solution
1. $600 = 2^3\cdot3\cdot5^2$, so $\varphi(600) = 600(1-\tfrac12)(1-\tfrac13)(1-\tfrac15) = 600 \cdot \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac45 = 160$.
2. $1001 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$, all distinct primes, so $\varphi(1001) = 6 \cdot 10 \cdot 12 = 720$.
3. $\varphi(2^{10}) = 2^{10} - 2^9 = 512$.
:::

:::corollary[Divisibility and parity]{#totient-parity}
$\varphi(n)$ is even for every $n > 2$. Moreover if $m \mid n$ then $\varphi(m) \mid \varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
For the parity: if $n$ has an odd prime factor $p$ with $p^a \| n$, then the factor $p^{a}-p^{a-1} = p^{a-1}(p-1)$ is even, so the product is. Otherwise $n = 2^a$ with $a \ge 2$, and $\varphi(n) = 2^{a-1}$ is even.

For the divisibility, compare prime by prime. Write $m = \prod p^{b_p}$ and $n = \prod p^{a_p}$ with $b_p \le a_p$. Then $\varphi(m) = \prod_{b_p \ge 1} p^{b_p - 1}(p-1)$ and $\varphi(n) = \prod_{a_p\ge1}p^{a_p-1}(p-1)$. Every prime $p$ with $b_p \ge 1$ also has $a_p \ge b_p \ge 1$, so each factor $p^{b_p-1}(p-1)$ of $\varphi(m)$ divides the corresponding factor $p^{a_p-1}(p-1)$ of $\varphi(n)$, and the remaining factors of $\varphi(n)$ are integers. Multiplying, $\varphi(m)\mid\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::pitfall
The formula sees only the *distinct* primes in the bracket form, which tempts one to write $\varphi(p^a) = p - 1$. It is $p^a - p^{a-1}$: the exponent has been absorbed into the leading $n$, not discarded. Similarly $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$ requires $\gcd(m,n) = 1$; for $m = n = 2$ it gives $1$ instead of the correct $\varphi(4) = 2$.
:::

## Multiplicativity, Euler's theorem, and the second proof

<!-- section: totient-multiplicative-euler -->

The totient is multiplicative, and the cleanest proof is structural: $\varphi(n)$ counts the units of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and the Chinese remainder theorem splits that unit group across coprime factors.

:::theorem[$\varphi$ counts units]{#totient-units}
For $n \ge 1$, the residue class of $k$ is invertible in the ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if $\gcd(k,n) = 1$. Hence $\left|(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right| = \varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
If $\gcd(k,n) = 1$, Bézout gives integers $x, y$ with $kx + ny = 1$, so $kx \equiv 1 \pmod n$ and $k$ is invertible. Conversely if $kx \equiv 1 \pmod n$ then $kx - 1 = ny$ for some $y$, so any common divisor of $k$ and $n$ divides $1$, forcing $\gcd(k,n) = 1$. The residues $1, \dots, n$ represent each class of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ exactly once, so counting invertible classes counts exactly the $k$ in that range with $\gcd(k,n)=1$.
:::

:::theorem[$\varphi$ is multiplicative]{#totient-multiplicative}
If $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ then $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
The Chinese remainder theorem gives a ring isomorphism

$$\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, \qquad k \bmod mn \;\longmapsto\; (k \bmod m,\ k \bmod n),$$

valid because $\gcd(m,n)=1$. A ring isomorphism carries units to units, and a pair is a unit in a product ring exactly when each coordinate is. Hence

$$\left|(\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z})^\times\right| = \left|(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times\right|\cdot\left|(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times\right|,$$

which by *$\varphi$ counts units* is $\varphi(mn) = \varphi(m)\varphi(n)$. Since $\varphi(1) = 1$, $\varphi$ is multiplicative.
:::

:::corollary[Euler's product formula, second proof]{#totient-formula-again}
$\varphi(n) = \prod_i\left(p_i^{a_i}-p_i^{a_i-1}\right)$.
:::

:::proof
By *$\varphi$ is multiplicative* and *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers*, $\varphi(n) = \prod_i \varphi(p_i^{a_i})$, and each factor is $p_i^{a_i}-p_i^{a_i-1}$ by *Totient of a prime power*.
:::

Comparing the two proofs is instructive. The inclusion–exclusion argument is self-contained and gives the answer for a single $n$ with no theory; the structural argument gives the answer for all $n$ at once and explains the product as a decomposition of a group. Both take about a page; only the second generalises, and it is the one that tells us what $\varphi(n)$ *is* rather than merely how large it is.

:::theorem[Euler's theorem]{#euler-theorem}
If $\gcd(a,n) = 1$ then

$$a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n .$$
:::

:::proof
The group $G = (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is finite of order $\varphi(n)$ by *$\varphi$ counts units*, and $a$ represents an element of $G$. By Lagrange's theorem the order of the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$ divides $|G| = \varphi(n)$, so $a^{\varphi(n)}$ is the identity of $G$, which is the class of $1$.

Without group theory the same argument runs directly: let $r_1, \dots, r_{\varphi(n)}$ be the residues coprime to $n$. Multiplication by $a$ permutes them, since $ar_i$ is again coprime to $n$ and $ar_i \equiv ar_j$ forces $r_i \equiv r_j$ after cancelling the invertible $a$. Taking the product of all of them, $\prod_i (a r_i) \equiv \prod_i r_i$, that is $a^{\varphi(n)}\prod_i r_i \equiv \prod_i r_i \pmod n$; the product $\prod r_i$ is coprime to $n$ and hence cancellable, leaving $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1$.
:::

:::corollary[Fermat's little theorem]{#fermat-little}
If $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$ then $a^{p-1}\equiv 1 \pmod p$.
:::

:::proof
Take $n = p$ in *Euler's theorem* and use $\varphi(p) = p - 1$.
:::

:::example[Reducing a large power]
Compute $7^{2024} \bmod 45$.
:::

:::solution
1. $45 = 3^2\cdot5$, so $\varphi(45) = (9-3)(5-1) = 24$, and $\gcd(7,45)=1$.
2. By *Euler's theorem*, $7^{24}\equiv 1 \pmod{45}$.
3. $2024 = 24\cdot 84 + 8$, so $7^{2024}\equiv 7^{8}\pmod{45}$.
4. $7^2 = 49 \equiv 4$; $7^4 \equiv 4^2 = 16$; $7^8 \equiv 16^2 = 256 \equiv 256 - 225 = 31 \pmod{45}$.
5. Final value: $31$.
:::

## Divisor sums of the totient

<!-- section: totient-applications-sums -->

One identity dominates the applications of $\varphi$, and it is the first genuinely non-obvious convolution identity in the chapter.

:::theorem[Gauss's divisor sum for $\varphi$]{#gauss-phi-sum}
For every $n \ge 1$,

$$\sum_{d \mid n}\varphi(d) = n, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \varphi * u = \mathrm{id}.$$
:::

:::proof
**By partitioning the fractions.** Consider the $n$ fractions $\tfrac1n, \tfrac2n, \dots, \tfrac nn$ and reduce each to lowest terms. Each reduced fraction has the form $a/d$ with $d \mid n$, $1 \le a \le d$ and $\gcd(a,d) = 1$; conversely every such $a/d$ arises exactly once, from $k = a\cdot(n/d)$. So the $n$ fractions are partitioned by their reduced denominator $d$, and the class with denominator $d$ has exactly $\varphi(d)$ members. Counting the whole set in two ways gives $n = \sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)$.

**By multiplicativity.** Alternatively, $\varphi * u$ is multiplicative by *Convolution preserves multiplicativity*, so it suffices to check it at prime powers. By *Local factors of a convolution*,

$$(\varphi * u)(p^a) = \sum_{j=0}^{a}\varphi(p^{j}) = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{a}\left(p^{j}-p^{j-1}\right) = p^{a},$$

the sum telescoping. Since $\mathrm{id}$ is also multiplicative and agrees at every prime power, the two functions are equal.
:::

:::remark
The two proofs are not the same argument in different clothes. The first is a bijection and gives the result for one $n$ without knowing $\varphi$ is multiplicative; the second is a telescoping sum and needs the theory. Given Möbius inversion, later in the chapter, this identity will be run backwards to *recover* $\varphi = \mu * \mathrm{id}$, which is how $\varphi$ is usually computed in the convolution ring.
:::

:::example[Checking Gauss's identity]
Verify $\sum_{d\mid 18}\varphi(d) = 18$.
:::

:::solution
1. Divisors of $18$: $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$.
2. Totients: $\varphi(1)=1$, $\varphi(2)=1$, $\varphi(3)=2$, $\varphi(6)=2$, $\varphi(9)=6$, $\varphi(18)=6$.
3. Sum: $1+1+2+2+6+6 = 18$.
:::

:::proposition[Sum of the coprime residues]{#coprime-sum}
For $n > 1$,

$$\sum_{\substack{1 \le k \le n \\ \gcd(k,n)=1}} k \;=\; \frac{n\,\varphi(n)}{2}.$$
:::

:::proof
Pair $k$ with $n-k$. Since $\gcd(k,n) = \gcd(n-k, n)$, the map $k \mapsto n-k$ is an involution of the set $C$ of residues coprime to $n$ in $\{1,\dots,n-1\}$ — note $k = n$ is excluded as $n>1$ means $\gcd(n,n) = n > 1$. It has no fixed point: $k = n-k$ would give $n = 2k$, so $\gcd(k,n) = k$, which is $1$ only if $n = 2$, and for $n = 2$ the set $C = \{1\}$ has odd size with $1 = 2-1$ its own partner, giving the sum $1 = 2\cdot1/2$ as claimed. For $n > 2$, $C$ splits into $\varphi(n)/2$ pairs each summing to $n$, so the total is $n\varphi(n)/2$.
:::

:::example[A totient equation]
Find all $n$ with $\varphi(n) = 8$.
:::

:::solution
1. Write $n = \prod p_i^{a_i}$; then $\varphi(n) = \prod p_i^{a_i-1}(p_i-1) = 8$, so every $p_i - 1$ divides $8$ and every odd $p_i$ contributes $p_i^{a_i-1}$ as a divisor of $8$, forcing $a_i = 1$ for odd $p_i$.
2. Odd primes with $p-1 \mid 8$: $p \in \{3, 5\}$ give $p-1 = 2, 4$. ($p=9$ is not prime; $p-1=8$ gives $p=9$, excluded.)
3. The power of $2$ contributes $1$ if $a = 0$ or $1$, and $2^{a-1}$ if $a \ge 1$.
4. Enumerating: $n = 15$ ($2\cdot4 = 8$), $n = 16$ ($2^3 = 8$), $n = 20 = 2^2\cdot5$ ($2\cdot4$), $n = 24 = 2^3\cdot3$ ($4\cdot 2$), $n = 30 = 2\cdot3\cdot5$ ($1\cdot2\cdot4$).
5. So the solution set is $\{15, 16, 20, 24, 30\}$, five values — the totient is far from injective.
:::

## The Möbius function

<!-- section: mobius-definition -->

The Möbius function exists to invert divisor sums. Its definition looks arbitrary — three cases, one of which is a sign — and the only honest motivation is the identity proved at the end of this section, which is what the definition was reverse-engineered to satisfy.

:::definition[Möbius function]{#mobius-def}
For $n \ge 1$,

$$\mu(n) = \begin{cases} 1 & n = 1,\\ (-1)^{r} & n = p_1p_2\cdots p_r \text{ with the } p_i \text{ distinct primes},\\ 0 & n \text{ is divisible by } p^2 \text{ for some prime } p.\end{cases}$$

Equivalently, $\mu(n) = (-1)^{\omega(n)}$ if $n$ is squarefree and $0$ otherwise.
:::

So $\mu$ is supported on the squarefree integers and records the parity of the number of prime factors there. The first values are $\mu(1)=1$, $\mu(2)=\mu(3)=-1$, $\mu(4)=0$, $\mu(5)=-1$, $\mu(6)=1$, $\mu(7)=-1$, $\mu(8)=\mu(9)=0$, $\mu(10)=1$.

:::proposition[$\mu$ is multiplicative]{#mobius-multiplicative}
$\mu$ is multiplicative: $\mu(1) = 1$ and $\mu(mn) = \mu(m)\mu(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m,n) = 1$. It is not completely multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
Let $\gcd(m,n) = 1$. If either $m$ or $n$ is divisible by a square $p^2$, then so is $mn$, and both sides are $0$. Otherwise both are squarefree; since they share no prime, $mn$ is squarefree too, and its distinct primes are those of $m$ together with those of $n$, with no overlap. Hence $\omega(mn) = \omega(m)+\omega(n)$ and

$$\mu(mn) = (-1)^{\omega(m)+\omega(n)} = (-1)^{\omega(m)}(-1)^{\omega(n)} = \mu(m)\mu(n).$$

It is not completely multiplicative because $\mu(4) = 0$ while $\mu(2)^2 = 1$.
:::

:::remark
On prime powers $\mu$ is as simple as a function can be: $\mu(p) = -1$, and $\mu(p^a) = 0$ for every $a \ge 2$. Combined with *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers* this is an alternative definition, and it is the one to use in a proof, since it turns any local factor $\sum_{j=0}^a \mu(p^j)f(p^{a-j})$ into a two-term expression.
:::

:::theorem[The Möbius sum identity]{#mobius-sum-identity}
For every $n \ge 1$,

$$\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d) = \begin{cases}1 & n = 1,\\ 0 & n > 1,\end{cases}$$

that is, $\mu * u = \varepsilon$.
:::

:::proof
For $n = 1$ the only divisor is $1$ and $\mu(1) = 1$.

For $n > 1$, let $p_1, \dots, p_r$ be the distinct primes dividing $n$, so $r \ge 1$. A divisor $d$ of $n$ contributes nothing unless it is squarefree, so only the $2^r$ divisors $d = \prod_{i\in S}p_i$ for $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,r\}$ matter, and such a $d$ contributes $(-1)^{|S|}$. Grouping the subsets by size,

$$\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d) = \sum_{k=0}^{r} \binom{r}{k}(-1)^{k} = (1 - 1)^{r} = 0,$$

by the binomial theorem, using $r \ge 1$ so that $0^r = 0$.

Alternatively and more briefly: $\mu * u$ is multiplicative by *Convolution preserves multiplicativity*, and at a prime power $(\mu * u)(p^a) = \mu(1) + \mu(p) + 0 + \cdots + 0 = 1 - 1 = 0$ for $a \ge 1$, which is $\varepsilon(p^a)$.
:::

:::intuition
The identity is inclusion–exclusion in disguise, and the disguise is thin: the alternating sum $\sum_k \binom{r}{k}(-1)^k$ is exactly the signed count that appears when one over-counts a union and corrects. This is why $\mu$ shows up wherever a counting problem is solved by sieving.
:::

## Möbius inversion

<!-- section: mobius-inversion -->

Möbius inversion is the statement that convolving with $u$ is an invertible operation, whose inverse is convolving with $\mu$. Once *The Möbius sum identity* is available and convolution is known to be associative, the proof is two lines; the content is entirely in that identity.

:::theorem[Möbius inversion]{#mobius-inversion-thm}
Let $f$ and $g$ be arithmetic functions. Then

$$g(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d) \ \text{ for all } n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,g(n/d)\ \text{ for all } n .$$

In convolution notation: $g = f * u$ if and only if $f = \mu * g$.
:::

:::proof
($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $g = f*u$. Convolve with $\mu$ and use commutativity and associativity, established in *Convolution makes a commutative ring*:

$$\mu * g = \mu * (f * u) = f * (\mu * u) = f * \varepsilon = f,$$

the third equality by *The Möbius sum identity* and the fourth because $\varepsilon$ is the convolution identity.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $f = \mu * g$. Then

$$f * u = (\mu * g) * u = g * (\mu * u) = g * \varepsilon = g,$$

by the same three facts. So each statement implies the other.
:::

:::remark
The symmetry of the two directions is not an accident of the proof; it is the statement that $u$ is a unit of the Dirichlet ring with inverse $\mu$. Every "inversion formula" in combinatorics is of this shape — an incidence algebra in which one element is invertible — and the Möbius function of the divisibility order is the prototype.
:::

:::example[Recovering $\varphi$ by inversion]
Apply Möbius inversion to Gauss's identity $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d) = n$.
:::

:::solution
1. The identity says $\mathrm{id} = \varphi * u$, so the hypothesis of *Möbius inversion* holds with $f = \varphi$, $g = \mathrm{id}$.
2. Inverting, $\varphi = \mu * \mathrm{id}$, that is $\varphi(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,\dfrac{n}{d}$.
3. Check at $n = 12$: the squarefree divisors are $1, 2, 3, 6$ with $\mu$-values $1, -1, -1, 1$, giving $12 - 6 - 4 + 2 = 4 = \varphi(12)$.
4. Dividing by $n$ turns the formula into $\varphi(n)/n = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)/d$, which on expanding over the squarefree divisors is exactly Euler's product $\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)$ — a third proof of *Euler's product formula*.
:::

:::corollary[Multiplicativity transfers through inversion]{#inversion-mult}
If $g = f * u$ then $f$ is multiplicative if and only if $g$ is.
:::

:::proof
If $f$ is multiplicative then so is $g = f*u$ by *Convolution preserves multiplicativity*, since $u$ is multiplicative. Conversely if $g$ is multiplicative then $f = \mu * g$ is multiplicative by the same theorem, since $\mu$ is multiplicative by *$\mu$ is multiplicative*.
:::

:::example[Inverting the divisor count]
Given that $d = u * u$, find the function $f$ with $f * u = d$, and verify at $n = 12$.
:::

:::solution
1. By *Möbius inversion*, $f = \mu * d$. But $d = u*u$, so $f = \mu * u * u = \varepsilon * u = u$.
2. So $f = u$, which is the identity $\sum_{d\mid n} 1 = d(n)$ we started from — consistent.
3. At $n = 12$: $\sum_{d\mid 12}\mu(d)\,d(12/d) = \mu(1)d(12) + \mu(2)d(6) + \mu(3)d(4) + \mu(4)d(3) + \mu(6)d(2) + \mu(12)d(1)$.
4. This is $6 - 4 - 3 + 0 + 2 + 0 = 1 = u(12)$.
:::

:::pitfall
Möbius inversion requires the relation to hold for **every** $n$, not just the one of interest; the inverse formula at $n$ uses $g$ at all divisors of $n$. And the $\mu$ and the argument must be paired as $\mu(d)g(n/d)$ — writing $\mu(n/d)g(d)$ gives the same sum by symmetry of the divisor pairing, but $\mu(d)g(d)$ gives nonsense.
:::

## The sum identity and what it does

<!-- section: mobius-sum-property -->

The identity $\mu * u = \varepsilon$ deserves a section of its own because almost every use of $\mu$ in practice is an application of it in the following form: it is a **detector for the condition $n = 1$**, and can be inserted into any sum to impose a coprimality or exactness constraint.

:::proposition[$\mu$ as an indicator]{#mu-indicator}
For positive integers $a, b$,

$$\sum_{d \mid \gcd(a,b)} \mu(d) = \begin{cases}1 & \gcd(a,b) = 1,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
:::

:::proof
Apply *The Möbius sum identity* with $n = \gcd(a,b)$: the sum is $\varepsilon(\gcd(a,b))$, which is $1$ exactly when $\gcd(a,b) = 1$.
:::

That single proposition converts "count the $k$ coprime to $n$" into a double sum with no coprimality condition, which can then be exchanged. It is the engine behind the sieve of Eratosthenes made quantitative.

:::example[Euler's formula by sieving]
Use *$\mu$ as an indicator* to recompute $\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\varphi(n) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon(\gcd(k,n)) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{d \mid \gcd(k,n)}\mu(d)$.
2. Exchange the order: $d$ ranges over divisors of $n$, and for fixed $d$ the inner condition $d \mid k$, $1 \le k \le n$ has exactly $n/d$ solutions.
3. So $\varphi(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,\dfrac nd$, which is $\varphi = \mu * \mathrm{id}$ again.
4. Expanding over squarefree divisors gives $n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)$.
:::

:::definition[Mertens function]{#mertens-function}
The **Mertens function** is the summatory function of $\mu$:

$$M(x) = \sum_{n \le x}\mu(n).$$
:::

The pointwise identity $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d) = 0$ says the signs cancel *within* each $n > 1$; the size of $M(x)$ asks whether they cancel *across* $n$, which is a far deeper question. $M(x) = O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis, and even $M(x) = o(x)$ is equivalent to the prime number theorem.

:::proposition[A crude bound on $M$]{#mertens-crude}
$|M(x)| \le x$ for all $x \ge 1$, and $M$ takes the values $M(1) = 1$, $M(2) = 0$, $M(3) = -1$, $M(4) = -1$, $M(10) = -1$, $M(100) = 1$.
:::

:::proof
The bound is the triangle inequality: $|M(x)| \le \sum_{n\le x}|\mu(n)| \le \lfloor x\rfloor \le x$, since $|\mu(n)|\le1$. The listed values are direct summation of the table of $\mu$ given after the definition, extended by the same rule.
:::

:::proposition[Squarefree indicator]{#squarefree-indicator}
For every $n \ge 1$,

$$\mu^{2}(n) = \sum_{d^{2}\mid n}\mu(d),$$

which is $1$ if $n$ is squarefree and $0$ otherwise.
:::

:::proof
Both sides are multiplicative: the left by *$\mu$ is multiplicative* and *Products and quotients*; the right because $\sum_{d^2\mid n}\mu(d)$ is the convolution-type sum whose value at $n$ depends only on the exponents, and splitting $n$ into coprime parts splits the condition $d^2 \mid n$ as $d = d_1d_2$ with $d_i^2$ dividing the respective part, exactly as in *Divisors of a coprime product*. So check at $p^a$: the $d$ with $d^2 \mid p^a$ are $d = p^j$ with $2j \le a$, and $\mu(p^j) = 0$ unless $j \in\{0,1\}$. For $a = 0$ or $a=1$ only $j=0$ qualifies, giving $1$; for $a \ge 2$ both $j=0$ and $j=1$ qualify, giving $1 - 1 = 0$. That is exactly $\mu^2(p^a)$.
:::

:::example[Counting squarefree integers up to $x$]
Use *Squarefree indicator* to estimate the number of squarefree integers not exceeding $x$.
:::

:::solution
1. $Q(x) = \sum_{n\le x}\mu^2(n) = \sum_{n \le x}\sum_{d^2\mid n}\mu(d)$.
2. Exchange: for each $d \le \sqrt x$ the number of $n \le x$ with $d^2 \mid n$ is $\lfloor x/d^2\rfloor$, so $Q(x) = \sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\mu(d)\lfloor x/d^2\rfloor$.
3. Replace the floor by $x/d^2 + O(1)$; the errors total $O(\sqrt x)$.
4. The main term is $x\sum_{d\le\sqrt x}\mu(d)/d^2 = x\left(1/\zeta(2) + O(1/\sqrt x)\right)$.
5. Hence $Q(x) = 6x/\pi^2 + O(\sqrt x)$, agreeing with the density found in *Counting squarefree integers*.
:::

## Applications of Möbius inversion

<!-- section: mobius-applications -->

Möbius inversion is used whenever a quantity is easy to compute "with multiplicity" and hard to compute exactly. Two standard examples follow, one from polynomial algebra and one from combinatorics on words; both have the same shape, a total that decomposes over divisors.

:::theorem[Counting monic irreducibles over a finite field]{#irreducible-count}
Let $q$ be a prime power and let $N_q(n)$ be the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$. Then

$$\sum_{d \mid n} d\,N_q(d) = q^{n}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad N_q(n) = \frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,q^{n/d}.$$
:::

:::proof
The field $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ is the splitting field of $x^{q^n}-x$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$, and that polynomial is squarefree since its derivative is $-1$. Its monic irreducible factors over $\mathbb{F}_q$ are exactly the monic irreducibles whose degree divides $n$: an irreducible of degree $d$ has its roots in $\mathbb{F}_{q^d}$, which sits inside $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$ precisely when $d \mid n$. Comparing degrees on both sides of the factorisation of $x^{q^n}-x$ gives $\sum_{d\mid n} d\,N_q(d) = q^n$.

Now set $f(d) = d\,N_q(d)$ and $g(n) = q^n$. The displayed identity is $g = f * u$, so *Möbius inversion* gives $f(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}$; dividing by $n$ gives the formula.
:::

:::example[Irreducible cubics and quartics over $\mathbb{F}_2$]
Compute $N_2(3)$ and $N_2(4)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $N_2(3) = \frac13\left(\mu(1)2^3 + \mu(3)2^1\right) = \frac13(8-2) = 2$. The two are $x^3+x+1$ and $x^3+x^2+1$.
2. $N_2(4) = \frac14\left(\mu(1)2^4 + \mu(2)2^2 + \mu(4)2^1\right) = \frac14(16 - 4 + 0) = 3$.
3. As a sanity check, $N_2(1) = 2$ (namely $x$ and $x+1$) and $1\cdot2 + 2\cdot N_2(2) = 4$ gives $N_2(2) = 1$, the polynomial $x^2+x+1$.
:::

:::proposition[Necklace counting]{#necklace}
The number of aperiodic binary strings of length $n$, up to nothing (as linear strings), is $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)2^{n/d}$, and the number of binary necklaces with primitive period $n$ is $\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)2^{n/d}$.
:::

:::proof
Every binary string of length $n$ has a unique primitive period $d \mid n$, namely the least $d$ such that the string is a $d$-block repeated $n/d$ times. Letting $A(d)$ be the number of strings of length $d$ that are aperiodic, classifying the $2^n$ strings by their primitive period gives $\sum_{d\mid n}A(d) = 2^n$. By *Möbius inversion*, $A(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)2^{n/d}$. Cyclic rotation acts freely on the aperiodic strings of length $n$ — a nontrivial rotation fixing a string would force a smaller period — so the orbits all have size $n$ and there are $A(n)/n$ of them.
:::

:::remark
The identical shape of the last two results is not coincidence: $N_q(n)$ counts orbits of the Frobenius map on $\mathbb{F}_{q^n}$, and the Frobenius is a cyclic rotation of the "string" of conjugates. The necklace formula and the irreducible-polynomial formula are the same theorem about a cyclic group acting freely.
:::

## Divisor functions

<!-- section: sigma-definition -->

:::definition[Divisor-power functions]{#sigma-def}
For $k \ge 0$ and $n \ge 1$,

$$\sigma_k(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} d^{\,k} .$$

In particular $\sigma_0 = d$ counts the divisors and $\sigma_1 = \sigma$ sums them. When $k$ is omitted, $\sigma$ means $\sigma_1$.
:::

:::theorem[Divisor-power sums as a convolution]{#sigma-convolution}
$\sigma_k = \mathrm{id}_k * u$, and consequently $\sigma_k$ is multiplicative for every $k \ge 0$.
:::

:::proof
By definition $(\mathrm{id}_k * u)(n) = \sum_{d\mid n} d^k \cdot 1 = \sigma_k(n)$. Both $\mathrm{id}_k$ and $u$ are completely multiplicative, hence multiplicative, so *Convolution preserves multiplicativity* makes $\sigma_k$ multiplicative.
:::

:::example[Divisor counts and sums]
Compute $d(720)$, $\sigma(720)$ and $\sigma_2(12)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $720 = 2^4\cdot3^2\cdot5$, so $d(720) = 5\cdot3\cdot2 = 30$.
2. $\sigma(720) = (2^5-1)\cdot\frac{3^3-1}{2}\cdot\frac{5^2-1}{4} = 31 \cdot 13\cdot 6 = 2418$.
3. $12 = 2^2\cdot3$, so $\sigma_2(12) = (1+4+16)(1+9) = 21\cdot 10 = 210$; directly, $1+4+9+16+36+144 = 210$.
:::

:::proposition[Divisors come in pairs]{#divisor-pairs}
The map $e \mapsto n/e$ is an involution on the divisors of $n$, and

$$\sigma_k(n) = n^{k}\,\sigma_{-k}(n) = n^k \sum_{d\mid n} d^{-k}.$$

Consequently $d(n)$ is odd if and only if $n$ is a perfect square.
:::

:::proof
If $e \mid n$ then $n/e \mid n$, and applying the map twice returns $e$, so it is an involution and in particular a bijection of the divisor set. Substituting $d = n/e$ in the defining sum gives $\sigma_k(n) = \sum_{e\mid n}(n/e)^k = n^k\sum_{e\mid n}e^{-k}$.

For the parity of $d(n)$: the involution pairs up divisors, so $d(n)$ is odd exactly when the involution has a fixed point, that is when $e = n/e$ for some divisor, i.e. $n = e^2$. (A fixed point is unique when it exists, since $e$ is determined as $\sqrt n$.)
:::

:::example[A divisor-count equation]
Find the smallest positive integer with exactly $12$ divisors.
:::

:::solution
1. If $n = \prod p_i^{a_i}$ then $d(n) = \prod(a_i+1)$, so the exponent pattern must have $\prod(a_i+1) = 12$.
2. The factorisations of $12$ into factors $\ge 2$ are $12$, $6\cdot2$, $4\cdot3$, $3\cdot2\cdot2$, $2\cdot 6$, and so on; the exponent multisets are $\{11\}$, $\{5,1\}$, $\{3,2\}$, $\{2,1,1\}$.
3. To minimise $n$, assign the largest exponent to the smallest prime: $2^{11} = 2048$; $2^5\cdot3 = 96$; $2^3\cdot3^2 = 72$; $2^2\cdot3\cdot5 = 60$.
4. The smallest is $60$, with divisors $1,2,3,4,5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60$.
:::

:::pitfall
$\sigma$ is multiplicative, not additive or completely multiplicative. $\sigma(4) = 7$ while $\sigma(2)^2 = 9$; and $\sigma(2+3) = \sigma(5) = 6$ has no relation to $\sigma(2)+\sigma(3) = 7$. Only coprime *products* split.
:::

## Formulas and Dirichlet series for $\sigma_k$

<!-- section: sigma-formula-series -->

:::theorem[Closed formula for $\sigma_k$]{#sigma-formula}
Let $n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$. For $k \ge 1$,

$$\sigma_k(n) = \prod_{i=1}^{r}\frac{p_i^{k(a_i+1)}-1}{p_i^{k}-1},$$

and for $k = 0$,

$$d(n) = \prod_{i=1}^{r}(a_i + 1).$$
:::

:::proof
By *Divisor-power sums as a convolution*, $\sigma_k$ is multiplicative, so by *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers* it is enough to evaluate at $p^a$. The divisors of $p^a$ are $1, p, \dots, p^a$, so

$$\sigma_k(p^a) = \sum_{j=0}^{a} p^{jk} = \sum_{j=0}^a (p^k)^{j}.$$

For $k \ge 1$ this is a geometric series with ratio $p^k \neq 1$, summing to $(p^{k(a+1)}-1)/(p^k-1)$. For $k = 0$ every term is $1$ and there are $a+1$ of them. Taking the product over $i$ gives both statements.
:::

:::corollary[Divisor series]{#divisor-series}
For $\mathrm{Re}(s) > \max(1, k+1)$,

$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sigma_k(n)}{n^{s}} = \zeta(s)\,\zeta(s-k).$$
:::

:::proof
This is *The series of $\mu$, $d$ and $\sigma_k$*, restated; it follows from $\sigma_k = \mathrm{id}_k * u$ and *Dirichlet series multiply by convolution*.
:::

:::example[Using the closed formula]
Compute $\sigma_3(10)$ two ways.
:::

:::solution
1. Directly: the divisors of $10$ are $1,2,5,10$, so $\sigma_3(10) = 1 + 8 + 125 + 1000 = 1134$.
2. By the formula: $\sigma_3(10) = \frac{2^{6}-1}{2^3-1}\cdot\frac{5^{6}-1}{5^3-1} = \frac{63}{7}\cdot\frac{15624}{124} = 9 \cdot 126 = 1134$.
:::

:::proposition[Bounds on $\sigma$]{#sigma-bounds}
For every $n \ge 1$,

$$n < \sigma(n) \le n\,d(n) \quad (n>1), \qquad \frac{\sigma(n)}{n} = \sum_{d\mid n}\frac1d < \prod_{p \mid n}\left(1 - \frac1p\right)^{-1}.$$
:::

:::proof
The lower bound holds because $1$ and $n$ are distinct divisors when $n>1$, so $\sigma(n) \ge n+1 > n$. The upper bound $\sigma(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}d \le \sum_{d\mid n} n = n\,d(n)$ is term by term.

For the last claim, *Divisors come in pairs* gives $\sigma(n)/n = \sum_{d\mid n}1/d$. Every divisor $d$ of $n$ is a product $\prod p^{c_p}$ with $c_p \le a_p$, so $\sum_{d\mid n}1/d = \prod_{p\mid n}\left(1 + p^{-1}+\cdots+p^{-a_p}\right)$, and each factor is a truncation of the geometric series $\left(1-1/p\right)^{-1}$, hence strictly smaller.
:::

:::remark
Combined with *Euler's product formula*, the last bound says $\sigma(n)\varphi(n) < n^2$ for all $n > 1$: the totient and the divisor sum pull in opposite directions, and their product is pinned near $n^2$. Indeed $\sigma(n)\varphi(n) > n^2\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-2})$, so the ratio $\sigma(n)\varphi(n)/n^2$ lies between $6/\pi^2$ and $1$.
:::

## Perfect numbers and the Euclid–Euler theorem

<!-- section: sigma-perfect-numbers -->

:::definition[Perfect, abundant, deficient]{#perfect-def}
Call $n$ **perfect** if $\sigma(n) = 2n$, **abundant** if $\sigma(n) > 2n$, and **deficient** if $\sigma(n) < 2n$. Equivalently, the sum of the *proper* divisors $s(n) = \sigma(n)-n$ equals, exceeds, or falls short of $n$.
:::

The first perfect numbers are $6 = 1+2+3$, $28 = 1+2+4+7+14$, $496$ and $8128$. Each is of the form $2^{p-1}(2^p-1)$ with $2^p-1$ prime, and the theorem below says the even ones are exactly these — a complete classification, proved in full, with the odd case still open after twenty-three centuries.

:::theorem[Euclid–Euler]{#euclid-euler}
An even number $n$ is perfect if and only if

$$n = 2^{p-1}\left(2^{p}-1\right)$$

for some prime $p$ such that $2^p - 1$ is also prime (a **Mersenne prime**).
:::

:::proof
($\Leftarrow$, Euclid.) Let $q = 2^p-1$ be prime and $n = 2^{p-1}q$. Since $q$ is odd, $\gcd(2^{p-1}, q) = 1$, so multiplicativity of $\sigma$ gives

$$\sigma(n) = \sigma(2^{p-1})\,\sigma(q) = \left(2^{p}-1\right)(q+1) = q\cdot 2^{p} = 2\cdot 2^{p-1}q = 2n .$$

($\Rightarrow$, Euler.) Let $n$ be even and perfect. Write $n = 2^{m}q$ with $m \ge 1$ and $q$ odd. Coprimality of $2^m$ and $q$ gives

$$2n = 2^{m+1}q = \sigma(n) = \sigma(2^{m})\sigma(q) = \left(2^{m+1}-1\right)\sigma(q).$$

Thus $\left(2^{m+1}-1\right) \mid 2^{m+1}q$, and since $2^{m+1}-1$ is odd it divides $q$; write $q = \left(2^{m+1}-1\right)c$. Substituting,

$$2^{m+1}\left(2^{m+1}-1\right)c = \left(2^{m+1}-1\right)\sigma(q), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \sigma(q) = 2^{m+1}c = q + c .$$

Now $c$ and $q$ are both divisors of $q$ — indeed $c = q/(2^{m+1}-1)$ — and they are distinct because $m \ge 1$ forces $2^{m+1}-1 \ge 3 > 1$. So $\sigma(q) \ge q + c$ with equality only if $q$ and $c$ are the *only* divisors of $q$. Equality holds, so $q$ has exactly two divisors, forcing $c = 1$ and $q$ prime. Then $q = 2^{m+1}-1$ and $n = 2^{m}\left(2^{m+1}-1\right)$.

It remains to see that $m+1$ is prime. If $m+1 = ab$ with $1 < a < m+1$, then $2^{a}-1$ is a proper divisor of $2^{m+1}-1$ strictly greater than $1$, by the factorisation $2^{ab}-1 = (2^a-1)\left(2^{a(b-1)}+\cdots+1\right)$, contradicting primality of $q$. Setting $p = m+1$ gives the stated form.
:::

:::remark
The theorem is a classification only of the *even* perfect numbers. Whether an odd perfect number exists is open; it is known that one would have to exceed $10^{1500}$, have at least ten distinct prime factors, and satisfy a long list of further constraints, every one of which is derived from the multiplicativity of $\sigma$ in essentially the manner above.
:::

:::example[Testing for perfection]
Determine whether $496$ and $945$ are perfect, abundant or deficient.
:::

:::solution
1. $496 = 2^4\cdot31$ and $31 = 2^5-1$ is prime, so *Euclid–Euler* applies with $p = 5$: $496$ is perfect. Check: $\sigma(496) = 31\cdot32 = 992 = 2\cdot496$.
2. $945 = 3^3\cdot5\cdot7$, so $\sigma(945) = 40\cdot6\cdot8 = 1920 > 1890 = 2\cdot945$.
3. So $945$ is abundant — and it is the smallest *odd* abundant number, which is why abundance is so strongly associated with even numbers.
:::

:::definition[Aliquot sequence, amicable pairs]{#aliquot}
The **aliquot sequence** of $n$ is $n, s(n), s(s(n)), \dots$ where $s(n) = \sigma(n)-n$. A pair $(m,n)$ with $s(m) = n$ and $s(n) = m$, $m \neq n$, is an **amicable pair**; a cycle of length $k$ is a **sociable chain**.
:::

:::example[The amicable pair $220$ and $284$]
Verify that $220$ and $284$ are amicable.
:::

:::solution
1. $220 = 2^2\cdot5\cdot11$, so $\sigma(220) = 7\cdot6\cdot12 = 504$ and $s(220) = 504-220 = 284$.
2. $284 = 2^2\cdot71$, so $\sigma(284) = 7\cdot72 = 504$ and $s(284) = 504-284 = 220$.
3. Each is the sum of the other's proper divisors, and note the coincidence that both have $\sigma = 504$ — which is forced, since $\sigma(m) = m+n = \sigma(n)$ for any amicable pair.
:::

## The Carmichael function

<!-- section: carmichael-definition -->

Euler's theorem says $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1$ for every $a$ coprime to $n$. It does not say $\varphi(n)$ is the smallest exponent that works, and usually it is not: modulo $8$ every odd $a$ satisfies $a^2\equiv 1$, while $\varphi(8) = 4$. The sharp exponent has a name and a formula.

:::definition[Carmichael function]{#carmichael-def}
For $n \ge 1$, $\lambda(n)$ is the least positive integer $m$ such that

$$a^{m}\equiv 1 \pmod n \quad\text{for every } a \text{ with } \gcd(a,n)=1 .$$

Equivalently, $\lambda(n)$ is the exponent of the group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$: the least common multiple of the orders of its elements.
:::

The two descriptions agree because $a^m \equiv 1$ for all units is exactly the statement that $m$ is a common multiple of all element orders, and the least such is the lcm.

:::theorem[Carmichael's formula]{#carmichael-formula}
$\lambda(1)=\lambda(2)=1$, $\lambda(4) = 2$, and

$$\lambda(2^{a}) = 2^{a-2}\ (a \ge 3), \qquad \lambda(p^{a}) = \varphi(p^{a}) = p^{a-1}(p-1)\ (p \text{ odd}),$$

while for $n = 2^{a_0}p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$,

$$\lambda(n) = \operatorname{lcm}\!\left(\lambda(2^{a_0}),\ \lambda(p_1^{a_1}),\ \dots,\ \lambda(p_r^{a_r})\right).$$
:::

:::proof
The last formula first. By the Chinese remainder theorem, as in the proof of *$\varphi$ is multiplicative*, $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong \prod_i (\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{a_i}\mathbb{Z})^\times$. An element of a direct product has order the lcm of the coordinate orders, so the exponent of a product of groups is the lcm of their exponents, which is the displayed formula.

For odd $p$, the group $(\mathbb{Z}/p^a\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic of order $\varphi(p^a)$ — the existence of a primitive root modulo an odd prime power — and a cyclic group of order $m$ has exponent $m$, since a generator has order $m$. Hence $\lambda(p^a) = \varphi(p^a)$.

For $p = 2$ the group is cyclic only for $a \le 2$: $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is trivial and $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{\pm1\}$ has exponent $2$. For $a \ge 3$ one has $(\mathbb{Z}/2^a\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong \{\pm1\}\times\langle 5\rangle$ with $5$ of order $2^{a-2}$, so the exponent is $\operatorname{lcm}(2, 2^{a-2}) = 2^{a-2}$ because $a - 2\ge1$. That $5$ has order $2^{a-2}$ follows by induction from $5^{2^{k}} = 1 + 2^{k+2}t$ with $t$ odd, which squares to $1 + 2^{k+3}t'$ with $t'$ odd.
:::

:::example[Computing $\lambda$]
Compute $\lambda(n)$ for $n = 8, 15, 16, 65, 561$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\lambda(8) = 2^{3-2} = 2$: every odd square is $1$ mod $8$.
2. $\lambda(15) = \operatorname{lcm}(\lambda(3),\lambda(5)) = \operatorname{lcm}(2,4) = 4$, while $\varphi(15) = 8$.
3. $\lambda(16) = 2^{4-2} = 4$, while $\varphi(16) = 8$.
4. $\lambda(65) = \operatorname{lcm}(4, 12) = 12$, while $\varphi(65) = 48$.
5. $561 = 3\cdot11\cdot17$, so $\lambda(561) = \operatorname{lcm}(2,10,16) = 80$, while $\varphi(561) = 2\cdot10\cdot16 = 320$.
:::

## $\lambda$ against $\varphi$

<!-- section: carmichael-euler-relation -->

:::theorem[$\lambda$ divides $\varphi$]{#lambda-divides-phi}
For every $n \ge 1$, $\lambda(n) \mid \varphi(n)$, and $\lambda(n) \le \varphi(n)$. Equality holds if and only if $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic, that is if and only if $n \in \{1, 2, 4, p^{a}, 2p^{a}\}$ for an odd prime $p$.
:::

:::proof
The exponent of a finite group divides its order: if $m$ is the exponent and $g$ any element, the order of $g$ divides $m$, and by Lagrange every element order divides $|G|$, so $m = \operatorname{lcm}$ of element orders divides $|G|$ as well — each element order divides $|G|$, hence so does their lcm. With $|G| = \varphi(n)$ by *$\varphi$ counts units* and $m = \lambda(n)$ by definition, $\lambda(n)\mid\varphi(n)$.

Equality $\lambda(n) = \varphi(n)$ means some element has order $|G|$, that is $G$ is cyclic; this is the classification of the $n$ possessing a primitive root, namely $n = 1, 2, 4, p^a, 2p^a$. For any other $n$ the Chinese remainder decomposition has at least two factors of even order, or the factor $(\mathbb{Z}/2^a\mathbb{Z})^\times$ with $a\ge3$, and in both cases the lcm is a proper divisor of the product.
:::

:::corollary[A sharper Euler theorem]{#sharper-euler}
If $\gcd(a,n)=1$ then $a^{\lambda(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod n$, and this recovers *Euler's theorem* since $\lambda(n)\mid\varphi(n)$.
:::

:::proof
The first statement is the definition of $\lambda$. For the second, write $\varphi(n) = \lambda(n)k$; then $a^{\varphi(n)} = \left(a^{\lambda(n)}\right)^{k}\equiv 1^k = 1$.
:::

:::example[Where $\lambda$ beats $\varphi$]
Compute the order of $3$ modulo $16$ and compare with $\lambda(16)$ and $\varphi(16)$.
:::

:::solution
1. $3^2 = 9$, $3^4 = 81 = 5\cdot16+1 \equiv 1 \pmod{16}$.
2. So $3$ has order $4 = \lambda(16)$, and no unit modulo $16$ has order $8 = \varphi(16)$.
3. Euler's exponent $\varphi(16)=8$ is therefore twice as large as it needs to be; in a modular exponentiation this is a factor-of-two waste, which is why $\lambda$ rather than $\varphi$ is the exponent used in RSA key generation.
:::

:::remark
The gap can be enormous. For $n = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdots p_r$ a primorial, $\varphi(n) = \prod(p_i-1)$ while $\lambda(n) = \operatorname{lcm}(p_i-1)$, and the lcm discards all the shared factors of $2$: for $n = 510510$ one has $\varphi(n) = 92160$ but $\lambda(n) = 144$.
:::

## Carmichael numbers and Korselt's criterion

<!-- section: carmichael-numbers -->

Fermat's little theorem gives a primality test: if $a^{n-1}\not\equiv 1 \pmod n$ for some $a$ coprime to $n$, then $n$ is composite. The test has false positives, and the numbers that fool it for *every* base are the subject here.

:::definition[Carmichael number]{#carmichael-number-def}
A composite $n$ is a **Carmichael number** if

$$a^{\,n-1}\equiv 1 \pmod n \quad \text{for every } a \text{ with } \gcd(a,n)=1 .$$

Equivalently, $a^{n}\equiv a \pmod n$ for every integer $a$.
:::

:::theorem[Korselt's criterion]{#korselt}
A composite $n$ is a Carmichael number if and only if $n$ is squarefree and

$$p - 1 \ \big|\ n-1 \quad\text{for every prime } p \mid n .$$
:::

:::proof
($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $n = p_1\cdots p_k$ is squarefree with each $p_i - 1 \mid n-1$, and let $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Then $p_i \nmid a$, so Fermat's little theorem gives $a^{p_i-1}\equiv1 \pmod{p_i}$, and since $p_i - 1$ divides $n-1$ we may raise to the appropriate power: $a^{n-1}\equiv 1 \pmod{p_i}$ for each $i$. The $p_i$ are distinct primes, so by the Chinese remainder theorem $a^{n-1}\equiv 1 \pmod{n}$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $n$ is composite and $a^{n-1}\equiv 1$ for every $a$ coprime to $n$. By the definition of the Carmichael function as the *least* such exponent, $\lambda(n) \mid n-1$. Since $\gcd(n, n-1) = 1$, it follows that $\gcd(\lambda(n), n) = 1$.

*Squarefree.* Suppose $p^2 \mid n$ for some prime $p$. If $p$ is odd, *Carmichael's formula* gives $\lambda(p^{a}) = p^{a-1}(p-1)$ with $a \ge 2$, so $p \mid \lambda(p^a) \mid \lambda(n)$, contradicting $\gcd(\lambda(n),n)=1$. If $p = 2$, then $4 \mid n$, so $n$ is even, $n - 1$ is odd, yet $\lambda(4) = 2$ divides $\lambda(n) \mid n-1$ — again a contradiction. Hence $n$ is squarefree.

*The divisibility.* Now $n = p_1\cdots p_k$ with distinct primes. If some $p_i$ is odd, then $p_i - 1$ is even, so $2 \mid \lambda(n) \mid n-1$, forcing $n$ odd; and if all $p_i = 2$ then $n = 2$ is not composite. So $n$ is odd and every $p_i$ is odd. By *Carmichael's formula*, $\lambda(n) = \operatorname{lcm}_i(p_i-1)$, and each $p_i - 1$ divides that lcm, which divides $n-1$. Hence $p_i - 1 \mid n-1$ for every $i$.
:::

:::corollary[A Carmichael number has at least three prime factors]{#carmichael-three-factors}
Every Carmichael number is odd and is a product of at least three distinct primes.
:::

:::proof
Oddness was established inside the proof of *Korselt's criterion*. Suppose $n = pq$ with $p < q$ distinct odd primes. Korselt requires $q - 1 \mid n - 1 = pq - 1$. But $pq - 1 = p(q-1) + (p-1)$, so $q-1 \mid p-1$, which is impossible since $0 < p - 1 < q-1$. Hence $k \neq 2$, and $k = 1$ would make $n$ prime, so $k \ge 3$.
:::

:::example[Verifying $561$]
Show that $561$ is a Carmichael number and that $1105$ is as well.
:::

:::solution
1. $561 = 3\cdot11\cdot17$, squarefree, and $n - 1 = 560$.
2. $3-1 = 2 \mid 560$; $11-1 = 10 \mid 560$ since $560 = 10\cdot56$; $17-1 = 16\mid 560$ since $560 = 16\cdot35$.
3. All three conditions hold and $561$ is composite, so *Korselt's criterion* certifies it. It is the smallest Carmichael number.
4. $1105 = 5\cdot13\cdot17$ with $n-1 = 1104$: $4 \mid 1104$ ($=4\cdot276$), $12\mid1104$ ($=12\cdot92$), $16\mid1104$ ($=16\cdot69$). So $1105$ is Carmichael too.
:::

:::example[A number Korselt rejects]
Is $1729 = 7\cdot13\cdot19$ a Carmichael number? Is $15 = 3 \cdot 5$?
:::

:::solution
1. For $1729$: $n-1 = 1728$. Check $6 \mid 1728$ ($=6\cdot288$), $12\mid1728$ ($=12\cdot144$), $18\mid1728$ ($=18\cdot96$). All hold, so $1729$ *is* Carmichael.
2. For $15$: it has only two prime factors, so *A Carmichael number has at least three prime factors* rules it out at once. Concretely $5-1 = 4$ does not divide $14$.
3. Indeed $2^{14} = 16384 = 15\cdot1092 + 4 \equiv 4 \pmod{15}$, so base $2$ detects that $15$ is composite.
:::

:::remark
Carmichael numbers are rare but infinite in number: Alford, Granville and Pomerance proved in 1994 that there are at least $x^{2/7}$ of them below $x$. Their existence is why the Fermat test is replaced in practice by the Miller–Rabin test, which has no analogue of a Carmichael number — no composite is a strong pseudoprime to more than a quarter of the bases.
:::

## Dirichlet convolution

<!-- section: convolution-definition -->

The operation has been used throughout; here it is defined properly and its elementary properties are established.

:::definition[Dirichlet convolution]{#convolution-def}
For arithmetic functions $f, g$, their **Dirichlet convolution** is the arithmetic function

$$(f*g)(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d)\,g\!\left(\frac nd\right) = \sum_{\substack{ab = n \\ a,b \ge 1}} f(a)g(b).$$
:::

The second form is the one to use in proofs: it is manifestly symmetric in $f$ and $g$, and it generalises immediately to three or more factors.

:::proposition[Commutativity]{#conv-commutative}
$f * g = g * f$ for all arithmetic functions $f, g$.
:::

:::proof
In the symmetric form, $(f*g)(n) = \sum_{ab=n}f(a)g(b)$. Swapping the names of the summation variables turns this into $\sum_{ba = n}f(b)g(a) = \sum_{ab=n}g(a)f(b) = (g*f)(n)$, since the condition $ab = n$ is symmetric and multiplication in $\mathbb{C}$ is commutative.
:::

:::proposition[The delta function is an identity]{#conv-identity}
$f * \varepsilon = \varepsilon * f = f$ for every $f$.
:::

:::proof
$(f*\varepsilon)(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}f(d)\varepsilon(n/d)$, and $\varepsilon(n/d) = 0$ unless $n/d = 1$, that is unless $d = n$. The single surviving term is $f(n)\varepsilon(1) = f(n)$. Commutativity gives the other order.
:::

:::example[Convolution by hand]
Compute $(\mu * \sigma)(12)$ and $(\varphi * \varphi)(12)$.
:::

:::solution
1. Divisors of $12$: $1,2,3,4,6,12$ with $\mu$-values $1,-1,-1,0,1,0$ and $\sigma$-values $1,3,4,7,12,28$.
2. $(\mu*\sigma)(12) = \mu(1)\sigma(12)+\mu(2)\sigma(6)+\mu(3)\sigma(4)+\mu(4)\sigma(3)+\mu(6)\sigma(2)+\mu(12)\sigma(1) = 28 - 12 - 7 + 0 + 3 + 0 = 12$.
3. That is no accident: $\sigma = \mathrm{id}*u$, so $\mu*\sigma = \mu*u*\mathrm{id} = \varepsilon*\mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id}$, giving $12$.
4. $(\varphi*\varphi)(12) = \varphi(1)\varphi(12)+\varphi(2)\varphi(6)+\varphi(3)\varphi(4)+\varphi(4)\varphi(3)+\varphi(6)\varphi(2)+\varphi(12)\varphi(1) = 4+2+4+4+2+4 = 20$.
:::

## The ring of arithmetic functions

<!-- section: convolution-ring -->

:::theorem[Associativity of convolution]{#conv-associative}
For all arithmetic functions $f, g, h$,

$$(f*g)*h = f*(g*h), \qquad \text{with common value}\quad n \mapsto \sum_{abc = n} f(a)g(b)h(c),$$

the sum over ordered triples of positive integers with product $n$.
:::

:::proof
Expand the left-hand side using the symmetric form of the definition twice:

$$\big((f*g)*h\big)(n) = \sum_{de = n}(f*g)(d)\,h(e) = \sum_{de=n}\ \sum_{ab = d} f(a)g(b)h(e).$$

A pair $(d, e)$ with $de = n$ together with a pair $(a,b)$ with $ab = d$ is the same data as a triple $(a,b,e)$ with $abe = n$: given the triple, $d = ab$ is determined, and given the pairs, the triple is read off. So the double sum is exactly $\sum_{abc=n}f(a)g(b)h(c)$.

The same expansion on the right gives

$$\big(f*(g*h)\big)(n) = \sum_{ad' = n} f(a)\,(g*h)(d') = \sum_{ad'=n}\sum_{bc = d'} f(a)g(b)h(c),$$

and pairs $(a, d')$ with $ad' = n$ together with $(b,c)$ with $bc = d'$ are again the same data as triples $(a,b,c)$ with $abc = n$. Both sides equal the symmetric triple sum, so they are equal.
:::

:::theorem[Convolution makes a commutative ring]{#convolution-ring-thm}
The set $\mathcal{A}$ of arithmetic functions, with pointwise addition and Dirichlet convolution, is a commutative ring with identity $\varepsilon$. It is moreover an integral domain.
:::

:::proof
Addition makes $\mathcal{A}$ an abelian group, as it is pointwise addition of complex-valued functions. Convolution is commutative by *Commutativity*, associative by *Associativity of convolution*, and has identity $\varepsilon$ by *The delta function is an identity*. Distributivity is immediate from the definition, since

$$\big(f*(g+h)\big)(n) = \sum_{ab=n}f(a)\big(g(b)+h(b)\big) = (f*g)(n) + (f*h)(n).$$

For the domain property, let $f, g$ be nonzero and let $m$, $m'$ be the least integers with $f(m)\neq0$, $g(m')\neq0$. Consider $(f*g)(mm') = \sum_{ab = mm'}f(a)g(b)$. A term is nonzero only if $a \ge m$ and $b \ge m'$; combined with $ab = mm'$ this forces $a = m$, $b = m'$. So $(f*g)(mm') = f(m)g(m')\neq0$ and $f*g \neq 0$.
:::

:::theorem[Units of the Dirichlet ring]{#conv-units}
An arithmetic function $f$ has a convolution inverse if and only if $f(1)\neq0$. The inverse $f^{-1}$ is then unique and determined recursively by

$$f^{-1}(1) = \frac{1}{f(1)}, \qquad f^{-1}(n) = -\frac{1}{f(1)}\sum_{\substack{d \mid n \\ d < n}} f\!\left(\frac nd\right) f^{-1}(d) \quad (n>1).$$
:::

:::proof
If $f*g = \varepsilon$ then evaluating at $n=1$ gives $f(1)g(1) = 1$, so $f(1)\neq0$.

Conversely suppose $f(1)\neq0$. We construct $g$ with $f*g = \varepsilon$ by strong induction on $n$. At $n=1$, $(f*g)(1) = f(1)g(1)$ must equal $1$, which forces and permits $g(1) = 1/f(1)$. For $n>1$ the requirement is

$$0 = \varepsilon(n) = (f*g)(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}f(n/d)\,g(d) = f(1)g(n) + \sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\ d<n}}f(n/d)g(d),$$

which determines $g(n)$ uniquely as the displayed expression, every $g(d)$ occurring in it having $d<n$ and so being already defined. The resulting $g$ satisfies $f*g = \varepsilon$ at every $n$ by construction, and uniqueness at each step gives uniqueness of $g$.
:::

:::example[Inverting $u$ from the recursion]
Use the recursion of *Units of the Dirichlet ring* to compute $u^{-1}(n)$ for $n \le 6$.
:::

:::solution
1. $u^{-1}(1) = 1$.
2. $u^{-1}(2) = -\big(u(2)u^{-1}(1)\big) = -1$; likewise $u^{-1}(3) = u^{-1}(5) = -1$.
3. $u^{-1}(4) = -\big(u^{-1}(1) + u^{-1}(2)\big) = -(1-1) = 0$.
4. $u^{-1}(6) = -\big(u^{-1}(1)+u^{-1}(2)+u^{-1}(3)\big) = -(1-1-1) = 1$.
5. The values $1,-1,-1,0,-1,1$ are exactly $\mu(1),\dots,\mu(6)$, as *The Möbius sum identity* predicts: $u^{-1} = \mu$.
:::

:::corollary[Möbius inversion restated]{#inversion-restated}
$u$ and $\mu$ are mutually inverse units of $\mathcal{A}$, and *Möbius inversion* is precisely the statement that convolution by a unit is a bijection of $\mathcal{A}$ with inverse convolution by the inverse unit.
:::

:::proof
$\mu * u = \varepsilon$ by *The Möbius sum identity*, so each is the inverse of the other by uniqueness in *Units of the Dirichlet ring*. In any ring, multiplication by a unit $v$ is a bijection with inverse multiplication by $v^{-1}$; applied to $v = u$ this reads $g = f*u \iff f = g*\mu$, which is the inversion formula.
:::

:::remark
The ring $\mathcal{A}$ is in fact a local ring: its non-units are exactly the $f$ with $f(1)=0$, and those form an ideal, since a sum of two such has $f(1)+g(1)=0$ and a product has $(f*g)(1) = f(1)g(1) = 0$. It is also isomorphic to a ring of formal power series in countably many variables, one per prime — a fact that makes the Euler product look like a change of coordinates.
:::

## The group of multiplicative functions

<!-- section: multiplicative-convolution -->

Inside the ring $\mathcal{A}$, the multiplicative functions form a group. This is the cleanest statement of the chapter's organising principle, and everything else has been proved already.

:::theorem[Multiplicative functions form a group]{#mult-group}
The set $\mathcal{M}$ of multiplicative functions is an abelian group under Dirichlet convolution, with identity $\varepsilon$. In particular the convolution inverse of a multiplicative function is multiplicative.
:::

:::proof
Closure is *Convolution preserves multiplicativity*; associativity and commutativity are inherited from $\mathcal{A}$; and $\varepsilon$ is multiplicative and is the identity by *The delta function is an identity*. Every $f \in \mathcal{M}$ has $f(1) = 1 \neq 0$, so by *Units of the Dirichlet ring* it has a unique inverse $g$ in $\mathcal{A}$; it remains to see $g$ is multiplicative.

Let $h$ be the multiplicative function defined on prime powers by $h(p^a) = g(p^a)$ — this exists by the converse half of *A multiplicative function is determined by its prime powers*. Then $f*h$ is multiplicative, and at a prime power

$$(f*h)(p^a) = \sum_{j=0}^{a}f(p^{a-j})h(p^{j}) = \sum_{j=0}^{a}f(p^{a-j})g(p^{j}) = (f*g)(p^a) = \varepsilon(p^a).$$

Two multiplicative functions agreeing on all prime powers are equal, so $f*h = \varepsilon$. By uniqueness of inverses, $h = g$, and $g$ is multiplicative.
:::

:::corollary[The completely multiplicative inverse]{#cm-inverse}
If $f$ is completely multiplicative then $f^{-1} = \mu f$, the pointwise product. In particular $u^{-1} = \mu$ and $\mathrm{id}^{-1} = \mu\,\mathrm{id}$.
:::

:::proof
Both $\mu f$ and $f$ are multiplicative — the first by *Products and quotients* — so $(\mu f) * f$ is multiplicative and may be checked at prime powers. There,

$$\big((\mu f)*f\big)(p^{a}) = \sum_{j=0}^{a}\mu(p^{j})f(p^{j})f(p^{a-j}) = f(p^{a}) - f(p)f(p^{a-1}),$$

since $\mu(p^j) = 0$ for $j\ge2$ and $f(p^j)f(p^{a-j}) = f(p^a)$ by complete multiplicativity. For $a \ge 1$ the two terms are both $f(p^a)$ and cancel, and for $a = 0$ the value is $1$. That is $\varepsilon$.
:::

:::example[Convolution identities as algebra]
Derive $\varphi = \mu * \mathrm{id}$ and $\sum_{d \mid n}\mu(d)d(n/d) = 1$ purely by algebra in $\mathcal{M}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Gauss's identity is $\varphi * u = \mathrm{id}$. Convolving both sides with $u^{-1} = \mu$ gives $\varphi = \mu*\mathrm{id}$.
2. $d = u*u$, so $\mu * d = \mu*u*u = \varepsilon*u = u$.
3. Evaluating the second at any $n$ gives $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,d(n/d) = u(n) = 1$.
4. Similarly $\mu * \sigma = \mu*u*\mathrm{id} = \mathrm{id}$, so $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\sigma(n/d) = n$.
:::

:::example[An inverse computed at prime powers]
Find the convolution inverse of $d$.
:::

:::solution
1. $d = u * u$, so $d^{-1} = \mu * \mu$ by uniqueness of inverses in a commutative ring.
2. At a prime power, $(\mu*\mu)(p^a) = \sum_{j}\mu(p^j)\mu(p^{a-j})$, which is $1$ for $a=0$, $-2$ for $a = 1$, $1$ for $a = 2$, and $0$ for $a\ge3$.
3. So $d^{-1}$ is the multiplicative function with $d^{-1}(p) = -2$, $d^{-1}(p^2) = 1$, and $d^{-1}(p^a)=0$ for $a\ge3$; for instance $d^{-1}(12) = d^{-1}(4)d^{-1}(3) = 1\cdot(-2) = -2$.
:::

:::pitfall
The group $\mathcal{M}$ is closed under convolution but **not** under addition: $\varepsilon + \varepsilon$ is not multiplicative, as noted in *Products and quotients*. $\mathcal{M}$ is a subgroup of the unit group of $\mathcal{A}$, never a subring.
:::

## Mertens' three theorems

<!-- section: mertens-three -->

The chapter closes with the average behaviour of the primes. The three statements below were proved by Mertens in 1874, two decades before the prime number theorem, and two of the three are genuinely elementary: they need only Chebyshev-type bounds and careful summation. They are the quantitative form of "the primes thin out like $1/\ln x$".

:::theorem[Mertens' first theorem]{#mertens-first}
For $x \ge 2$,

$$\sum_{p \le x}\frac{\ln p}{p} = \ln x + O(1),$$

the sum over primes $p \le x$, with the implied constant absolute.
:::

:::theorem[Mertens' second theorem]{#mertens-second}
There is a constant $M \approx 0.2614972$, the **Meissel–Mertens constant**, with

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p = \ln\ln x + M + O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}\right).$$
:::

:::theorem[Mertens' third theorem]{#mertens-third}
With $\gamma$ the Euler–Mascheroni constant,

$$\prod_{p \le x}\left(1 - \frac1p\right) = \frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\ln x}\left(1 + O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}\right)\right).$$
:::

The three are a chain: the first is the raw input, the second follows from it by partial summation, and the third follows from the second by taking logarithms — except for the value of the constant, which is the one genuinely hard point and the only place where analytic machinery is unavoidable.

:::remark
The second theorem says the sum of reciprocals of primes diverges, and tells us *how*: like $\ln\ln x$, which is the slowest divergence one is likely to meet. To make $\sum_{p\le x}1/p$ exceed $4$ one needs $x$ beyond $10^{18}$. Compare $\sum_{n\le x}1/n = \ln x + \gamma + O(1/x)$: the primes are sparse enough to cost an entire logarithm.
:::

:::intuition
If "the probability that $n$ is prime" is $1/\ln n$, then $\sum_{p\le x}1/p$ should behave like $\int_2^x \frac{dt}{t\ln t} = \ln\ln x + C$, which is exactly Mertens' second theorem. The heuristic gets the shape right and says nothing about the constant $M$ — which is the honest summary of the situation.
:::

## Proving the three theorems

<!-- section: mertens-proofs -->

:::lemma[Legendre's factorial formula]{#legendre-formula}
For $n \ge 1$,

$$\ln (n!) = \sum_{p \le n}\ \sum_{k \ge 1}\left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor \ln p .$$
:::

:::proof
By unique factorisation, $n! = \prod_{p\le n}p^{v_p}$ where $v_p$ is the exponent of $p$ in $n!$. Counting the multiples of $p$, then of $p^2$, and so on among $1, \dots, n$, each multiple of $p^k$ contributing one to the count for every $j \le k$, gives $v_p = \sum_{k\ge1}\lfloor n/p^k\rfloor$, a finite sum. Taking logarithms gives the formula.
:::

:::proof[Proof of Mertens' first theorem]
Apply *Legendre's factorial formula* and split off $k = 1$:

$$\ln(n!) = \sum_{p\le n}\left\lfloor\frac np\right\rfloor\ln p + \sum_{p \le n}\sum_{k\ge2}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^k}\right\rfloor\ln p .$$

The second sum is $O(n)$: bounding each floor by $n/p^k$ and summing the geometric series gives at most $n\sum_p \frac{\ln p}{p(p-1)}$, and that series converges, being dominated by $\sum_m \frac{\ln m}{m(m-1)}$.

In the first sum write $\lfloor n/p\rfloor = n/p + O(1)$. The error contributes $O\!\left(\sum_{p\le n}\ln p\right) = O(n)$, using Chebyshev's estimate $\sum_{p\le n}\ln p = O(n)$ — itself elementary, from the fact that the binomial coefficient $\binom{2m}{m}$ is divisible by every prime in $(m, 2m]$ and is at most $4^m$. Hence

$$\ln(n!) = n\sum_{p\le n}\frac{\ln p}{p} + O(n).$$

Stirling's formula in its crude form gives $\ln(n!) = n\ln n + O(n)$. Equating and dividing by $n$,

$$\sum_{p\le n}\frac{\ln p}{p} = \ln n + O(1).$$

Finally, for real $x$ the sum over $p \le x$ equals the sum over $p \le \lfloor x\rfloor$ and $\ln\lfloor x\rfloor = \ln x + O(1)$, giving the statement for all $x \ge 2$.
:::

:::proof[Proof of Mertens' second theorem]
Let $A(t) = \sum_{p\le t}\frac{\ln p}{p}$, so that $A(t) = \ln t + R(t)$ with $R$ bounded, by *Mertens' first theorem*. Write $\frac1p = \frac{\ln p}{p}\cdot\frac{1}{\ln p}$ and apply partial summation with the weight $1/\ln t$:

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p = \frac{A(x)}{\ln x} + \int_{2}^{x}\frac{A(t)}{t\,\ln^{2}t}\,dt .$$

Substituting $A(t) = \ln t + R(t)$, the first term is $1 + O(1/\ln x)$ and the integral splits as

$$\int_2^x \frac{dt}{t\ln t} + \int_2^{x}\frac{R(t)}{t\ln^2 t}\,dt = \ln\ln x - \ln\ln 2 + \int_2^{\infty}\frac{R(t)\,dt}{t\ln^2t} - \int_x^\infty \frac{R(t)\,dt}{t\ln^2 t}.$$

The infinite integral converges absolutely because $R$ is bounded and $\int_2^\infty \frac{dt}{t\ln^2 t}$ is finite, and the same bound makes the tail $O(1/\ln x)$. Collecting the constants into a single $M$ gives the theorem. The numerical value $M = \gamma + \sum_p\left(\ln(1-1/p) + 1/p\right)$ follows by comparing with the third theorem.
:::

:::proof[Sketch of Mertens' third theorem]
Take logarithms of the product and expand:

$$\ln\prod_{p\le x}\left(1-\frac1p\right) = \sum_{p\le x}\ln\left(1-\frac1p\right) = -\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p - \sum_{p\le x}\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{1}{k\,p^{k}} .$$

The double sum converges absolutely as $x \to\infty$, to a constant $-C$ say, with tail $O(1/x)$. By *Mertens' second theorem* the whole expression is $-\ln\ln x - M + C + O(1/\ln x)$, so

$$\prod_{p\le x}\left(1-\frac1p\right) = \frac{e^{C-M}}{\ln x}\left(1+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\ln x}\right)\right).$$

Everything to this point is elementary. What is *not* elementary is the evaluation $C - M = -\gamma$. **This is where the sketch stops being a proof**: identifying the constant requires comparing the Euler product of $\zeta$ near $s = 1$ with the pole $\zeta(s) \sim 1/(s-1)$ and using $\Gamma$-function asymptotics, or equivalently invoking the prime number theorem in the form $\sum_{p\le x}\ln p = x + o(x/\ln x)$. Assuming that input, the constant comes out as $e^{-\gamma}$ and the theorem follows.
:::

:::remark
It is worth being precise about what is assumed where. Mertens' first and second theorems are proved above from Stirling's formula and Chebyshev's bound, both elementary; no zero-free region and no prime number theorem is used, and indeed Mertens proved them before the PNT existed. Only the *constant* in the third theorem needs more, and the shape $c/\ln x$ of the third theorem does not.
:::

## What the theorems are used for

<!-- section: mertens-applications -->

:::proposition[The density of coprime pairs to a primorial]{#primorial-density}
Let $P_r = p_1p_2\cdots p_r$ be the product of the first $r$ primes. Then

$$\frac{\varphi(P_r)}{P_r} = \prod_{i \le r}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_i}\right) \sim \frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\ln p_r} \longrightarrow 0 .$$

Hence $\liminf_{n} \varphi(n)/n = 0$.
:::

:::proof
The product formula is *Euler's product formula* applied to the squarefree $P_r$. The asymptotic is *Mertens' third theorem* with $x = p_r$, since the primes $\le p_r$ are exactly $p_1,\dots,p_r$. The right-hand side tends to $0$ because there are infinitely many primes, so $p_r \to\infty$, and exhibiting a sequence along which $\varphi(n)/n \to 0$ establishes the liminf.
:::

:::example[How small can $\varphi(n)/n$ get?]
Estimate $\varphi(n)/n$ for $n = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13 = 30030$ and compare with Mertens' prediction.
:::

:::solution
1. Exactly: $\varphi(30030) = 1\cdot2\cdot4\cdot6\cdot10\cdot12 = 5760$, so $\varphi(30030)/30030 = 5760/30030 = 0.19181\ldots$
2. Mertens predicts $e^{-\gamma}/\ln 13 = 0.5615/2.5649 = 0.2189$.
3. The agreement is about $14\%$ — unimpressive, and exactly what one expects from an asymptotic with a relative error of order $1/\ln x = 0.39$ at this size. The approximation is a statement about $p_r \to\infty$, not about $p_r = 13$.
:::

:::proposition[Sieve heuristic bound]{#sieve-heuristic}
The number of integers in $[1,x]$ with no prime factor below $z$ is at most $C\,x\,\prod_{p<z}(1-1/p) + O(2^{\pi(z)})$ for an absolute constant $C$, which by *Mertens' third theorem* is $O(x/\ln z)$.
:::

:::proof
By inclusion–exclusion over the squarefree $d$ built from primes below $z$, the count is $\sum_{d} \mu(d)\lfloor x/d\rfloor$, which equals $x\prod_{p<z}(1-1/p)$ plus an error of at most one per term, and there are $2^{\pi(z)}$ terms. Applying *Mertens' third theorem* to the product gives the stated order $x\,e^{-\gamma}/\ln z$.
:::

:::remark
Taking $z = \sqrt x$ in the last proposition would count the primes up to $x$ and give $\pi(x) = O(x/\ln x)$ — except that the error term $2^{\pi(\sqrt x)}$ swamps the main term long before that. Controlling that error is the entire subject of sieve theory, and the Legendre sieve above is its crudest form.
:::

## Mertens, the zeta function and the Euler product

<!-- section: mertens-zeta-relation -->

The third theorem has a clean interpretation in terms of $\zeta$, which also explains where $e^{-\gamma}$ comes from.

:::proposition[The product and the pole]{#product-pole}
For $s > 1$ real,

$$\prod_p\left(1-\frac1{p^{s}}\right)^{-1} = \zeta(s) = \frac{1}{s-1} + \gamma + O(s-1) \quad\text{as } s \to 1^{+}.$$

Thus the Euler product diverges as $s \to 1^+$ at exactly the rate $1/(s-1)$, and the constant term of the Laurent expansion is $\gamma$.
:::

:::proof
The product formula is *Euler's product for zeta*. For the expansion, compare with the integral:

$$\zeta(s) - \frac{1}{s-1} = \sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{n^s} - \int_1^\infty\frac{dt}{t^{s}} = \sum_{n\ge1}\int_{n}^{n+1}\left(\frac1{n^s}-\frac1{t^s}\right)dt .$$

Each integrand is bounded by $s/n^{s+1}$ in absolute value, so the series converges uniformly for $s$ in a neighbourhood of $1$ and defines a continuous function there. Its value at $s = 1$ is $\sum_n \int_n^{n+1}(1/n - 1/t)\,dt = \lim_N\left(\sum_{n\le N}1/n - \ln N\right) = \gamma$, which is the definition of the Euler–Mascheroni constant.
:::

:::remark
Compare the two appearances of $\gamma$. In *The product and the pole* it arises from the difference between a harmonic sum and a logarithm; in *Mertens' third theorem* it appears in the exponent, $e^{-\gamma}$. They are the same constant for the same reason: the finite Euler product $\prod_{p\le x}(1-1/p)^{-1}$ must approximate $\zeta(s)$ with $s-1 \approx 1/\ln x$, and matching $\zeta(s) \approx 1/(s-1) + \gamma$ against $\ln x$ produces exactly the factor $e^{\gamma}$. Making that matching rigorous is the analytic step the sketch above declined to take.
:::

:::proposition[Möbius and the reciprocal of zeta]{#mobius-zeta}
For $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$, $\sum_{n\ge1}\mu(n)n^{-s} = 1/\zeta(s)$, and the statement $M(x) = o(x)$ is equivalent to $\zeta(1+it)\neq0$ for all real $t$, hence to the prime number theorem.
:::

:::proof
The series identity is *The series of $\mu$, $d$ and $\sigma_k$*. For the equivalence we only prove the easy direction: if $M(x) = o(x)$ then partial summation shows $\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}$ converges for $\mathrm{Re}(s) \ge 1$, so $1/\zeta(s)$ extends continuously to the line $\mathrm{Re}(s)=1$, forcing $\zeta(1+it)\neq0$. The converse, that non-vanishing on the line implies $M(x) = o(x)$, is a Tauberian theorem and is not proved here.
:::

:::example[Reading off a density from a zeta quotient]
The Dirichlet series of $\mu^2$ is $\zeta(s)/\zeta(2s)$. What density does that predict, and why?
:::

:::solution
1. As $s \to 1^+$, $\zeta(s)$ has a simple pole with residue $1$ while $\zeta(2s)\to\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$.
2. So $\zeta(s)/\zeta(2s) \sim \frac{6/\pi^2}{s-1}$: a simple pole of residue $6/\pi^2$.
3. A residue $c$ at $s=1$ corresponds to a summatory function $\sim cx$, so the squarefree integers have density $6/\pi^2 \approx 0.6079$ — matching the direct count in *Counting squarefree integers up to $x$*.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading "multiplicative" as linear.** $f(m+n)$ has nothing to do with $f(m)$ and $f(n)$; and $f(mn) = f(m)f(n)$ needs $\gcd(m,n)=1$ unless $f$ is *completely* multiplicative. $\varphi(4) = 2$, not $\varphi(2)^2 = 1$.

- **Forgetting the exponent in $\varphi(p^a)$.** The value is $p^a - p^{a-1}$, not $p-1$. The bracket form $n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p)$ sees only the distinct primes because the exponents are already inside the leading $n$.

- **Checking an identity at primes only.** Two multiplicative functions can agree at every prime and differ everywhere else; the correct check is at every prime *power*. This is what separates $d$ from $2^{\omega}$, which agree at all primes.

- **Writing $\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)g(d)$ in the inversion formula.** The pairing is $\mu(d)\,g(n/d)$; the arguments must multiply to $n$.

- **Applying Möbius inversion to one value of $n$.** The hypothesis $g = f*u$ must hold at every positive integer; knowing it at a single $n$ says nothing.

- **Assuming $\lambda(n) = \varphi(n)$.** Equality holds only when a primitive root exists, that is for $n = 1, 2, 4, p^a, 2p^a$. For $n = 15$ the correct exponent is $4$, not $8$.

- **Treating $\varepsilon$ and $u$ as the same thing.** $\varepsilon$ is the convolution identity and is supported at $n = 1$; $u$ is constantly $1$ and is the *summing* operator. Convolving with $u$ takes divisor sums; convolving with $\varepsilon$ does nothing.

- **Calling every Fermat pseudoprime a Carmichael number.** A Carmichael number must fool the test for *every* coprime base, which by *Korselt's criterion* forces squarefreeness and at least three prime factors. $341 = 11\cdot31$ is a pseudoprime to base $2$ only.

- **Expecting $\sigma(n)/n$ or $\varphi(n)/n$ to converge.** Neither has a limit; both have a whole interval of limit points. Only averages and the extreme behaviour described by Mertens' theorems are stable.

- **Adding multiplicative functions.** A sum of two multiplicative functions has value $2$ at $n=1$ and so is never multiplicative. The closure properties are pointwise product, quotient by a nowhere-zero function, and Dirichlet convolution.
