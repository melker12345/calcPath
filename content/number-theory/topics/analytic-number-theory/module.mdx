# Analytic Number Theory

The Riemann zeta function, the prime number theorem, Dirichlet L-functions, and Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions.

The Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, with analytic continuation to the whole complex plane except a simple pole at $s = 1$.

It encodes the distribution of primes via its Euler product $\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$.

The Riemann hypothesis (all nontrivial zeros have $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$) is one of the most famous open problems, with consequences for the error term in the prime number theorem.

Zeta is the prototype L-function, and its study is central to analytic number theory.

## Definition and Euler Product

<!-- section: zeta-definition -->

For $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$,
$$
\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}.
$$

The product is over all primes $p$; the equality is exactly unique factorization (every $n$ has unique prime factors, so the geometric series for each prime multiply together to rebuild the whole sum — see the worked example below).

At $s = 1$ the series is the harmonic series $\sum 1/n$, which diverges; and the prime harmonic sum $\sum_p 1/p$ diverges too (Euler's stronger form of Euclid's theorem), which already tells you there are infinitely many primes.

Here "$\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$" is not optional: it is exactly the condition that makes the series converge absolutely. Writing $s = \sigma + it$, the term $|n^{-s}| = n^{-\sigma}$, so $\sum_n n^{-\sigma}$ converges precisely when $\sigma > 1$. For $\sigma \le 1$ the defining sum diverges and $\zeta(s)$ must be reached by analytic continuation (next section).

**ELI5**

- $\zeta(s)$ is the "sum of 1 over $n$ to the $s$", a knob-tunable version of the harmonic series: turn the knob $s$ up and the tail terms shrink faster, so the sum converges; set $s = 1$ and it blows up.
- The Euler product says "every number is built from primes, one independent choice of exponent per prime". Multiplying out the geometric series for each prime rebuilds every integer exactly once — that is unique factorization written as an identity.

**Worked Example: why the sum equals the product.**

Take one prime factor and expand it as a geometric series (valid because $|p^{-s}| = p^{-\sigma} < 1$ when $\sigma > 1$):
$$
(1 - p^{-s})^{-1} = 1 + p^{-s} + p^{-2s} + p^{-3s} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} p^{-ks}.
$$
Now multiply these over all primes. A single term of the expanded product picks one power $p^{-k_p s}$ from each prime and multiplies them:
$$
\prod_p p^{-k_p s} = \left(\prod_p p^{k_p}\right)^{-s} = n^{-s}, \qquad n = \prod_p p^{k_p}.
$$
By unique factorization, each integer $n \ge 1$ arises from exactly one choice of exponents $(k_p)$, so summing over all choices gives $\sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$ with each $n$ counted once. Hence $\prod_p (1-p^{-s})^{-1} = \sum_n n^{-s}$.

Sanity check on the first few terms with $p = 2, 3$: $(1 + 2^{-s} + 4^{-s} + \cdots)(1 + 3^{-s} + 9^{-s} + \cdots)$ produces $1, 2^{-s}, 3^{-s}, 4^{-s}, 6^{-s}, 8^{-s}, 9^{-s}, \dots$ — exactly the integers whose only prime factors are $2$ and $3$, each once.

**Worked Example: $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$ (the Basel problem, solved by Euler in 1734).**

Euler's route: compare the power-series and product forms of $\sin$. Since $\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} = \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{n^2}\right)$ has roots at every nonzero integer, and also equals $1 - \frac{(\pi x)^2}{6} + \cdots$ from the Taylor series of $\sin$, matching the coefficient of $x^2$ gives
$$
-\frac{\pi^2}{6} = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \zeta(2) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}.
$$
Sanity check numerically: $1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{16} + \tfrac1{25} = 1.4636\ldots$, and partial sums creep up toward $\pi^2/6 = 1.6449\ldots$; the Euler product $\prod_p (1 - p^{-2})^{-1}$ gives the same value.

## Analytic Continuation and Functional Equation

<!-- section: zeta-continuation -->

$\zeta(s)$ extends to a meromorphic function on all of $\mathbb{C}$, with a single pole at $s = 1$ (simple, residue $1$: near $s=1$, $\zeta(s) = \frac{1}{s-1} + \gamma + \cdots$ where $\gamma$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant). "Meromorphic" here means holomorphic everywhere except isolated poles; "analytic continuation" means this extension is the unique holomorphic function agreeing with the convergent series on $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$.

The functional equation relates $\zeta(s)$ and $\zeta(1-s)$:
$$
\pi^{-s/2} \, \Gamma(s/2) \, \zeta(s) = \pi^{-(1-s)/2} \, \Gamma((1-s)/2) \, \zeta(1-s).
$$

Or in $\xi$ form, $\xi(s) = \xi(1-s)$ where $\xi(s) = \tfrac12 s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)$ is entire (the extra factor $\tfrac12 s(s-1)$ kills both the pole of $\zeta$ at $s=1$ and the pole of $\Gamma(s/2)$ at $s=0$).

This symmetry comes from Poisson summation applied to the theta function, or equivalently from contour integration.

**ELI5**

- Although the sum only converges for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, there is exactly one "smooth" way to extend $\zeta$ to the rest of the plane — like extending $\frac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots$ (only valid for $|x|<1$) to the single formula $\frac{1}{1-x}$ that works everywhere except $x=1$. The functional equation is a mirror across the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$ relating the value at $s$ to the value at $1-s$.
- The gamma factors are the "missing prime": they play the role for the point at infinity (the archimedean place) that $(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ plays for each finite prime, making the completed function perfectly symmetric.

**Worked Example: the trivial zeros $\zeta(-2k) = 0$ for $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$**

Read the functional equation at $s = -2k$ (a negative even integer). The right-hand side involves $\zeta(1-s) = \zeta(1+2k)$, a finite nonzero number, and finite $\pi$ and $\Gamma$ factors. On the left, the factor $\Gamma(s/2) = \Gamma(-k)$ has a **pole** (the Gamma function has simple poles at $0, -1, -2, \dots$). For the left side to stay finite and match the finite right side, that pole must be cancelled by a zero of $\zeta(s)$. Hence $\zeta(-2k) = 0$. These are the trivial zeros. (At $s = 0$ the same $\Gamma$ pole is instead cancelled by the pole of $\zeta(1-s) = \zeta(1)$, so $s=0$ is not a zero; indeed $\zeta(0) = -\tfrac12$.)

As a related regularized value, $\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}$: plugging $s = -1$ into the functional equation and using $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$, $\Gamma(1) = 1$, $\Gamma(-\tfrac12) = -2\sqrt{\pi}$ gives this value (the "$1+2+3+\cdots = -\tfrac1{12}$" that appears in physics is shorthand for $\zeta(-1)$, not a literal sum).

## The Riemann Hypothesis and Zeros

<!-- section: zeta-rh -->

The nontrivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ all lie in the critical strip $0 < \operatorname{Re}(s) < 1$. ("Nontrivial" distinguishes them from the trivial zeros at $s = -2, -4, -6, \dots$ found in the previous section.)

The Riemann hypothesis (RH): every nontrivial zero has $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$ — they all sit on the critical line, the vertical line down the middle of the strip.

Known partial results: there are infinitely many zeros exactly on the line (Hardy, 1914), and more than $40\%$ of all nontrivial zeros lie on it (Levinson $\approx 34\%$, Conrey $> 40\%$). Because $\zeta(\bar s) = \overline{\zeta(s)}$ and the functional equation is symmetric about the line, zeros come in symmetric packages: if $\rho$ is a zero, so are $\bar\rho$, $1-\rho$, and $1-\bar\rho$.

The trivial zeros at negative even integers come from the Gamma poles in the functional equation, as shown above.

The prime number theorem is equivalent to the weaker statement that there are no zeros on the boundary line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$; RH is the much stronger claim that pins them all to $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$.

**ELI5**

- The zeros of zeta are like tuning forks: each one adds a "wave" of a certain frequency to the count of primes. RH says all the forks are the same size (amplitude $\sim \sqrt{x}$), so no single wave dominates and $\pi(x) \approx \operatorname{li}(x)$ as accurately as possible. A zero off the line would be a louder fork, making the primes clump or thin out more than expected.
- It is arguably the most important unsolved problem in pure mathematics — a Clay Millennium Prize problem worth one million dollars.

**Worked Example: locating and verifying the first zero.**

The lowest nontrivial zero sits at $\rho_1 = \tfrac12 + 14.134725\ldots\, i$ (and, by the symmetry above, its conjugate $\tfrac12 - 14.134725\ldots\, i$). It has $\operatorname{Re}(\rho_1) = 1/2$, consistent with RH. The next few are at heights $t \approx 21.022$, $25.011$, $30.425$, all with real part $1/2$. RH has been verified numerically for more than $10^{13}$ zeros this way, none off the line — strong evidence, but not a proof, since there are infinitely many.

These zeros are not idle: the Hadamard product writes $\zeta$ (essentially) as $\prod_\rho (1 - s/\rho)$ over its zeros, and this product is what turns into the explicit formula for $\psi(x)$, the Chebyshev prime-counting function (see the PNT sections).

## Euler Product and Primes

<!-- section: zeta-primes -->

Taking the logarithm of the Euler product and expanding $-\log(1 - x) = \sum_{k\ge1} x^k/k$ with $x = p^{-s}$:
$$
\log \zeta(s) = \sum_p -\log(1 - p^{-s}) = \sum_p \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{p^{-ks}}{k}, \qquad \operatorname{Re}(s) > 1.
$$
Differentiating in $s$ (each term $p^{-ks}$ has derivative $-(\log p)\,p^{-ks}$) gives the logarithmic derivative
$$
-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}, \qquad \Lambda(n) = \begin{cases} \log p & \text{if } n = p^k \text{ is a prime power},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
$$
Here $\Lambda$ is the **von Mangoldt function**. This Dirichlet series is the bridge: its poles come from the pole and zeros of $\zeta$, and a contour integral of it (the explicit formula) converts those analytic features directly into a count of primes.

**ELI5**

- The primes are the "atoms" of the Euler product; every integer is a molecule built from them. So facts about the assembled molecule ($\zeta$'s growth, poles, and zeros) translate back into facts about the atoms (how primes are spread out).
- The logarithmic derivative $-\zeta'/\zeta$ is a "prime detector": it is a clean sum over prime powers, and it lights up (has a pole) exactly where $\zeta$ has its pole or a zero.
- If $\zeta$ has no zeros near the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$, the detector stays calm there, which forces the primes to be regularly spread with no anomalously large gaps.

**Worked Example: reading $-\zeta'/\zeta$ near $s = 1$.**

The pole of $\zeta$ at $s=1$ (residue $1$) becomes a pole of $-\zeta'/\zeta$: since $\zeta(s) \sim \frac{1}{s-1}$ there, $\zeta'(s) \sim -\frac{1}{(s-1)^2}$, so
$$
-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} \sim -\frac{-(s-1)^{-2}}{(s-1)^{-1}} = \frac{1}{s-1}.
$$
A simple pole with residue $1$. When Perron's formula turns $\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n) = \psi(x)$ into a contour integral of $-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\frac{x^s}{s}$, this residue contributes the main term $x$ (from $\operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\frac{x^s}{s} = x$). That is the analytic seed of $\psi(x) \sim x$, i.e. the PNT. Any zero $\rho$ of $\zeta$ contributes an extra pole of residue giving a term $-x^\rho/\rho$ — the oscillations.

As a consequence, the PNT ($\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$) is equivalent to $\zeta(s) \ne 0$ for all $s$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ (a zero there would put a rival pole on the contour, cancelling or distorting the main term). Wider zero-free regions to the left of that line give sharper error terms.

## Common Mistakes

- **The series $\sum n^{-s}$ converges for all $s$**: No — only for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$. Elsewhere $\zeta(s)$ is defined by analytic continuation, not by the sum.
- **RH is proved**: No, it is open (though verified numerically for more than $10^{13}$ zeros).
- **Trivial zeros are the only zeros**: No, there are infinitely many nontrivial zeros in the critical strip.
- **$\zeta(1)$ is a finite value**: No — $s=1$ is a pole (the harmonic series diverges); the divergence is Euler's signal of infinitely many primes.
- **The functional equation is simple/elementary**: It involves the Gamma function, which brings its own poles and rapid growth; the symmetry only looks clean in the completed $\xi(s)$.

The prime number theorem (PNT) states that the number of primes $\le x$, written $\pi(x)$, is asymptotically $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ (equivalently $\pi(x) \sim \operatorname{li}(x)$, the logarithmic integral $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x \frac{dt}{\log t}$, which is a better approximation).

Proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, using the fact that $\zeta(s) \ne 0$ on the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$.

It is the fundamental theorem on the distribution of primes, with many refinements (zero-free regions, explicit formulas from the zeros of zeta).

Equivalent forms involve the Chebyshev function $\psi(x) \sim x$, or the $n$th prime $p_n \sim n \log n$.

## Statement and Equivalent Forms

<!-- section: pnt-statement -->

$$
\pi(x) \sim \operatorname{li}(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x} \qquad \text{as } x \to \infty.
$$

Equivalent forms:

- $\sum_{p \le x} \log p \sim x$ (equivalently Chebyshev's $\psi(x) = \sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n) \sim x$);
- the $n$th prime satisfies $p_n \sim n \log n$.

Throughout, $f(x) \sim g(x)$ means $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)/g(x) = 1$ — the ratio tends to $1$, **not** that the difference tends to $0$. (Indeed $\pi(x) - x/\log x \to \infty$.)

**ELI5**

- Near a large number $x$, primes are about as dense as $1/\log x$: pick a random integer around $x$ and its "chance" of being prime is roughly $1/\log x$. Bigger numbers, rarer primes, but only logarithmically rarer.
- So there are about $x/\log x$ primes up to $x$, and consecutive primes are on average about $\log x$ apart.

**Worked Example: watching the ratio approach $1$.**

Compute $x/\log x$ (natural log) against the true prime count:

- $x = 10$: $\pi(10) = 4$ (namely $2,3,5,7$); $10/\log 10 = 10/2.3026 \approx 4.34$; ratio $\pi/(x/\log x) \approx 4/4.34 = 0.92$.
- $x = 100$: $\pi(100) = 25$; $100/\log 100 = 100/4.6052 \approx 21.7$; ratio $\approx 25/21.7 = 1.15$.
- $x = 10^6$: $\pi(10^6) = 78498$; $10^6/\log 10^6 = 10^6/13.8155 \approx 72382$; ratio $\approx 78498/72382 = 1.085$.

Sanity check: the ratios ($0.92, 1.15, 1.085, \dots$) are settling toward $1$, but slowly — exactly the "$\sim$ is a ratio, not a difference" caveat. The logarithmic integral does far better: $\operatorname{li}(10^6) = 78628$, off the true $78498$ by only $130$.

## Proof Ideas (Zero Free Region)

<!-- section: pnt-proof-ideas -->

The core input (Hadamard / de la Vallée Poussin): $\zeta(s) \ne 0$ on the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ (for $s \ne 1$).

Consequently $-\zeta'/\zeta(s) = \sum_n \Lambda(n) n^{-s}$ has no pole on that line except the one inherited from the pole of $\zeta$ at $s = 1$. That single pole, with residue $1$, is what produces the main term.

By a Tauberian theorem, or by contour integration via Perron's formula, one then deduces $\psi(x) \sim x$, which is equivalent to $\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$.

Pushing further, a **zero-free region** to the left of $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ gives a quantitative error term: the classical region yields
$$
\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O\!\left(x \exp(-c\sqrt{\log x})\right)
$$
for some constant $c > 0$.

**ELI5**

- Think of the pole at $s=1$ as a spotlight producing the main count $x$. A zero sitting on the line $\operatorname{Re}(s)=1$ would be a second spotlight of equal reach that cancels or distorts the first, thinning the primes or opening large gaps. The theorem says no such rival spotlight exists on the line.
- In the contour-integral picture you slide the integration line leftward past $\operatorname{Re}(s)=1$: crossing the pole at $s=1$ hands you the main term $x$, and each zero you approach contributes a smaller oscillating correction (the error).

**Worked Example: from zero-free width to error size.**

The classical zero-free region says $\zeta(\sigma + it) \ne 0$ whenever
$$
\sigma > 1 - \frac{c}{\log(|t| + 2)}.
$$
The width of the region shrinks as the height $|t|$ grows — the region hugs the line more tightly higher up. In the explicit formula, a zero at real part $\sigma < 1$ contributes a term of size $\sim x^{\sigma}$. Balancing the smallest available $1-\sigma \approx c/\log(|t|+2)$ against the range of heights $t$ that must be summed leads (after optimizing) to the term $x\exp(-c'\sqrt{\log x})$ quoted above. Sanity check on the shape: as $x \to \infty$, $\exp(-c'\sqrt{\log x}) \to 0$ slower than any power $x^{-\delta}$, so the error is $o(x)$ (enough for the PNT) but far weaker than the $O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})$ that RH would give — matching the intuition that a wider zero-free region (RH pushes it all the way to $\sigma = 1/2$) buys a smaller error.

## Explicit Formula and Zeros

<!-- section: pnt-explicit-formula -->

The explicit formula (Riemann–von Mangoldt) for the Chebyshev function $\psi(x) = \sum_{n \le x}\Lambda(n)$ is
$$
\psi(x) = x - \sum_\rho \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \log(2\pi) - \tfrac12 \log\!\left(1 - x^{-2}\right),
$$
where $\rho$ runs over all nontrivial zeros of $\zeta$ (summed symmetrically, pairing $\rho$ with $\bar\rho$). The last two terms are small/constant; the trivial zeros produce the $-\tfrac12\log(1-x^{-2})$ piece.

So the prime count is a clean **main term $x$** minus **oscillations coming from the zeros**.

RH is equivalent to the sharp bound $\psi(x) = x + O\!\left(\sqrt{x}\,\log^2 x\right)$.

**ELI5**

- The primes are not laid down smoothly; they carry ripples. Each zero $\rho = \beta + i\gamma$ contributes a wave: writing $x^\rho = x^\beta\,e^{i\gamma\log x}$, its size is $x^\beta/|\rho|$ and it oscillates in $\log x$ at frequency $\gamma$. The zeros are the "musical notes" whose sum is the prime music.
- If every $\beta = 1/2$ (RH), every wave is capped at amplitude $\sqrt{x}/|\rho|$, so nothing sticks out — the smallest possible wiggle. A zero with $\beta > 1/2$ would be a louder note, a bigger error.

**Worked Example: how the pair of lowest zeros makes a visible wave.**

Take just the first zero $\rho_1 = \tfrac12 + i\,\gamma_1$ with $\gamma_1 = 14.1347$, together with its conjugate $\bar\rho_1$. Their combined contribution to $\psi(x)$ is
$$
-\frac{x^{\rho_1}}{\rho_1} - \frac{x^{\bar\rho_1}}{\bar\rho_1} = -2\,\frac{\sqrt{x}}{|\rho_1|}\cos\!\big(\gamma_1 \log x - \arg \rho_1\big),
$$
using $x^{\rho_1} = \sqrt{x}\,e^{i\gamma_1\log x}$ and $|\rho_1| = \sqrt{0.25 + 14.1347^2} = 14.1435$. This is a genuine cosine wave in the variable $\log x$: amplitude $2\sqrt{x}/14.14 \approx 0.141\sqrt{x}$, oscillating with "period" $2\pi/\gamma_1 \approx 0.444$ in $\log x$. Summing such waves over all zeros reproduces the jumps of $\psi$ at prime powers. Sanity check: each term's amplitude scales like $\sqrt{x}$ (since $\operatorname{Re}\rho_1 = 1/2$), matching the RH error bound $O(\sqrt{x}\log^2 x)$ — the $\log^2 x$ accounts for how many zeros contribute meaningfully. The famous fact that $\operatorname{li}(x) > \pi(x)$ for all computed $x$ (a bias first shown to reverse only astronomically far out, by Littlewood/Skewes) is precisely the low zeros nudging the count in one direction.

## History and Equivalent Statements

<!-- section: pnt-history-equivalents -->

Timeline:

- Chebyshev (1850s) proved two-sided bounds $c_1\,x/\log x < \pi(x) < c_2\,x/\log x$ with explicit constants close to $1$ (elementary, but not the asymptotic).
- Riemann's 1859 memoir introduced the complex-analytic viewpoint, gave the explicit formula, and conjectured RH.
- Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin (1896) proved the PNT.
- Erdős and Selberg (1949) gave an elementary proof of the PNT — no complex analysis, no zeta function.

Many equivalent statements exist: $\zeta$ has no zeros on $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$; the Mertens sum $M(x) = \sum_{n\le x}\mu(n) = o(x)$ (where $\mu$ is the Möbius function); $\psi(x) \sim x$; and others.

**ELI5**

- Before 1896 we only had a "sandwich": $\pi(x)$ was trapped between two constant multiples of $x/\log x$, but the constants were not known to be $1$, so the exact density was open.
- Nailing the ratio down to exactly $1$ ("$\sim$") required the zeta function and the fact that its zeros stay off the edge line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ — or, later, the clever elementary bookkeeping of Erdős and Selberg.

**Worked Example: the engine of the elementary proof — Selberg's symmetry formula.**

Selberg's identity (1949) is
$$
\sum_{n \le x} \Lambda(n) \log n \; + \sum_{mn \le x} \Lambda(m)\Lambda(n) \;=\; 2x \log x + O(x).
$$
Everything here is elementary: $\Lambda$ and $\log$ only, no complex variable. The point is that the two sums are "dual" — if the first (a weighted prime count) were biased above its expected value on some range, the second (a count of products of two prime powers) would be biased below, and the exact balance $2x\log x$ forbids either bias from persisting. Erdős and Selberg turned this rigidity into $\psi(x) \sim x$. Sanity check on the main term's plausibility: heuristically $\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n)\log n \approx \int_1^x \log t\,dt \approx x\log x$ (using $\psi(x)\approx x$), and the double sum contributes another $\approx x\log x$, so their total $\approx 2x\log x$ — consistent with the identity, though the identity itself is proved unconditionally.

## Common Mistakes

- **PNT says primes are random with probability $1/\log x$**: A good heuristic (Cramér's model), but the theorem is a deterministic statement proved by analysis, not probability.
- **RH is needed for the PNT**: No — the PNT is a theorem, proved without RH. RH only upgrades the *error term* to the much stronger $O(\sqrt{x}\,\log^2 x)$.
- **$\pi(x) \sim x/\log x$ means it counts exactly**: The $\sim$ is asymptotic (ratio $\to 1$); the absolute error grows. For all currently computed $x$, $\operatorname{li}(x) > \pi(x)$, though Littlewood proved this inequality reverses infinitely often far out.
- **The elementary proof is easy**: The Erdős–Selberg proof uses no complex analysis but is quite intricate — "elementary" means "no zeta", not "short".
- **PNT for arithmetic progressions is just Dirichlet's theorem**: Dirichlet's theorem gives infinitude only; the quantitative PNT in AP (Siegel–Walfisz, Bombieri–Vinogradov) needs zero-free regions for the $L$-functions.

Dirichlet $L$-functions generalize the zeta function: for a Dirichlet character $\chi$ mod $q$,
$$
L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \chi(n)\, n^{-s} = \prod_p \left(1 - \chi(p)\, p^{-s}\right)^{-1} \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1),
$$
with the principal character giving essentially $\zeta$ (and its pole).

They are the "zeta functions" attached to abelian characters — of $\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$ or of ideal class groups — and their non-vanishing and zero-free regions are central to primes in arithmetic progressions, class number problems, and the distribution of primes in number fields.

Analytic continuation and a functional equation exist for every $L(s,\chi)$, and the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH) is the RH for all of them at once.

## Definition and Euler Product

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-def -->

For a Dirichlet character $\chi$ mod $q$ (a function on integers that is completely multiplicative, periodic with period $q$, and vanishes on integers sharing a factor with $q$),
$$
L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \chi(n)\, n^{-s} \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1).
$$

If $\chi = \chi_0$ is the principal character (value $1$ on integers coprime to $q$, else $0$), then $L(s, \chi_0) = \zeta(s) \prod_{p \mid q} (1 - p^{-s})$, which still has the pole at $s = 1$.

For nontrivial $\chi$ the series and Euler product converge for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, and $L(s,\chi)$ continues to an **entire** function (no pole) — the pole at $s=1$ disappears because the character's values cancel in the sum.

A character $\chi$ is **primitive** of conductor $q$ if it is not induced from a character of any proper divisor of $q$; imprimitive characters share an $L$-function with their primitive inducer up to finitely many Euler factors.

**ELI5**

- Like zeta, but each term $n^{-s}$ is tinted by $\chi(n)$ — a $+1$, $-1$, or a complex phase (root of unity) decided by $n$'s residue class mod $q$. Terms in "coprime-to-$q$" classes get a phase; the rest are switched off to $0$.
- The Euler product survives, just "twisted": each local factor becomes $(1 - \chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}$.

**Worked Example: the non-principal character mod 4.**

Mod $4$ the units are $\{1, 3\}$, and the non-principal character is $\chi(1) = 1$, $\chi(3) = -1$, $\chi(\text{even}) = 0$. So
$$
L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n \text{ odd}} \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\left[\frac{1}{(4k+1)^s} - \frac{1}{(4k+3)^s}\right] = 1 - \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{5^s} - \frac{1}{7^s} + \cdots
$$
Because the coefficients alternate and the terms decrease, this series actually converges for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ (conditionally), unlike $\zeta$ — a first hint that non-principal $L$-functions are better behaved (entire). At $s = 1$ it is the Leibniz series:
$$
L(1,\chi) = 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \tfrac17 + \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}.
$$
Sanity check: $L(1,\chi) = \pi/4 \approx 0.785 \ne 0$ — the non-vanishing at $s=1$ that (as we'll see) forces infinitely many primes into both classes $1 \bmod 4$ and $3 \bmod 4$.

## Analytic Continuation and Functional Equation

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-continuation -->

Every $L(s,\chi)$ continues to an entire function if $\chi$ is nontrivial, or to a meromorphic function with a simple pole at $s = 1$ if $\chi$ is principal.

There is a functional equation relating $L(s,\chi)$ to $L(1-s, \bar\chi)$ (note the **complex-conjugate** character on the right) with Gamma factors and a **root number** $\varepsilon$, a complex number of absolute value $1$ built from the Gauss sum $\tau(\chi) = \sum_{a \bmod q}\chi(a)e^{2\pi i a/q}$.

For a primitive character $\chi$ mod $q$, define the parity $a = 0$ if $\chi(-1) = 1$ (even character) and $a = 1$ if $\chi(-1) = -1$ (odd character). The completed function
$$
\Lambda(s,\chi) = \left(\frac{q}{\pi}\right)^{(s+a)/2}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+a}{2}\right) L(s,\chi)
$$
satisfies $\Lambda(s,\chi) = \varepsilon(\chi)\,\Lambda(1-s, \bar\chi)$ with $|\varepsilon(\chi)| = 1$, where $\varepsilon(\chi) = \tau(\chi)/(i^a\sqrt{q})$.

**ELI5**

- The twisted sum extends leftward by the same tricks as zeta (theta functions, Poisson summation, contours), but now carrying an extra "size parameter" — the conductor $q$ — and a character twist inside the Gamma factor.
- The root number $\varepsilon$ is the "sign" of the symmetry, a pure phase read off from the Gauss sum. For zeta it is simply $+1$.

**Worked Example: the mod 4 character is odd and self-conjugate.**

For the non-principal $\chi$ mod $4$: $\chi(-1) = \chi(3) = -1$, so it is **odd**, giving parity $a = 1$. Its values are real ($\pm 1$), so $\bar\chi = \chi$ and the functional equation relates $L(s,\chi)$ to $L(1-s,\chi)$ — the same $L$. The Gauss sum is
$$
\tau(\chi) = \chi(1)e^{2\pi i/4} + \chi(3)e^{2\pi i\cdot 3/4} = e^{i\pi/2} - e^{i 3\pi/2} = i - (-i) = 2i,
$$
so $\varepsilon(\chi) = \tau(\chi)/(i^{1}\sqrt{4}) = 2i/(2i) = 1$. Sanity check: $|\varepsilon| = 1$ as required, and $\varepsilon = +1$ means $\Lambda(s,\chi) = \Lambda(1-s,\chi)$ is symmetric about $\operatorname{Re}(s)=1/2$, just like $\xi(s)$ for zeta. (This character is the one attached to the field $\mathbb{Q}(i)$.)

## Non-vanishing and Zero Free Regions

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-nonvanish -->

$L(1,\chi) \ne 0$ for every nontrivial $\chi$ (Dirichlet) — the crucial analytic fact behind his theorem.

For a real primitive character $\chi$ (a quadratic character, attached to a quadratic field of discriminant $D$), the class number formula makes $L(1,\chi)$ explicit and manifestly positive:
$$
L(1,\chi) = \frac{2\pi h}{w\sqrt{|D|}} \;\; (D < 0, \text{ imaginary quadratic}), \qquad L(1,\chi) = \frac{2 h \log \varepsilon}{\sqrt{D}} \;\; (D > 0, \text{ real quadratic}),
$$
where $h \ge 1$ is the class number, $w$ the number of roots of unity, and $\varepsilon > 1$ the fundamental unit. Both are $> 0$, so $L(1,\chi) > 0$.

Zero-free regions look like zeta's, with one dangerous exception: a real character can in principle have a **Siegel (exceptional) zero** — a real zero very close to $s = 1$. No such zero is known, but Siegel's theorem only rules them out *ineffectively* (the constants cannot be computed), which limits how uniform the PNT in AP can be made.

**ELI5**

- There is no "bad" cancellation at $s = 1$ in the twisted sum, and that positive leftover is what forces a positive density of primes into each coprime class.
- Real characters correspond to quadratic fields, and $L(1,\chi)$ literally measures the class number — the size of the field's ideal class group. A large class number means a large $L(1,\chi)$.

**Worked Example: $L(1,\chi)$ for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1}) = \mathbb{Q}(i)$.**

The imaginary quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has discriminant $D = -4$, class number $h = 1$ (it is a PID), and $w = 4$ roots of unity ($\pm 1, \pm i$). Its associated real character is exactly the mod-$4$ character above. The class number formula predicts
$$
L(1,\chi) = \frac{2\pi h}{w\sqrt{|D|}} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 1}{4 \cdot \sqrt{4}} = \frac{2\pi}{8} = \frac{\pi}{4}.
$$
Sanity check: this matches the Leibniz value $L(1,\chi) = \pi/4$ computed earlier — two independent routes to the same number, and it is $> 0$, confirming non-vanishing. Because $L(1,\chi)$ is proportional to $h$, the formula also converts lower bounds on $L(1,\chi)$ into lower bounds on class numbers.

## Applications

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-applications -->

- Dirichlet's theorem on primes in AP (from $L(1,\chi) \ne 0$).
- Prime number theorem in AP (from zero-free regions for $L$-functions).
- Class number formula and lower bounds for class numbers.
- Artin's conjecture on primitive roots (known under GRH).
- Distribution of quadratic residues and non-residues, least quadratic non-residue bounds, etc.

The GRH for all Dirichlet $L$-functions implies strong, uniform error terms in AP and across many related problems.

**ELI5**

- Each $L$-function is a detector tuned to one "color" (residue class), assembled from the characters. Together they separate the primes by color.
- Non-vanishing at $s=1$ turns the detector "on" (infinitely many primes of that color); zero-free regions sharpen it into exact proportions.

**Worked Example: the orthogonality that isolates one class.**

To count primes $\equiv a \pmod q$, use character orthogonality: for $\gcd(a,q)=1$,
$$
\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi \bmod q} \overline{\chi(a)}\,\chi(n) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n \equiv a \pmod q,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise (for } \gcd(n,q)=1). \end{cases}
$$
So $\sum_{p \le x,\; p\equiv a} 1 = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi \overline{\chi(a)}\sum_{p\le x}\chi(p)$. The principal character $\chi_0$ contributes the main term (its $L$ has the pole $\Rightarrow$ the term $\sim \operatorname{li}(x)$); each nontrivial $\chi$ contributes $\sum_p \chi(p)$, which is *small* precisely because $L(s,\chi) \ne 0$ near $s=1$. Sanity check on the constant: there are $\varphi(q)$ characters, and dividing by $\varphi(q)$ hands each of the $\varphi(q)$ coprime classes an equal $\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\operatorname{li}(x)$ share — the equidistribution of the PNT in AP. The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem makes this uniform on average over moduli $q$ up to about $x^{1/2}$, using zero-density estimates for the whole family of $L$-functions.

## Common Mistakes

- **$L(s,\chi)$ is always entire**: No — the principal character has a pole at $s=1$, just like zeta.
- **All characters are real $\pm 1$**: No — when $(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^*$ is not $2$-torsion (i.e. $\varphi(d) > 2$ with elements of order $>2$) there are complex characters taking values in higher roots of unity.
- **The conductor is always the modulus**: For imprimitive $\chi$ the true conductor is a proper divisor, and $L(s,\chi)$ equals the primitive one's $L$ up to the removed Euler factors.
- **Siegel zeros are known not to exist**: A real character *might* have one; Siegel's theorem bounds how close it can be, but ineffectively. No example is known.
- **GRH for Dirichlet $L$ is proved**: No — it is open, exactly like RH for zeta.

Dirichlet's theorem (1837): if $a$ and $d$ are coprime positive integers ($\gcd(a,d) = 1$), then there are infinitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod d$.

This generalizes Euclid's proof of the infinitude of primes (recovered as the case $d = 1$).

The proof introduces $L$-functions $L(s,\chi)$ for Dirichlet characters $\chi$ mod $d$, shows $L(1,\chi) \ne 0$ for nontrivial $\chi$, and deduces that primes are equidistributed among the coprime residue classes (the PNT in AP is the quantitative version).

It is the first major result of analytic number theory and a seed of class field theory (abelian extensions correspond to ray class groups; here the classes mod $d$).

## Statement

<!-- section: dirichlet-ap-statement -->

If $\gcd(a,d) = 1$, then there are infinitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod d$.

Moreover these primes have density $1/\varphi(d)$ among all primes (the PNT in AP, quantitative version): the $\varphi(d)$ coprime classes each get an equal share.

**ELI5**

- In any arithmetic progression $a, a+d, a+2d, \dots$ whose start $a$ and step $d$ share no common factor, primes never run out.
- You cannot "hide" from primes by fixing a coprime remainder mod $d$ — every allowed lane on the highway keeps getting prime traffic, and in the long run equally.

**Worked Example: the two coprime classes mod 4.**

Take $d = 4$. The coprime classes are $a = 1$ and $a = 3$ (since $\gcd(1,4) = \gcd(3,4) = 1$), so $\varphi(4) = 2$.

- Primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$: $5, 13, 17, 29, 37, 41, \dots$ — infinite by Dirichlet.
- Primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$: $3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31, \dots$ — also infinite.

Each class has density $1/\varphi(4) = 1/2$ among odd primes. Contrast a **non**-coprime class: $\gcd(2,4) = 2$, and every term of $2, 6, 10, 14, \dots$ is even, so the only prime that can ever appear is $2$ itself — at most one prime, never infinitely many. This is exactly why the coprimality hypothesis is required.

## Dirichlet Characters and L-functions

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-functions -->

A Dirichlet character $\chi$ mod $d$ is a group homomorphism from $(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^*$ to $\mathbb{C}^*$, extended to all integers by $\chi(k) = 0$ when $\gcd(k,d) > 1$, and made periodic mod $d$. (Since $(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^*$ is finite, every value $\chi(k)$ for $\gcd(k,d)=1$ is a root of unity, so $|\chi(k)| = 1$ there.)

There are exactly $\varphi(d)$ characters mod $d$ — as many as the order of the group they are defined on.

The principal character $\chi_0$ takes value $1$ on integers coprime to $d$ and $0$ otherwise.

The $L$-function is
$$
L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \chi(n) n^{-s} = \prod_p \left(1 - \chi(p) p^{-s}\right)^{-1} \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1),
$$
with the usual modification of finitely many Euler factors if $\chi$ is imprimitive. For nontrivial $\chi$, $L(s,\chi)$ extends to an entire function.

**ELI5**

- Characters are "colorings" of the coprime residues mod $d$ by roots of unity that respect multiplication ($\chi(mn) = \chi(m)\chi(n)$). The principal one paints everything the same color.
- The $L$-function is zeta with each integer tinted by its character value; whether $L(1,\chi) \ne 0$ decides if a given color has "enough" primes.

**Worked Example: all characters mod 4, and the Leibniz value.**

There are $\varphi(4) = 2$ characters mod $4$, both determined by their value on the generator $3$ of $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^* = \{1,3\}$:

- $\chi_0$ (principal): $\chi_0(1) = \chi_0(3) = 1$, $\chi_0(\text{even}) = 0$.
- $\chi_1$ (non-principal): $\chi_1(1) = 1$, $\chi_1(3) = -1$, i.e. $\chi_1(n) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ for odd $n$ (check: $n=1 \to (-1)^0 = 1$; $n=3 \to (-1)^1 = -1$; $n=5 \to (-1)^2 = 1$).

For $\chi_1$,
$$
L(s,\chi_1) = \sum_{n \text{ odd}} \frac{(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{n^s} = 1 - \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{5^s} - \frac{1}{7^s} + \cdots,
$$
and at $s = 1$ this is Leibniz's series $L(1,\chi_1) = 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \tfrac17 + \cdots = \pi/4 \ne 0$. Sanity check: two characters, $\varphi(4) = 2$ — the counts agree, and the non-principal $L$ is nonzero at $1$, the hook Dirichlet needs.

## Non-vanishing at s=1

<!-- section: dirichlet-nonvanishing -->

The key analytic step: for every nontrivial character $\chi$ mod $d$, $L(1,\chi) \ne 0$.

A uniform argument uses the **Dedekind zeta function** $\zeta_d(s) = \prod_{\chi \bmod d} L(s,\chi)$ (the product over all characters, essentially the zeta function of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_d)$). Its coefficients are nonnegative, and $\zeta_d(s) \to +\infty$ as $s \to 1^+$ because the $\chi_0$ factor has a pole there. If some nontrivial $L(1,\chi)$ vanished, that zero would cancel the single pole from $\chi_0$, making $\zeta_d$ finite (even $\to 0$) at $s=1$ — contradicting a product of Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients that must blow up. Hence no nontrivial $L(1,\chi)$ can vanish. (For real characters, the class number formula gives $L(1,\chi) > 0$ directly; the subtle case is complex $\chi$, handled by the observation that $L(1,\chi) = 0$ would force $L(1,\bar\chi) = 0$ too, i.e. a double cancellation, which the single pole cannot supply.)

Once $L(1,\chi) \ne 0$ for all nontrivial $\chi$, taking logarithms and applying orthogonality shows
$$
\sum_{\substack{p \le x \\ p \equiv a \,(d)}} \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{\varphi(d)} \log\log x + O(1) \xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{} \infty,
$$
so there are infinitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod d$.

**ELI5**

- The principal $L$ carries a pole at $s=1$ — a single "source of infinity" that drives the divergence of $\sum 1/p$. If one nontrivial $L$ had a zero at $s=1$, it would plug that source, and one residue class would run dry of primes. Nothing is allowed to plug it, so every class stays wet.
- The non-vanishing itself is secured by positivity: a product/combination of the $L(1,\chi)$ is forced $> 0$ (for real $\chi$, literally a positive class number).

**Worked Example: $d = 4$ made completely explicit.**

Two characters: $\chi_0$ (pole at $s=1$) and $\chi_1$ (non-principal) with $L(1,\chi_1) = \pi/4 \ne 0$. Split the prime sum by orthogonality of these two characters:
$$
\sum_{p \equiv 1\,(4)}\frac1p = \tfrac12\Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_p \tfrac1p}_{\text{diverges}} + \sum_p \tfrac{\chi_1(p)}{p}\Big), \qquad \sum_{p \equiv 3\,(4)}\frac1p = \tfrac12\Big(\sum_p \tfrac1p - \sum_p \tfrac{\chi_1(p)}{p}\Big).
$$
The twisted sum $\sum_p \chi_1(p)/p$ **converges** (finite) precisely because $L(1,\chi_1) = \pi/4 \ne 0$ keeps $\log L(s,\chi_1)$ bounded as $s\to 1$. So both class-sums equal $\tfrac12(\infty) \pm (\text{finite}) = \infty$. Sanity check: adding them back gives $\sum_p 1/p$ over all odd primes, which indeed diverges — and each class carries half the divergence, the density $1/\varphi(4) = 1/2$. Hence infinitely many primes $\equiv 1$ and $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

## Quantitative Version (PNT in AP)

<!-- section: dirichlet-pnt-ap -->

The prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions: for fixed $d$ and $\gcd(a,d) = 1$,
$$
\pi(x; d, a) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(d)}\operatorname{li}(x) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(d)}\,\frac{x}{\log x},
$$
where $\pi(x; d, a)$ counts primes $\le x$ with $p \equiv a \pmod d$.

Proved by de la Vallée Poussin using zero-free regions for the $L$-functions. The one soft spot is a possible Siegel (exceptional) real zero for a real character; Siegel's theorem bounds its effect but only with **ineffective** constants, so the strongest uniform versions (Siegel–Walfisz) carry an uncomputable implied constant.

The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem recovers strength *on average*: the PNT in AP holds for almost all moduli $d$ up to about $x^{1/2 - \varepsilon}$, which is often as good as GRH would give for many applications.

**ELI5**

- Not merely infinite: the primes split *evenly* among the $\varphi(d)$ coprime remainders, each getting density $1/\varphi(d)$. No coprime class is favored in the long run.
- The proof needs the $L$-functions to have no zeros hugging the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ — the same "no rival spotlight" idea as for zeta, now for each character's $L$.

**Worked Example: the $1/4$ vs $3/4$ counts mod 4.**

With $d = 4$, $\varphi(4) = 2$, so each coprime class gets density $1/2$ of the primes. Predicted counts up to $x$: $\pi(x; 4, 1) \approx \pi(x; 4, 3) \approx \tfrac12\operatorname{li}(x)$. Check at $x = 10^4$: there are $1229$ primes $\le 10^4$; excluding $2$, the $1228$ odd primes split as $\pi(10^4; 4, 1) = 609$ and $\pi(10^4; 4, 3) = 619$. Sanity check: $609 + 619 = 1228$ (all odd primes accounted for), and the two counts are within about $0.8\%$ of the even split $614$ — close, and provably converging to exactly $1/2$ each as $x \to \infty$. (The slight lead for $3 \bmod 4$ is the well-known "Chebyshev bias" toward non-residues, a lower-order effect that does not change the $1/2$ limiting density.)

## Common Mistakes

- **The theorem needs no coprimality**: It does — $\gcd(a,d) = 1$ is required. If $\gcd(a,d) > 1$ the progression holds at most one prime (namely $a$ itself, if $a$ happens to be that shared prime).
- **Dirichlet characters are only the Legendre symbol**: No — there are $\varphi(d)$ characters mod $d$, including complex-valued ones when $(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^*$ has elements of order $> 2$.
- **$L(1,\chi) \ne 0$ is only about class numbers**: The class number formula proves it directly for *real* characters (positive class number $\Rightarrow$ positive $L(1,\chi)$); complex characters need the separate cancellation argument.
- **Siegel's theorem is effective**: The constant bounding the exceptional (Siegel) zero is ineffective, so results relying on it inherit uncomputable constants.
- **PNT in AP holds uniformly for all $d$**: For $d$ growing with $x$ you need Bombieri–Vinogradov (on average) or GRH for full uniform strength.
