# Analytic Number Theory

The primes are defined by a condition that mentions no analysis at all: $p > 1$ has no divisor other than $1$ and itself. Yet the only known descriptions of how they are *distributed* — how many lie below $x$, how they split between the residue classes modulo $q$, how large the $n$-th one is — come from studying a complex-analytic function. That is the subject of this chapter, and the hinge on which all of it turns is a single identity discovered by Euler,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{s}} \;=\; \prod_{p \text{ prime}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1},$$

valid whenever the left side converges absolutely. The left-hand side knows nothing about primes; the right-hand side is nothing *but* primes. The identity is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic rewritten as an analytic statement, and every result in this chapter is, at bottom, a consequence of pushing that equation into regions where one side or the other is hard to control.

The plan is to keep the elementary and the analytic honestly separated. A great deal is elementary: the convergence of the zeta series, the Euler product itself, the pole at $s=1$, Euler's proof that $\sum_p 1/p$ diverges, Chebyshev's bounds on $\pi(x)$, Mertens' estimate, the whole theory of Dirichlet characters and their orthogonality, and the reduction of Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions to one non-vanishing statement. All of that is proved here in full. A few results genuinely require complex analysis — the analytic continuation past $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 0$, the functional equation, the explicit formula, the final Tauberian step of the prime number theorem — and for those we give a *labelled sketch* that says exactly which machinery is being borrowed. Nothing is asserted silently.

Throughout, $p$ always denotes a prime, $s = \sigma + it$ is a complex variable with $\sigma = \operatorname{Re}(s)$, and $\log$ is the natural logarithm. We write $f(x) \sim g(x)$ for $f(x)/g(x) \to 1$, and $f = O(g)$ for $|f| \le Cg$ with some constant $C$ and all large $x$. Familiarity with the multiplicative functions of *Arithmetic Functions* — in particular $\mu$, $\varphi$ and the notion of a multiplicative function — is assumed.

## The series and where it converges

<!-- section: zeta-definition -->

:::definition[Riemann zeta function]{#zeta-def}
For a complex number $s$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$, the **Riemann zeta function** is

$$\zeta(s) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{s}}, \qquad n^{s} := e^{s\log n}.$$

The value $n^{-s}$ has modulus $n^{-\sigma}$, since $|n^{-it}| = |e^{-it\log n}| = 1$.
:::

The whole of the zeta function's behaviour is decided by that last observation: the size of a term depends only on the real part of $s$, while the imaginary part only rotates it. So convergence is a question about the real series $\sum n^{-\sigma}$, and that is settled by comparison with an integral.

:::theorem[Convergence of the zeta series]{#zeta-convergence}
The series $\sum_{n\ge 1} n^{-s}$ converges absolutely for $\sigma = \operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$ and diverges for $s$ real with $s \le 1$. Moreover, for real $s > 1$,

$$\frac{1}{s-1} \;\le\; \zeta(s) \;\le\; 1 + \frac{1}{s-1}.$$
:::

:::proof
Fix real $\sigma > 0$. The function $x \mapsto x^{-\sigma}$ is positive and decreasing on $[1,\infty)$, so for every integer $n \ge 2$,

$$\int_{n}^{n+1} \frac{dx}{x^{\sigma}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{n^{\sigma}} \;\le\; \int_{n-1}^{n} \frac{dx}{x^{\sigma}} .$$

Summing the right-hand inequality over $2 \le n \le N$ and adding the term $n=1$ gives

$$\sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{1}{n^{\sigma}} \;\le\; 1 + \int_{1}^{N} \frac{dx}{x^{\sigma}} = 1 + \frac{1 - N^{1-\sigma}}{\sigma - 1} \quad (\sigma \neq 1),$$

and summing the left-hand inequality over $1 \le n \le N$ gives $\sum_{n=1}^{N} n^{-\sigma} \ge \int_{1}^{N+1} x^{-\sigma}\,dx$.

If $\sigma > 1$ then $N^{1-\sigma} \to 0$, so the partial sums are bounded above by $1 + 1/(\sigma-1)$ and are increasing, hence convergent; and the lower bound tends to $1/(\sigma-1)$. Since $|n^{-s}| = n^{-\sigma}$, convergence of $\sum n^{-\sigma}$ *is* absolute convergence of $\sum n^{-s}$.

If $\sigma = 1$ the lower bound is $\log(N+1) \to \infty$, and if $\sigma < 1$ it is $\big((N+1)^{1-\sigma}-1\big)/(1-\sigma) \to \infty$. In both cases the series diverges.
:::

Two things are worth extracting. First, the bound $\zeta(\sigma) \le 1 + 1/(\sigma-1)$ is not a crude estimate that will later be improved: it is already sharp enough to locate the pole, and we shall use it again in the corollary *Simple pole at $s=1$*. Second, the divergence at $\sigma = 1$ is the harmonic series, and everything interesting in this chapter is downstream of the fact that the harmonic series *just barely* diverges.

:::intuition
Think of $\zeta(\sigma)$ for real $\sigma$ as a thermostat. Turn $\sigma$ up and the terms $n^{-\sigma}$ collapse fast, so the sum is barely more than $1$. Turn $\sigma$ down toward $1$ and the terms thin out so slowly that the total blows up like $1/(\sigma-1)$. The primes live at exactly this critical setting, which is why the pole at $s=1$ carries so much arithmetic information.
:::

:::example[Bounding $\zeta(2)$ by hand]
Use the integral comparison to bound $\zeta(2)$, and compare with the exact value $\pi^{2}/6$.
:::

:::solution
1. The theorem *Convergence of the zeta series* with $\sigma = 2$ gives $1 \le \zeta(2) \le 2$.
2. Sharpen it by summing the first three terms exactly and bounding the tail: $\sum_{n \ge 4} n^{-2} \le \int_{3}^{\infty} x^{-2}dx = 1/3$ and $\ge \int_{4}^{\infty}x^{-2}dx = 1/4$.
3. With $1 + 1/4 + 1/9 = 49/36 \approx 1.3611$ this gives $1.6111 \le \zeta(2) \le 1.6944$.
4. The exact value is $\pi^{2}/6 = 1.644934\ldots$, comfortably inside. The tail bound is what makes such estimates cheap: truncating at $N$ leaves an error between $1/(N+1)$ and $1/N$.
:::

:::definition[Dirichlet series]{#dirichlet-series-def}
A **Dirichlet series** is a series of the form

$$D(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}}, \qquad a_{n} \in \mathbb{C}.$$

Zeta is the case $a_{n} \equiv 1$. A Dirichlet series has an **abscissa of absolute convergence** $\sigma_{a}$: it converges absolutely for $\sigma > \sigma_{a}$ and not for $\sigma < \sigma_{a}$.
:::

:::proposition[Zeta is holomorphic on the half-plane]{#zeta-holomorphic}
The series $\sum n^{-s}$ converges uniformly on every half-plane $\sigma \ge 1 + \delta$ with $\delta > 0$. Consequently $\zeta$ is holomorphic on $\sigma > 1$, and there it may be differentiated term by term:

$$\zeta'(s) = -\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} \frac{\log n}{n^{s}} .$$
:::

:::proof
On $\sigma \ge 1+\delta$ we have $|n^{-s}| = n^{-\sigma} \le n^{-1-\delta}$, and $\sum n^{-1-\delta}$ converges by the theorem *Convergence of the zeta series*. The Weierstrass $M$-test therefore gives uniform convergence there. Each partial sum $\sum_{n \le N} n^{-s} = \sum_{n\le N} e^{-s\log n}$ is entire, and a locally uniform limit of holomorphic functions is holomorphic with termwise-convergent derivatives, by Weierstrass' convergence theorem. Differentiating $e^{-s\log n}$ in $s$ gives $-\log n \cdot n^{-s}$.
:::

:::remark
Holomorphy is not a technicality here; it is the entire reason zeta is useful. A merely continuous function agreeing with $\sum n^{-s}$ on $\sigma>1$ could be extended to the rest of the plane in infinitely many ways and would tell us nothing. A holomorphic function has at most **one** holomorphic extension to a connected larger domain, so "the value of $\zeta$ at $-1$" is a well-defined arithmetic quantity rather than a choice.
:::

## Continuation past the half-plane

<!-- section: zeta-continuation -->

The series defines $\zeta$ only on $\sigma>1$, and the most important point of the whole theory, $s=1$, is on the boundary. The first step is to peel off the divergence explicitly.

:::theorem[Continuation to $\sigma > 0$]{#zeta-cont-half}
The function

$$\zeta(s) - \frac{1}{s-1}$$

extends to a holomorphic function on the half-plane $\sigma > 0$. Explicitly, for $\sigma > 1$,

$$\zeta(s) = \frac{1}{s-1} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \delta_{n}(s), \qquad \delta_{n}(s) = \frac{1}{n^{s}} - \int_{n}^{n+1}\frac{dx}{x^{s}},$$

and the series $\sum_{n} \delta_{n}(s)$ converges locally uniformly on $\sigma > 0$.
:::

:::proof
For $\sigma > 1$ the integral $\int_{1}^{\infty} x^{-s}dx$ converges and equals $1/(s-1)$. Splitting it over the intervals $[n, n+1]$,

$$\zeta(s) - \frac{1}{s-1} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n^{s}} - \int_{n}^{n+1} \frac{dx}{x^{s}}\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_{n}(s),$$

which is legitimate because both series converge absolutely there. Now estimate a single $\delta_n$ for arbitrary $s$ with $\sigma>0$. Since $\delta_{n}(s) = \int_{n}^{n+1}\left(n^{-s} - x^{-s}\right)dx$ and

$$n^{-s} - x^{-s} = s\int_{n}^{x} \frac{du}{u^{s+1}},$$

we get $|n^{-s}-x^{-s}| \le |s| \int_{n}^{n+1} u^{-\sigma-1}du \le |s| \, n^{-\sigma-1}$ for $n \le x \le n+1$, hence

$$|\delta_{n}(s)| \;\le\; \frac{|s|}{n^{\sigma+1}} .$$

On a compact subset of $\{\sigma>0\}$ we have $|s| \le M$ and $\sigma \ge \delta > 0$, so $|\delta_{n}(s)| \le M n^{-1-\delta}$, a convergent bound independent of $s$. By the $M$-test the sum converges uniformly there, and each $\delta_{n}$ is holomorphic on $\sigma>0$, so the sum is too. It agrees with $\zeta(s)-1/(s-1)$ on $\sigma>1$, so it is *the* continuation.
:::

:::corollary[Simple pole at $s=1$ with residue $1$]{#zeta-pole}
$\zeta$ continues meromorphically to $\sigma>0$ with a single simple pole at $s=1$, of residue $1$. Equivalently,

$$\lim_{s\to 1} (s-1)\zeta(s) = 1, \qquad \zeta(s) = \frac{1}{s-1} + \gamma + O(|s-1|)$$

near $s=1$, where $\gamma$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Continuation to $\sigma>0$*, $\zeta(s) = 1/(s-1) + h(s)$ with $h$ holomorphic on $\sigma>0$. A holomorphic $h$ plus a simple pole is a simple pole, and multiplying by $s-1$ gives $(s-1)\zeta(s) = 1 + (s-1)h(s) \to 1$. Finally $h(1) = \sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac1n - \log\frac{n+1}{n}\right) = \lim_{N}\left(\sum_{n\le N}\frac1n - \log(N+1)\right) = \gamma$, the sum telescoping in its logarithms; this is precisely the definition of $\gamma$.
:::

The same peeling can be pushed further, and there is a slicker device that reaches $\sigma>0$ in one line.

:::proposition[The alternating (eta) representation]{#eta-rep}
For $\sigma>1$, with $\eta(s) = \sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}n^{-s}$,

$$\eta(s) = \left(1 - 2^{1-s}\right)\zeta(s), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \zeta(s) = \frac{\eta(s)}{1-2^{1-s}} .$$

The series for $\eta$ converges (conditionally) for $\sigma>0$, so this formula continues $\zeta$ to $\sigma>0$ directly.
:::

:::proof
For $\sigma>1$ all series converge absolutely, so we may rearrange:

$$\zeta(s) - \eta(s) = 2\sum_{n \text{ even}} \frac{1}{n^{s}} = 2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2m)^{s}} = 2^{1-s}\zeta(s),$$

since subtracting $\eta$ from $\zeta$ leaves twice the even terms. Rearranging gives $\eta(s) = (1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$. That $\sum(-1)^{n-1}n^{-s}$ converges for $\sigma>0$ follows from summation by parts: the partial sums of $(-1)^{n-1}$ are bounded by $1$, and $n^{-s}$ has bounded variation tending to $0$ along $[1,\infty)$ when $\sigma>0$.
:::

:::pitfall
The eta formula has *removable* trouble: $1-2^{1-s}$ vanishes at $s = 1 + 2\pi i k/\log 2$ for every integer $k$. At all those points except $s=1$, $\eta$ vanishes too and the quotient is fine, but one cannot see that from the formula alone. The formula is a convenient continuation, not a proof that $s=1$ is the only pole — that came from the theorem *Continuation to $\sigma>0$*.
:::

:::example[Recovering $\zeta(2)$ from the alternating series]
The alternating series $\eta(2) = 1 - \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 - \tfrac1{16}+\cdots$ sums to $\pi^{2}/12$. Use the proposition *The alternating (eta) representation* to deduce $\zeta(2)$.
:::

:::solution
1. At $s=2$ the factor is $1 - 2^{1-s} = 1 - 2^{-1} = \tfrac12$.
2. So $\zeta(2) = \eta(2)/\tfrac12 = 2\eta(2)$.
3. With $\eta(2)=\pi^{2}/12 = 0.8224670\ldots$ this gives $\zeta(2) = \pi^{2}/6 = 1.6449341\ldots$
4. The practical point: $\eta$'s alternating series converges (slowly, but it converges) at values of $s$ where $\zeta$'s does not, so this identity is how one computes $\zeta$ numerically near the critical strip.
:::

Beyond $\sigma>0$ one needs genuine machinery, and the payoff is a symmetry rather than merely a larger domain.

:::theorem[Functional equation]{#functional-equation}
$\zeta$ continues to a meromorphic function on all of $\mathbb{C}$, holomorphic except for the simple pole at $s=1$. Writing

$$\xi(s) = \pi^{-s/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{s}{2}\right)\zeta(s),$$

the **completed zeta function** $\xi$ satisfies

$$\xi(s) = \xi(1-s) \qquad \text{for all } s \in \mathbb{C}.$$

Unwound, $\zeta(s) = 2^{s}\pi^{s-1}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-s)\,\zeta(1-s)$.
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes the theta transformation and Mellin inversion; not elementary.* Start from $\Gamma(s/2)\pi^{-s/2}n^{-s} = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^{s/2-1}e^{-\pi n^{2}x}\,dx$, obtained by substituting $x \mapsto x/(\pi n^{2})$ in the Gamma integral. Summing over $n \ge 1$ for $\sigma>1$ and exchanging sum and integral (justified by absolute convergence) gives

$$\xi(s) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^{s/2-1}\,\omega(x)\,dx, \qquad \omega(x) = \sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\pi n^{2}x}.$$

The borrowed input is the theta transformation $\theta(1/x) = x^{1/2}\theta(x)$ for $\theta(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\pi n^{2}x}$, itself a consequence of the Poisson summation formula applied to the Gaussian. Splitting the integral at $x=1$ and applying the transformation to the piece over $(0,1)$ yields

$$\xi(s) = \frac{1}{s(s-1)} + \int_{1}^{\infty}\left(x^{s/2-1}+x^{(1-s)/2-1}\right)\omega(x)\,dx .$$

The integral converges for *every* $s$, because $\omega(x)$ decays like $e^{-\pi x}$; and the whole expression is visibly unchanged by $s \mapsto 1-s$. This both continues $\xi$ to $\mathbb{C}$ and proves the symmetry, with the two poles of $\xi$ at $s=0,1$ coming from the explicit fraction. The pole at $s=0$ is cancelled by the pole of $\Gamma(s/2)$ when one solves for $\zeta$.
:::

## The Riemann hypothesis

<!-- section: zeta-rh -->

:::definition[Critical strip, trivial and nontrivial zeros]{#critical-strip}
The **critical strip** is $\{0 \le \sigma \le 1\}$. The zeros of $\zeta$ at $s=-2,-4,-6,\dots$ are the **trivial zeros**; all other zeros lie in the critical strip and are the **nontrivial zeros**. The line $\sigma = \tfrac12$ is the **critical line**.
:::

:::proposition[Where the zeros can be]{#zero-location}
$\zeta(s) \neq 0$ for $\sigma > 1$; $\zeta(-2k)=0$ for every integer $k \ge 1$; and $\zeta$ has no zeros with $\sigma<0$ other than those.
:::

:::proof
For $\sigma>1$ non-vanishing follows from the theorem *Euler product* below: a convergent infinite product of nonzero factors is nonzero, which we verify there. For $\sigma<0$, use the unwound functional equation $\zeta(s) = 2^{s}\pi^{s-1}\sin(\pi s/2)\Gamma(1-s)\zeta(1-s)$. When $\sigma<0$ the argument $1-s$ has real part $>1$, so $\zeta(1-s)\neq 0$; also $\Gamma(1-s)$ has no zeros anywhere, and $2^{s}\pi^{s-1}$ never vanishes. So the zeros of $\zeta$ in $\sigma<0$ are exactly the zeros of $\sin(\pi s/2)$ there, namely $s=-2,-4,\dots$ (the zero of the sine at $s=0$ is cancelled by the pole of $\Gamma(1-s)$ at $s=1$ under the reflection, and $s=0$ is not a zero: $\zeta(0)=-1/2$).
:::

:::definition[The Riemann hypothesis]{#rh}
The **Riemann hypothesis** is the assertion

$$\zeta(\rho)=0 \ \text{ and } \ 0\le\operatorname{Re}(\rho)\le1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \operatorname{Re}(\rho)=\tfrac12 .$$

That is, every nontrivial zero lies on the critical line. It is **open**. It has been verified for the first $1.2 \times 10^{13}$ zeros by height, and it is known that a positive proportion of the zeros lie on the line, but no proof is known and none of the partial results approaches one.
:::

:::theorem[Von Koch's error term]{#von-koch}
The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement

$$\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O\!\left(\sqrt{x}\,\log x\right) \qquad (x \to \infty),$$

where $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_{0}^{x}\frac{dt}{\log t}$ is the logarithmic integral (principal value at $t=1$).
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes the explicit formula and Hadamard product theory.* Both directions run through the theorem *Explicit formula* below, which expresses $\psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho} x^{\rho}/\rho - \log(2\pi) - \tfrac12\log(1-x^{-2})$ as a sum over the nontrivial zeros. A zero $\rho = \beta+i\gamma$ contributes a term of modulus $x^{\beta}/|\rho|$. If every $\beta = \tfrac12$ the sum is $O(\sqrt x \log^{2}x)$ after controlling the zero density, giving $\psi(x) = x + O(\sqrt x\log^{2}x)$, and partial summation converts this to the stated bound for $\pi(x)$. Conversely, a zero with $\beta > \tfrac12$ forces $\psi(x)-x$ to exceed $x^{\beta-\varepsilon}$ infinitely often — an oscillation theorem of Landau's type — contradicting the error bound. Neither direction is elementary: both need the Hadamard factorisation of $\xi$ to know that the zero sum converges at all.
:::

:::remark
The error term is the whole content. The prime number theorem says $\pi(x)/\operatorname{li}(x)\to1$; what is unknown is the *rate*. Unconditionally the best known error is roughly $x\exp(-c(\log x)^{3/5}(\log\log x)^{-1/5})$, which is smaller than $x/\log^{A}x$ for every $A$ but enormously larger than $\sqrt{x}\log x$. The gap between those two bounds is exactly the gap between a zero-free region shaped like a thin sliver and a zero-free half-strip.
:::

:::example[Reading the error numerically]
At $x = 10^{6}$: $\pi(x) = 78498$, $\operatorname{li}(x) \approx 78627.5$, and $x/\log x \approx 72382.4$. Which approximation is better, and is the error consistent with von Koch's bound?
:::

:::solution
1. Error of $\operatorname{li}$: $78627.5 - 78498 = 129.5$, a relative error of about $0.165\%$.
2. Error of $x/\log x$: $78498 - 72382.4 = 6115.6$, a relative error of about $7.8\%$ — nearly fifty times worse.
3. Von Koch's bound at $x=10^{6}$ reads $\sqrt{x}\log x = 1000 \times 13.8 = 13815$. The actual error $129.5$ is far below it, as expected: the bound is an upper limit on the worst oscillation, not a prediction.
4. The lesson: $x/\log x$ is the right *shape* but the wrong constant at any finite height, because $\operatorname{li}(x) = \frac{x}{\log x}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\log x} + \frac{2}{\log^{2}x}+\cdots\right)$ and the correction $1/\log x$ is still $7\%$ at $x=10^{6}$.
:::

## The Euler product and the primes

<!-- section: zeta-primes -->

This is the section the chapter exists for. Everything before it is about a series; everything after it is about primes, and the bridge is one identity.

:::theorem[Euler product]{#euler-product}
For $\sigma = \operatorname{Re}(s)>1$,

$$\zeta(s) = \prod_{p} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1},$$

the product taken over all primes, converging absolutely. In particular $\zeta(s)\neq 0$ for $\sigma>1$.
:::

:::proof
Fix $s$ with $\sigma>1$ and let $P$ be a finite set of primes. Since $|p^{-s}| = p^{-\sigma} \le 2^{-\sigma} < 1$, each factor is the sum of a convergent geometric series,

$$\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p^{ks}} .$$

Multiply these together over $p \in P$. Each of the finitely many series converges absolutely, so the product may be expanded term by term and the resulting multiple series rearranged freely:

$$\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1} = \sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{r}\ge0} \frac{1}{\left(p_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}}\right)^{s}} = \sum_{n \in N(P)} \frac{1}{n^{s}},$$

where $N(P)$ is the set of positive integers all of whose prime factors lie in $P$. Here **both halves of unique factorization are used**: *existence* of a factorization says every $n \in N(P)$ is hit, and *uniqueness* says it is hit exactly once, so no $n$ is counted twice and the right-hand sum is a genuine subsum of $\zeta(s)$, not an overcount.

Now take $P = P_{y} = \{p \le y\}$. Every integer $n \le y$ has all its prime factors $\le y$, so $N(P_y) \supseteq \{1,2,\dots,\lfloor y\rfloor\}$, and therefore

$$\left|\zeta(s) - \prod_{p\le y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1}\right| = \left|\sum_{n \notin N(P_{y})}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\right| \le \sum_{n > y}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma}} .$$

The last sum is the tail of a convergent series, so it tends to $0$ as $y \to \infty$. Hence the partial products converge to $\zeta(s)$.

For absolute convergence of the product, note $\sum_{p}|p^{-s}| \le \sum_{n\ge2} n^{-\sigma} < \infty$, which is the standard criterion for $\prod(1-a_{p})^{-1}$ to converge absolutely to a nonzero limit. Non-vanishing follows: each factor is nonzero, and the limit of the partial products is nonzero because $\sum_p |p^{-s}|$ converges.
:::

:::intuition
Expanding the product is exactly the act of building an integer by choosing its exponent of $2$, then of $3$, then of $5$, and so on. The product is a shopping list with one aisle per prime; unique factorization is the statement that every integer appears at exactly one checkout.
:::

:::example[Truncating the Euler product at $s=2$]
Compute $\prod_{p\le7}(1-p^{-2})^{-1}$ and compare with $\zeta(2)=1.644934$.
:::

:::solution
1. $p=2$: $(1-\tfrac14)^{-1} = \tfrac43 = 1.333333$.
2. $p=3$: multiply by $(1-\tfrac19)^{-1} = \tfrac98$, giving $\tfrac32 = 1.5$.
3. $p=5$: multiply by $(1-\tfrac1{25})^{-1} = \tfrac{25}{24}$, giving $1.5625$.
4. $p=7$: multiply by $(1-\tfrac1{49})^{-1} = \tfrac{49}{48}$, giving $1.595052$.
5. The deficit $1.644934-1.595052 = 0.049882$ is exactly $\sum 1/n^{2}$ over those $n$ having a prime factor larger than $7$, the smallest being $n=11$: the tail estimate inside the proof of the theorem *Euler product* made visible.
:::

:::corollary[Euclid's theorem, analytically]{#euclid-analytic}
There are infinitely many primes.
:::

:::proof
Suppose there were finitely many. Then the product $\prod_{p}(1-p^{-\sigma})^{-1}$ is a finite product of real numbers, hence bounded as $\sigma \downarrow 1$ — its limit is the finite number $\prod_{p}(1-p^{-1})^{-1}$. But by the theorem *Euler product* it equals $\zeta(\sigma)$, which by the theorem *Convergence of the zeta series* satisfies $\zeta(\sigma)\ge 1/(\sigma-1) \to \infty$. Contradiction.
:::

That argument proves more than Euclid's, because it is quantitative: the primes must be numerous enough for their product to diverge. Squeezing the rate out of it is Euler's theorem.

:::theorem[Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals]{#prime-reciprocals}
$$\sum_{p} \frac{1}{p} = \infty .$$

More precisely, for real $\sigma > 1$,

$$\sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}} = \log\frac{1}{\sigma-1} + O(1) \qquad (\sigma \downarrow 1).$$
:::

:::proof
Take logarithms in the theorem *Euler product* for real $\sigma>1$, which is legitimate since all factors are positive reals:

$$\log \zeta(\sigma) = \sum_{p} -\log\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}\right) = \sum_{p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k\,p^{k\sigma}} = \sum_{p}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}} + R(\sigma),$$

using $-\log(1-x)=\sum_{k\ge1}x^{k}/k$ for $|x|<1$. The remainder collects the terms $k\ge2$ and is uniformly bounded:

$$0 \le R(\sigma) = \sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{1}{k p^{k\sigma}} \le \sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{1}{p^{k}} = \sum_{p}\frac{1}{p(p-1)} \le \sum_{m\ge2}\frac{1}{m(m-1)} = 1 .$$

So $\sum_{p}p^{-\sigma} = \log\zeta(\sigma) - R(\sigma) = \log\zeta(\sigma)+O(1)$. By the corollary *Simple pole at $s=1$ with residue $1$*, $\zeta(\sigma)\sim 1/(\sigma-1)$, so $\log\zeta(\sigma) = \log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+o(1)$, which is the stated asymptotic.

Now let $\sigma \downarrow 1$. If $\sum_{p}1/p$ converged, say to $S$, then $\sum_{p}p^{-\sigma} \le S$ for every $\sigma>1$, since each term only decreases. But the right side tends to $+\infty$. Hence $\sum_p 1/p$ diverges.
:::

:::remark
This is strictly stronger than Euclid. The squares are infinite in number but $\sum 1/n^{2}$ converges; so do the powers of $2$. Euler's theorem says the primes are *denser* than any such thin set — dense enough that their reciprocals behave like the harmonic series, only $\log\log$ slowly. Mertens later showed $\sum_{p\le x}1/p = \log\log x + M + o(1)$ with $M = 0.2614972\ldots$
:::

:::proposition[The Möbius function inverts zeta]{#mobius-inverse}
For $\sigma>1$,

$$\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}, \qquad \frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = -\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}},$$

where $\mu$ is the Möbius function and $\Lambda(n) = \log p$ if $n=p^{k}$ and $0$ otherwise.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Euler product*, $1/\zeta(s) = \prod_{p}(1-p^{-s})$. Expanding this product over $p \le y$ gives $\sum_{n\in N(P_y)} \mu(n)n^{-s}$: choosing the term $-p^{-s}$ from a subset $S$ of the primes and $1$ from the rest produces the squarefree integer $n = \prod_{p\in S}p$ with sign $(-1)^{|S|} = \mu(n)$, and non-squarefree $n$ never arise, which is exactly the definition of $\mu$. The tail estimate of the theorem *Euler product* applies verbatim since $|\mu(n)|\le 1$.

For the second identity take the logarithmic derivative of $\zeta(s)=\prod_p (1-p^{-s})^{-1}$, permissible on $\sigma>1$ because the product converges locally uniformly and has no zeros:

$$\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{p}\frac{d}{ds}\log\left(1-p^{-s}\right)^{-1} = -\sum_{p}\frac{\log p \cdot p^{-s}}{1-p^{-s}} = -\sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\log p}{p^{ks}},$$

and $\sum_p\sum_k \log p \cdot p^{-ks}$ is precisely $\sum_{n}\Lambda(n)n^{-s}$.
:::

:::theorem[Mertens' first estimate]{#mertens}
$$\sum_{p \le x}\frac{\log p}{p} = \log x + O(1).$$
:::

:::proof
Count the exponent of $p$ in $N!$ by Legendre's formula: it is $\sum_{k\ge1}\lfloor N/p^{k}\rfloor$. Hence

$$\log (N!) = \sum_{p\le N}\log p\sum_{k\ge1}\left\lfloor \frac{N}{p^{k}}\right\rfloor .$$

The $k=1$ terms give $\sum_{p\le N}\log p \lfloor N/p\rfloor = N\sum_{p\le N}\frac{\log p}{p} + O\!\left(\sum_{p\le N}\log p\right)$, since $\lfloor N/p\rfloor = N/p + O(1)$. The terms $k \ge 2$ contribute at most

$$\sum_{p\le N}\log p\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{N}{p^{k}} = N\sum_{p}\frac{\log p}{p(p-1)} = O(N),$$

the last series converging by comparison with $\sum_{m} \log m/m^{2}$. By the theorem *Chebyshev's bounds* below, $\sum_{p\le N}\log p = O(N)$, so both error terms are $O(N)$ and

$$\log(N!) = N\sum_{p\le N}\frac{\log p}{p} + O(N).$$

Finally Stirling — or just the integral comparison $\log(N!) = \sum_{n\le N}\log n = N\log N - N + O(\log N)$ — gives $\log(N!) = N\log N + O(N)$. Dividing by $N$ yields $\sum_{p\le N}\frac{\log p}{p} = \log N + O(1)$, and passing from an integer $N$ to a real $x$ changes the sum by nothing and $\log x$ by $O(1)$.
:::

:::example[How slowly $\sum 1/p$ grows]
Given $\sum_{p\le x}1/p = \log\log x + 0.2615 + o(1)$, estimate the sum for $x = 10^{6}$ and find how large $x$ must be for the sum to exceed $4$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\log(10^{6}) = 6\log 10 = 13.8155$, so $\log\log(10^{6}) = \log 13.8155 = 2.6258$.
2. The sum is about $2.6258 + 0.2615 = 2.887$.
3. For the sum to reach $4$ we need $\log\log x \approx 3.7385$, so $\log x \approx e^{3.7385} \approx 42.0$, i.e. $x \approx e^{42} \approx 1.7\times10^{18}$.
4. Compare: the first million primes contribute under $3$. Divergence this slow is why no numerical experiment could ever have suggested the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals*.
:::

## The prime number theorem and its equivalents

<!-- section: pnt-statement -->

:::definition[Counting functions]{#counting-functions}
For $x \ge 1$ set

$$\pi(x) = \#\{p \le x\}, \qquad \theta(x) = \sum_{p\le x}\log p, \qquad \psi(x) = \sum_{n \le x}\Lambda(n) = \sum_{p^{k}\le x}\log p .$$

$\theta$ and $\psi$ are the **Chebyshev functions**.
:::

:::theorem[Prime number theorem]{#pnt}
$$\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x} \sim \operatorname{li}(x) \qquad (x\to\infty).$$
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — the analytic input is isolated in the section "How the theorem is proved".* The chain is: (i) the proposition *Chebyshev functions are interchangeable* reduces the claim to $\psi(x)\sim x$; (ii) the proposition *The Möbius function inverts zeta* turns $\psi$ into a contour integral of $-\zeta'/\zeta$; (iii) the theorem *Non-vanishing on the line $\sigma=1$* shows the only pole of $-\zeta'/\zeta$ on $\sigma \ge 1$ is the simple pole at $s=1$ of residue $1$; (iv) a Tauberian theorem converts that analytic fact into $\psi(x)\sim x$. Steps (i) and (iii) are proved in full below; (ii) and (iv) are the borrowed complex analysis.
:::

:::proposition[Chebyshev functions are interchangeable]{#chebyshev-equivalence}
The three statements

$$\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x}, \qquad \theta(x)\sim x, \qquad \psi(x)\sim x$$

are equivalent.
:::

:::proof
*$\psi$ versus $\theta$.* By definition $\psi(x)-\theta(x) = \sum_{k\ge2}\theta(x^{1/k})$. The sum is empty once $x^{1/k}<2$, i.e. for $k > \log_{2}x$, and $\theta(y)\le y\log y$ trivially, so

$$0 \le \psi(x)-\theta(x) \le \log_{2}x \cdot \sqrt{x}\log x = O\!\left(\sqrt x \log^{2}x\right) = o(x).$$

Hence $\psi(x)\sim x \iff \theta(x)\sim x$.

*$\theta$ versus $\pi$.* One direction: $\theta(x) = \sum_{p\le x}\log p \le \pi(x)\log x$. The other: for any $0<\varepsilon<1$,

$$\theta(x) \ge \sum_{x^{1-\varepsilon}<p\le x}\log p \ge (1-\varepsilon)\log x \left(\pi(x)-\pi(x^{1-\varepsilon})\right) \ge (1-\varepsilon)\log x\left(\pi(x)-x^{1-\varepsilon}\right).$$

Dividing by $x$ and using $x^{1-\varepsilon}\log x = o(x)$,

$$\frac{\theta(x)}{x} \le \frac{\pi(x)\log x}{x} \le \frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\theta(x)}{x} + o(1).$$

If $\theta(x)\sim x$, letting $x\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ squeezes $\pi(x)\log x/x \to 1$. Conversely if $\pi(x)\sim x/\log x$ the same two inequalities force $\theta(x)/x\to1$.
:::

:::theorem[PNT is equivalent to $p_{n}\sim n\log n$]{#pn-asymptotic}
Let $p_{n}$ denote the $n$-th prime. Then

$$\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{n}\sim n\log n .$$
:::

:::proof
($\Rightarrow$) Put $x = p_{n}$, so $\pi(p_{n}) = n$ and the hypothesis gives

$$\frac{n\log p_{n}}{p_{n}} \longrightarrow 1. \tag{$*$}$$

Taking logarithms of $(*)$: $\log n + \log\log p_{n} - \log p_{n} \to 0$. Since $p_n \to \infty$, $\log\log p_{n} = o(\log p_{n})$, so dividing by $\log p_{n}$ gives

$$\frac{\log n}{\log p_{n}} \longrightarrow 1 .$$

Multiplying this by $(*)$ gives $n\log n / p_{n} \to 1$, i.e. $p_{n}\sim n\log n$.

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $p_{n}\sim n\log n$. Then $\log p_{n} = \log n + \log\log n + o(1) \sim \log n$, so

$$\frac{n\log p_{n}}{p_{n}} = \frac{n \log p_n}{n \log n}\cdot\frac{n\log n}{p_{n}} \longrightarrow 1,$$

which is $(*)$. For general $x$, choose $n$ with $p_{n}\le x<p_{n+1}$, so $\pi(x)=n$ and

$$\frac{n\log p_{n}}{p_{n}} \ge \frac{\pi(x)\log x}{x}\cdot\frac{\log p_n}{\log x} \cdot \frac{x}{p_n}\Big/\frac{x}{p_n} \ \text{ is squeezed between } \ \frac{n\log p_{n}}{p_{n+1}} \ \text{ and } \ \frac{n\log p_{n+1}}{p_{n}} .$$

Both bounds tend to $1$, because $p_{n+1}/p_{n} \to 1$ and $\log p_{n+1}/\log p_{n}\to1$ follow from $p_{n}\sim n\log n$. Hence $\pi(x)\log x/x\to1$.
:::

:::example[Estimating the millionth prime]
Use $p_{n}\sim n\log n$ to estimate $p_{1000000}$, and compare with the true value $15{,}485{,}863$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\log(10^{6}) = 13.8155$, so the first-order estimate is $10^{6}\times13.8155 = 13{,}815{,}500$.
2. That is low by about $11\%$. The next term in the expansion is $p_{n} = n(\log n + \log\log n - 1 + o(1))$.
3. $\log\log(10^{6}) = \log 13.8155 = 2.6258$, giving $10^{6}(13.8155 + 2.6258 - 1) = 15{,}441{,}300$.
4. That is within $0.3\%$ of $15{,}485{,}863$. The lesson matches the example *Reading the error numerically*: $n\log n$ has the right growth but the secondary terms matter at any height a computer can reach.
:::

## How the theorem is proved

<!-- section: pnt-proof-ideas -->

The proof has one genuinely hard point and it is not where a first reading expects. Getting a contour integral for $\psi(x)$ is bookkeeping. Knowing that the contour may be pushed onto the line $\sigma=1$ requires knowing that $\zeta$ has no zero there, and *that* is the theorem which resisted Riemann, Chebyshev and everyone else until 1896.

:::lemma[The three-four-one inequality]{#341}
For every real $\vartheta$,

$$3 + 4\cos\vartheta + \cos 2\vartheta \;\ge\; 0 .$$
:::

:::proof
Using $\cos2\vartheta = 2\cos^{2}\vartheta-1$,

$$3+4\cos\vartheta+\cos2\vartheta = 2 + 4\cos\vartheta + 2\cos^{2}\vartheta = 2(1+\cos\vartheta)^{2} \ge 0 .$$
:::

:::theorem[Non-vanishing on the line $\sigma=1$]{#zeta-nonvanishing}
$\zeta(1+it)\neq0$ for every real $t \neq 0$.
:::

:::proof
For real $\sigma>1$ and real $t$, take real parts in the logarithm of the Euler product. From the proof of the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals*,

$$\log|\zeta(\sigma+it)| = \operatorname{Re}\sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{1}{k\,p^{k(\sigma+it)}} = \sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\cos(kt\log p)}{k\,p^{k\sigma}} .$$

Apply the lemma *The three-four-one inequality* with $\vartheta = kt\log p$ to each term of the combination

$$3\log|\zeta(\sigma)| + 4\log|\zeta(\sigma+it)| + \log|\zeta(\sigma+2it)| = \sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{3+4\cos\vartheta+\cos2\vartheta}{k\,p^{k\sigma}} \;\ge\; 0 .$$

Exponentiating,

$$\left|\zeta(\sigma)\right|^{3}\left|\zeta(\sigma+it)\right|^{4}\left|\zeta(\sigma+2it)\right| \;\ge\; 1 \qquad (\sigma>1). \tag{$\dagger$}$$

Now suppose $\zeta(1+it)=0$ for some $t \neq 0$. As $\sigma\downarrow1$:

- $|\zeta(\sigma)|^{3} = O\!\left((\sigma-1)^{-3}\right)$, by the corollary *Simple pole at $s=1$ with residue $1$*;
- $|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^{4} = O\!\left((\sigma-1)^{4}\right)$, since a zero at $1+it$ makes $\zeta(\sigma+it) = O(\sigma-1)$;
- $|\zeta(\sigma+2it)| = O(1)$, since $\zeta$ is holomorphic at $1+2it$ — here $t\neq0$ is used, so that $1+2it \neq 1$.

The left side of $(\dagger)$ is therefore $O(\sigma-1)$, which tends to $0$ and contradicts $(\dagger)$.
:::

:::intuition
The inequality is a rigidity statement. A zero on $\sigma=1$ would mean the phases $\cos(t\log p)$ conspire to be near $-1$ for essentially all primes. The combination $3+4\cos+\cos2$ punishes that conspiracy: if $\cos\vartheta\approx-1$ then $\cos2\vartheta\approx+1$ and the weighted sum stays non-negative. The pole at $s=1$, weighted by $3$, then has more room than a fourth-order zero can consume.
:::

:::example[Where the inequality is tight]
Find every $\vartheta$ at which $3+4\cos\vartheta+\cos2\vartheta = 0$, and evaluate the expression at $\vartheta = \pi/2$.
:::

:::solution
1. By the proof of the lemma *The three-four-one inequality*, the expression equals $2(1+\cos\vartheta)^{2}$.
2. This vanishes exactly when $\cos\vartheta = -1$, i.e. $\vartheta = \pi + 2\pi k$ for integer $k$.
3. At $\vartheta=\pi/2$: $\cos\vartheta = 0$ and $\cos2\vartheta = -1$, so the value is $3+0-1 = 2$; and $2(1+0)^{2}=2$ agrees.
4. The double zero at $\vartheta=\pi$ is why the weights $3,4,1$ work where $3,2,1$ would not: a simple zero would leave the factor $|\zeta(\sigma)|^{3}$ unmatched.
:::

:::theorem[The Tauberian step]{#tauberian}
Let $F(s) = \int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\psi(x)-x\right)x^{-s-1}dx$. If $F$ extends holomorphically to an open set containing $\{\sigma\ge1\}$, then $\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi(x)-x}{x^{2}}dx$ converges, and consequently $\psi(x)\sim x$.
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes Cauchy's theorem and a contour deformation (Newman's method); not elementary.* Writing $g(t) = \psi(e^{t})e^{-t}-1$, the hypothesis makes the Laplace transform of $g$ holomorphic on a neighbourhood of $\operatorname{Re}(z)\ge0$, and $g$ is bounded by the theorem *Chebyshev's bounds*. Newman's analytic theorem — proved by integrating $\int_0^T g(t)e^{-zt}\,dt$ against $e^{zT}(1+z^{2}/R^{2})/z$ around a contour that bulges to the left of $\operatorname{Re}(z)=0$, and estimating the three arcs separately — then gives convergence of $\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)\,dt$. The passage from that convergence to $\psi(x)\sim x$ is elementary: if $\psi(x)\ge\lambda x$ for some $\lambda>1$ on a sequence of $x$, then since $\psi$ is non-decreasing, $\int_{x}^{\lambda x}\frac{\psi(u)-u}{u^{2}}du \ge \int_{1}^{\lambda}\frac{\lambda - v}{v^{2}}dv > 0$, a fixed positive amount, contradicting convergence; the case $\psi(x)\le\lambda x$ with $\lambda<1$ is symmetric.

That $F$ satisfies the hypothesis is where the theorem *Non-vanishing on the line $\sigma=1$* enters: $F(s) = -\frac{1}{s}\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} - \frac{1}{s-1}$ for $\sigma>1$, and the two poles at $s=1$ — one from the zeta pole, one explicit — cancel, while the non-vanishing rules out any other pole on the line.
:::

:::remark
There is also an *elementary* proof of the prime number theorem, found by Erdős and Selberg in 1949, which uses no complex analysis at all; its engine is Selberg's symmetry formula $\sum_{p\le x}\log^{2}p + \sum_{pq\le x}\log p\log q = 2x\log x + O(x)$. It is elementary in the technical sense only: it is considerably longer and harder to motivate than the analytic proof.
:::

## The explicit formula

<!-- section: pnt-explicit-formula -->

The prime number theorem says $\psi(x) = x + o(x)$. The explicit formula says what the $o(x)$ *is*: a sum of waves, one per zero of zeta.

:::theorem[Explicit formula]{#explicit-formula}
For $x>1$ not a prime power,

$$\psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho} - \log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(1-x^{-2}\right),$$

the sum running over all nontrivial zeros $\rho$ of $\zeta$, taken in order of increasing $|\operatorname{Im}\rho|$.
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes Perron's formula, the residue theorem, and the Hadamard product for $\xi$.* Perron's formula gives, for $c>1$,

$$\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\left(-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\right)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\,ds,$$

which is legitimate by the proposition *The Möbius function inverts zeta* together with the Dirichlet-series inversion $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int (y^{s}/s)ds = 1$ or $0$ according as $y>1$ or $y<1$. Now move the contour left to $\operatorname{Re}(s)=-\infty$ and collect residues of $\left(-\zeta'/\zeta\right)x^{s}/s$:

- the pole of $\zeta$ at $s=1$ gives residue $x$;
- each nontrivial zero $\rho$ gives $-x^{\rho}/\rho$;
- $s=0$ gives $-\zeta'(0)/\zeta(0) = -\log(2\pi)$;
- the trivial zeros at $-2k$ give $-\sum_{k\ge1}x^{-2k}/(2k) = \tfrac12\log(1-x^{-2})$, with a sign.

The borrowed input is the Hadamard product $\xi(s) = e^{a+bs}\prod_{\rho}\left(1-\frac{s}{\rho}\right)e^{s/\rho}$, which is what bounds $\zeta'/\zeta$ on the shifted contours well enough for the contour shift to be justified and for the zero sum to converge conditionally.
:::

:::corollary[Zero-free region controls the error]{#zero-free-error}
If $\zeta(s)\neq0$ in a region $\sigma > 1 - \eta(t)$, then $\psi(x) = x + O\!\left(x\,e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}\right)$ for the classical choice $\eta(t) = c/\log(|t|+2)$. Under the Riemann hypothesis the bound improves to $\psi(x) = x + O\!\left(\sqrt x\log^{2}x\right)$.
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes the theorem "Explicit formula" and standard zero-density counting.* Truncate the zero sum at height $T$, incurring an error $O(x\log^{2}(xT)/T)$. Each surviving zero contributes at most $x^{\beta}/|\rho|$, and the zero-free region bounds $\beta \le 1 - c/\log T$, so the truncated sum is $O\!\left(x^{1-c/\log T}\log^{2}T\right)$. Optimising over $T$ by taking $\log T = \sqrt{\log x}$ balances the two errors and gives the stated bound. Under RH, $\beta=\tfrac12$ makes every term $\sqrt x/|\rho|$ and the count $N(T)\sim\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}$ of zeros to height $T$ makes the sum $O(\sqrt x\log^{2}x)$.
:::

:::example[One zero, one wave]
The first nontrivial zero is $\rho_{1} = \tfrac12 + 14.1347\,i$. What oscillation does the pair $\rho_{1},\overline{\rho_{1}}$ contribute to $\psi(x)$ at $x = 10^{6}$?
:::

:::solution
1. The pair contributes $-2\operatorname{Re}\left(x^{\rho_{1}}/\rho_{1}\right)$, of amplitude $2x^{1/2}/|\rho_{1}|$.
2. $|\rho_{1}| = \sqrt{0.25 + 14.1347^{2}} = 14.1436$, and $\sqrt{10^{6}} = 1000$.
3. Amplitude $= 2000/14.1436 = 141.4$.
4. Against $\psi(10^{6}) \approx 10^{6}$ this is a relative wobble of $1.4\times10^{-4}$: the leading zero writes a ripple on $\psi$ with wavelength $2\pi/14.13$ in $\log x$, i.e. about a factor $e^{0.445}=1.56$ in $x$.
:::

## Chebyshev's bounds, and what came before

<!-- section: pnt-history-equivalents -->

Fifty years before the prime number theorem, Chebyshev proved that $\pi(x)$ has the right *order*, by an argument that needs nothing beyond binomial coefficients. It is the strongest fully elementary statement in this chapter, and the prime number theorem's own proof (in the theorem *The Tauberian step*) leans on it.

:::theorem[Chebyshev's bounds]{#chebyshev-bounds}
There exist absolute constants $0 < c_{1} < c_{2}$ with

$$c_{1}\frac{x}{\log x} \;\le\; \pi(x) \;\le\; c_{2}\frac{x}{\log x} \qquad (x \ge 2).$$

One may take $c_{1} = \log 2 \approx 0.693$ and $c_{2} = 4\log 2 \approx 2.77$ for all sufficiently large $x$. Equivalently $\theta(x) \asymp x$.
:::

:::proof
*Upper bound.* Consider $\binom{2n}{n}$. Every prime $p$ with $n<p\le 2n$ divides the numerator $(2n)!$ exactly once and does not divide $(n!)^{2}$, so

$$\prod_{n<p\le2n}p \;\Big|\; \binom{2n}{n} \;\le\; 2^{2n},$$

the bound because $\binom{2n}{n}$ is one term of $\sum_{k}\binom{2n}{k}=2^{2n}$. Taking logarithms, $\theta(2n)-\theta(n) \le 2n\log 2$. Summing this over $n = 2^{j}$ for $j < J$ telescopes:

$$\theta(2^{J}) \le \log 2\sum_{j<J}2^{j+1} < 2^{J+1}\log 2 .$$

For general $x$ pick $J$ with $2^{J-1} \le x < 2^{J}$, so $\theta(x)\le\theta(2^{J}) < 4x\log2$. Then, for $1<y<x$,

$$\theta(x) \ge \sum_{y<p\le x}\log p \ge \left(\pi(x)-\pi(y)\right)\log y \ge \left(\pi(x)-y\right)\log y .$$

Taking $y = x/\log^{2}x$ gives $\pi(x) \le \frac{\theta(x)}{\log y} + \frac{x}{\log^{2}x}$, and $\log y = \log x - 2\log\log x \sim \log x$, so $\pi(x) \le (4\log 2 + o(1))\,x/\log x$.

*Lower bound.* The exponent of $p$ in $\binom{2n}{n}$ is $\sum_{k\ge1}\left(\lfloor 2n/p^{k}\rfloor - 2\lfloor n/p^{k}\rfloor\right)$, by Legendre's formula applied to numerator and denominator. Each bracket is $0$ or $1$, and it vanishes for $p^{k}>2n$; so the exponent is at most $\lfloor \log(2n)/\log p\rfloor$, and $p^{(\text{exponent})} \le 2n$. Hence

$$\binom{2n}{n} \le \prod_{p\le 2n}2n = (2n)^{\pi(2n)} .$$

On the other side $\binom{2n}{n} \ge \frac{4^{n}}{2n+1}$, since it is the largest of the $2n+1$ binomial coefficients summing to $4^{n}$. Taking logarithms,

$$\pi(2n)\log(2n) \;\ge\; 2n\log 2 - \log(2n+1),$$

so $\pi(2n) \ge (\log 2 + o(1))\frac{2n}{\log 2n}$. For a general $x$, apply this with $2n = 2\lfloor x/2\rfloor$ and note $\pi$ is non-decreasing while $x/\log x$ changes by a factor $1+o(1)$.
:::

:::corollary[Order of $\psi$ and $\theta$]{#psi-order}
$\theta(x) = O(x)$ and $\psi(x) = O(x)$; in particular $\sum_{p\le x}\log p = O(x)$, the fact used in the theorem *Mertens' first estimate*.
:::

:::proof
$\theta(x) < 4x\log2$ was proved above. For $\psi$, the proof of the proposition *Chebyshev functions are interchangeable* gives $\psi(x)-\theta(x) = O(\sqrt x\log^{2}x)$, so $\psi(x) = O(x)$ as well.
:::

:::remark
Chebyshev also showed that *if* $\pi(x)\log x/x$ has a limit, that limit must be $1$ — so the only thing left open in 1850 was the existence of the limit. And in 1852 he deduced **Bertrand's postulate**: there is always a prime between $n$ and $2n$. The telescoping upper bound above is the same computation Erdős later compressed into his famous one-page proof.
:::

## Dirichlet characters and their L-functions

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-def -->

To study primes in a fixed residue class we need a way to *select* that class inside a sum, multiplicatively — because the Euler product needs multiplicativity. Dirichlet characters are exactly the multiplicative functions that see a residue class.

:::definition[Dirichlet character]{#character-def}
Let $q \ge 1$. A **Dirichlet character modulo $q$** is a function $\chi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}$ such that

1. $\chi(n+q)=\chi(n)$ for all $n$;
2. $\chi(mn)=\chi(m)\chi(n)$ for all $m,n$;
3. $\chi(n)\neq0$ if and only if $\gcd(n,q)=1$.

Equivalently, $\chi$ is a group homomorphism $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, extended by $0$. The **principal character** $\chi_{0}$ takes the value $1$ on every $n$ coprime to $q$.
:::

:::proposition[Character values are roots of unity]{#char-roots}
If $\chi$ is a character mod $q$ and $\gcd(n,q)=1$, then $\chi(n)^{\varphi(q)}=1$; in particular $|\chi(n)|=1$ and $\chi(n)^{-1}=\overline{\chi(n)}$.
:::

:::proof
By Euler's theorem $n^{\varphi(q)}\equiv1 \pmod q$, so by periodicity and multiplicativity $\chi(n)^{\varphi(q)} = \chi\!\left(n^{\varphi(q)}\right) = \chi(1) = 1$; and $\chi(1)=1$ because $\chi(1)=\chi(1)^{2}$ with $\chi(1)\neq0$. A root of unity has modulus $1$, and for such a number the inverse is the conjugate.
:::

:::definition[Dirichlet L-function]{#l-function-def}
For a character $\chi$ mod $q$ and $\operatorname{Re}(s)>1$,

$$L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}} .$$
:::

The series converges absolutely for $\sigma>1$ by comparison with $\zeta(\sigma)$, since $|\chi(n)|\le1$; that is the same argument as the theorem *Convergence of the zeta series* and we do not repeat it.

:::theorem[Euler product for $L(s,\chi)$]{#l-euler-product}
For $\sigma>1$,

$$L(s,\chi) = \prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1},$$

converging absolutely; in particular $L(s,\chi)\neq0$ for $\sigma>1$.
:::

:::proof
The proof of the theorem *Euler product* goes through with $n^{-s}$ replaced by $\chi(n)n^{-s}$, and the only new ingredient is complete multiplicativity. For a finite set $P$ of primes, each factor expands as a geometric series, $|\chi(p)p^{-s}| \le p^{-\sigma}<1$:

$$\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1} = \prod_{p\in P}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\chi(p)^{k}}{p^{ks}} = \sum_{n\in N(P)}\frac{\chi(p_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots\chi(p_{r})^{k_{r}}}{n^{s}} = \sum_{n \in N(P)}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}},$$

the last equality being $\chi(p_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}}) = \chi(p_{1})^{k_{1}}\cdots\chi(p_{r})^{k_{r}}$, which is property 2 of the definition *Dirichlet character* applied repeatedly. Unique factorization again guarantees each $n \in N(P)$ appears exactly once. Taking $P=\{p\le y\}$, the difference from $L(s,\chi)$ is bounded by $\sum_{n>y}n^{-\sigma}\to0$. Absolute convergence and non-vanishing follow as before from $\sum_{p}p^{-\sigma}<\infty$.
:::

:::example[The characters modulo $4$]
List the Dirichlet characters mod $4$ and write down their L-functions at $s=1$.
:::

:::solution
1. $(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^{\times} = \{1,3\}$ has order $\varphi(4)=2$, so there are two characters.
2. $\chi_{0}$: $\chi_{0}(1)=\chi_{0}(3)=1$, and $\chi_{0}(n)=0$ for even $n$.
3. $\chi_{1}$: the nontrivial homomorphism to $\{\pm1\}$, so $\chi_{1}(1)=1$, $\chi_{1}(3)=-1$, i.e. $\chi_{1}(n)=(-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.
4. $L(1,\chi_{1}) = 1 - \tfrac13+\tfrac15-\tfrac17+\cdots = \pi/4 \approx 0.7854$, Leibniz's series — and crucially it is **nonzero**.
5. $L(s,\chi_{0}) = (1-2^{-s})\zeta(s)$, so it inherits the pole at $s=1$.
:::

## Continuation of $L(s,\chi)$

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-continuation -->

:::proposition[The principal character gives back zeta]{#l-principal}
For $\sigma>1$,

$$L(s,\chi_{0}) = \zeta(s)\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right).$$

Hence $L(s,\chi_{0})$ continues meromorphically to $\sigma>0$ with a simple pole at $s=1$ of residue $\varphi(q)/q$.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Euler product for $L(s,\chi)$*, $L(s,\chi_{0}) = \prod_{p\nmid q}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$, since $\chi_{0}(p)=1$ for $p\nmid q$ and $\chi_{0}(p)=0$ otherwise. Multiplying and dividing by the missing factors $\prod_{p\mid q}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$ turns this into $\zeta(s)\prod_{p\mid q}(1-p^{-s})$. The finite product is entire, so the continuation and the pole come from $\zeta$; the residue is $1 \cdot \prod_{p\mid q}(1-p^{-1}) = \varphi(q)/q$ by the product formula for Euler's totient.
:::

:::theorem[Non-principal L-series converge for $\sigma>0$]{#l-nonprincipal-conv}
Let $\chi\neq\chi_{0}$ be a character mod $q$. Then the partial sums $S(x) = \sum_{n\le x}\chi(n)$ satisfy $|S(x)| \le \varphi(q)$ for all $x$, and consequently $\sum_{n}\chi(n)n^{-s}$ converges — locally uniformly, hence to a holomorphic function — on the half-plane $\sigma>0$. In particular $L(s,\chi)$ is holomorphic at $s=1$ and $L(1,\chi)$ is a well-defined complex number.
:::

:::proof
*Bounded partial sums.* By the corollary *Characters detect a residue class* below (whose proof uses only the orthogonality relation over a full period), $\sum_{n=a+1}^{a+q}\chi(n) = 0$ for every $a$: a non-principal character sums to zero over any complete residue system. So $S(x)$ depends only on the incomplete last block, which has at most $q$ terms of modulus $\le1$ and in fact at most $\varphi(q)$ nonzero ones. Hence $|S(x)|\le\varphi(q)$.

*Convergence.* Apply summation by parts (Abel summation): for $1 \le M < N$,

$$\sum_{M<n\le N}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}} = \frac{S(N)}{N^{s}} - \frac{S(M)}{M^{s}} + s\int_{M}^{N}\frac{S(u)}{u^{s+1}}du .$$

With $|S| \le \varphi(q)$ and $\sigma>0$, the first two terms are $O(\varphi(q)M^{-\sigma})$ and the integral is bounded by $\varphi(q)|s|\int_{M}^{\infty}u^{-\sigma-1}du = \varphi(q)|s|M^{-\sigma}/\sigma$. All of these tend to $0$ as $M\to\infty$, uniformly for $s$ in a compact subset of $\{\sigma>0\}$ (where $|s|\le C$ and $\sigma\ge\delta$). By the Cauchy criterion the series converges locally uniformly there, and a locally uniform limit of the holomorphic partial sums is holomorphic.
:::

:::pitfall
Convergence for $\sigma>0$ is *not* absolute convergence; $\sum|\chi(n)|n^{-\sigma}$ still diverges for $\sigma\le1$. So the Euler product of the theorem *Euler product for $L(s,\chi)$* is **not** valid on $0<\sigma\le1$, and one may not conclude $L(1,\chi)\neq0$ from it. That is precisely why non-vanishing at $s=1$ is a theorem and not an observation.
:::

:::example[Bounded partial sums for a character mod $5$]
The residue $2$ generates $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, with $2^{1},2^{2},2^{3},2^{4} \equiv 2,4,3,1$. Let $\chi$ be the character with $\chi(2)=i$. Tabulate $\chi$ and check the bound of the theorem *Non-principal L-series converge for $\sigma>0$*.
:::

:::solution
1. From $\chi(2)=i$: $\chi(4)=i^{2}=-1$, $\chi(3)=i^{3}=-i$, $\chi(1)=i^{4}=1$, and $\chi(5)=0$.
2. Partial sums $S(x)$ for $x=1,\dots,5$: $1$, $1+i$, $1+i-i = 1$, $1-1 = 0$, $0$.
3. The largest modulus is $|1+i| = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414$, comfortably below the bound $\varphi(5)=4$.
4. Over each full block of five consecutive integers the sum is $1+i-i-1+0 = 0$, which is the orthogonality relation of the theorem *Orthogonality of characters* in action — and it is the only reason $L(s,\chi)$ converges below $\sigma=1$ at all.
:::

:::theorem[Functional equation for primitive characters]{#l-functional}
Let $\chi$ be a primitive character mod $q$ with $\chi(-1)=(-1)^{\kappa}$, $\kappa\in\{0,1\}$. Then

$$\Lambda(s,\chi) = \left(\frac{q}{\pi}\right)^{(s+\kappa)/2}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+\kappa}{2}\right)L(s,\chi)$$

is entire and satisfies $\Lambda(s,\chi) = \varepsilon(\chi)\,\Lambda(1-s,\overline{\chi})$ with $|\varepsilon(\chi)|=1$.
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — assumes Poisson summation and Gauss sums; the same machinery as the theorem "Functional equation".* Write $\chi(n)$ via the Gauss sum $\tau(\chi)=\sum_{a \bmod q}\chi(a)e^{2\pi i a/q}$ as a finite linear combination of additive characters, which is where primitivity is needed. Substituting into the theta function $\theta_{\chi}(x)=\sum_{n}\chi(n)n^{\kappa}e^{-\pi n^{2}x/q}$ and applying Poisson summation converts $\theta_\chi(1/x)$ into $\theta_{\overline\chi}(x)$ times $\varepsilon(\chi)$ and a power of $x$. Feeding that into the Mellin integral for $\Lambda(s,\chi)$ gives the symmetry. The constant is $\varepsilon(\chi)=\tau(\chi)/(i^{\kappa}\sqrt q)$, and $|\tau(\chi)|=\sqrt q$ for primitive $\chi$ is the standard Gauss-sum evaluation.
:::

## Non-vanishing at $s=1$

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-nonvanish -->

Everything in the next three sections rests on one statement, and it is fair to call it the hard input of the whole theory.

:::theorem[Non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$]{#l-nonvanishing}
For every non-principal character $\chi$ modulo $q$,

$$L(1,\chi) \neq 0 .$$
:::

:::proof
*Partly elementary, partly a labelled sketch; this is the deep input the rest of the chapter assumes.*

**The auxiliary product.** Set $Z_{q}(s) = \prod_{\chi \bmod q} L(s,\chi)$, the product over all $\varphi(q)$ characters. By the proposition *Coefficients of the character product are non-negative* below, $Z_{q}$ has a Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficients and constant term $1$ on $\sigma>1$.

**The complex case, in full.** Suppose $\chi$ is not real, so $\chi \neq \overline\chi$, and suppose $L(1,\chi)=0$. Then also $L(1,\overline\chi)=\overline{L(1,\chi)}=0$: two distinct factors of $Z_{q}$ vanish to order at least $1$ each. The only factor with a pole is $L(s,\chi_{0})$, a simple pole by the proposition *The principal character gives back zeta*. Hence $Z_{q}$ vanishes at $s=1$: the two zeros outweigh the one pole. But a product of non-negative-coefficient Dirichlet series with constant term $1$ satisfies $Z_{q}(\sigma)\ge1$ for real $\sigma>1$ — every term of $\prod_\chi \sum \cdots$ is non-negative and the $n=1$ term is $1$ — and $Z_{q}$ is continuous at $s=1$ once the pole has been cancelled, so $Z_{q}(1)\ge1 \neq 0$. Contradiction.

**The real case, sketched.** If $\chi$ is real and non-principal, only one factor could vanish, and it exactly cancels the simple pole, so the argument above gives no contradiction — $Z_{q}$ would merely be holomorphic and the inequality $Z_q \ge 1$ is not violated. Two standard routes close the gap, neither elementary: (i) *Landau's theorem* on Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficients says $Z_{q}$ has a singularity at its abscissa of convergence; if $L(1,\chi)=0$ then $Z_{q}$ is entire on $\sigma>0$, forcing the series to converge there, which contradicts the fact that the $n=$ square terms alone make it diverge at $s=1/2$. (ii) *Dirichlet's class number formula* identifies $L(1,\chi)$ for a real primitive $\chi$ of conductor $q$ with $2\pi h/(w\sqrt{|d|})$ or $2h\log\varepsilon_{0}/\sqrt d$ for the associated quadratic field, a positive quantity because a class number $h$ is a positive integer. Either route settles it, and no proof avoiding both is known to be substantially simpler.
:::

:::proposition[Coefficients of the character product are non-negative]{#char-product-coeffs}
For $\sigma>1$,

$$\prod_{\chi \bmod q} L(s,\chi) = \prod_{p\nmid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{f_{p}s}}\right)^{-\varphi(q)/f_{p}},$$

where $f_{p}$ is the multiplicative order of $p$ modulo $q$. Consequently the product equals a Dirichlet series $\sum_{n} a_{n}n^{-s}$ with $a_{n}\ge0$ and $a_{1}=1$.
:::

:::proof
Fix $p \nmid q$ and let $f=f_{p}$ be its order in $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$. As $\chi$ runs over all $\varphi(q)$ characters, $\chi(p)$ runs over the $f$-th roots of unity, each attained exactly $\varphi(q)/f$ times — this is the statement that the characters of a finite abelian group restricted to the cyclic subgroup generated by $p$ realise every character of that subgroup equally often. Therefore, with $T=p^{-s}$,

$$\prod_{\chi}\left(1-\chi(p)T\right) = \left(\prod_{\omega^{f}=1}(1-\omega T)\right)^{\varphi(q)/f} = \left(1-T^{f}\right)^{\varphi(q)/f},$$

using the factorisation $\prod_{\omega^{f}=1}(X-\omega)=X^{f}-1$. Taking the reciprocal and multiplying over $p\nmid q$ gives the displayed formula, via the theorem *Euler product for $L(s,\chi)$*.

Each factor $\left(1-p^{-fs}\right)^{-\varphi(q)/f}$ expands as a power series in $p^{-fs}$ with non-negative (binomial) coefficients, so the whole product, expanded by unique factorization exactly as in the theorem *Euler product*, is a Dirichlet series with non-negative coefficients; the empty choice gives $a_{1}=1$.
:::

:::remark
A hypothetical zero of a real $L(s,\chi)$ at a real $s$ very close to $1$ is called a **Siegel zero**. No such zero is known to exist and none is known not to; ruling them out is a stronger version of the theorem *Non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$* and remains open. Their possible existence is why the error term in the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions carries an ineffective constant in the Siegel–Walfisz theorem: one can prove the constant exists without being able to compute it.
:::

## What L-functions are good for

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-applications -->

:::theorem[Prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions]{#pnt-ap-thm}
Let $\gcd(a,q)=1$ and $\pi(x;q,a) = \#\{p\le x : p\equiv a \bmod q\}$. Then

$$\pi(x;q,a) \sim \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\cdot\frac{x}{\log x}.$$
:::

:::proof
*Proof sketch — the same analytic package as the theorem "Prime number theorem", applied to each $L(s,\chi)$.* Orthogonality (the theorem *Orthogonality of characters*) gives

$$\psi(x;q,a) = \sum_{\substack{n\le x\\ n\equiv a}}\Lambda(n) = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi(a)}\sum_{n\le x}\chi(n)\Lambda(n).$$

The $\chi_{0}$ term gives $\psi(x)+O(\log^2 x) \sim x$ by the theorem *Prime number theorem*. For each $\chi\neq\chi_{0}$ one shows the inner sum is $o(x)$ by running the Tauberian argument on $-L'(s,\chi)/L(s,\chi)$; this needs $L(s,\chi)\neq0$ on the whole line $\sigma=1$, which for $s=1$ itself is the theorem *Non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$* and for $s=1+it$, $t\neq0$, follows from the three-four-one argument of the theorem *Non-vanishing on the line $\sigma=1$* applied to $L$. Hence $\psi(x;q,a)\sim x/\varphi(q)$, and the proposition *Chebyshev functions are interchangeable* transfers this to $\pi(x;q,a)$.
:::

:::remark
Two refinements are worth naming. **Siegel–Walfisz**: the asymptotic holds uniformly for $q \le (\log x)^{A}$, with an ineffective implied constant. **Linnik's theorem**: the least prime $\equiv a \bmod q$ is $O(q^{L})$ for an absolute $L$; the current record is $L=5$. Both are what make the theorem usable as an input to other arguments rather than merely true.
:::

:::example[How many primes below $10^{5}$ should be $\equiv 1 \bmod 8$?]
Predict the count from the theorem *Prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions*, and compare with the true value $2384$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\varphi(8)=4$, so each of the four classes $1,3,5,7 \bmod 8$ should get about a quarter of the primes.
2. $\pi(10^{5}) = 9592$, and $9592/4 = 2398$.
3. The prediction $2398$ against the truth $2384$ is an error of $14$, or $0.6\%$.
4. Using $x/(\varphi(q)\log x)$ instead gives $100000/(4\times11.5129) = 2171$ — much worse, for exactly the reason the example *Relative error of the two approximations at $x=10^{4}$* identified. Dividing the *true* $\pi(x)$ by $\varphi(q)$ is the accurate way to use the theorem at finite height.
:::

## Dirichlet's theorem

<!-- section: dirichlet-ap-statement -->

:::theorem[Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions]{#dirichlet-theorem}
Let $q\ge1$ and $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $\gcd(a,q)=1$. Then the arithmetic progression

$$a,\; a+q,\; a+2q,\; a+3q,\;\dots$$

contains infinitely many primes. In fact $\sum_{p\equiv a \ (q)} 1/p = \infty$.
:::

:::proof
This is proved in the section "Reducing the theorem to one non-vanishing", as a corollary of the theorem *Density of primes in a residue class*. The hypothesis $\gcd(a,q)=1$ is necessary: if $d = \gcd(a,q)>1$ then every term of the progression is divisible by $d$, so the progression contains at most the single prime $d$ itself.
:::

:::example[Which progressions can contain infinitely many primes?]
Decide for $\{6n+4\}$, $\{10n+3\}$ and $\{15n+5\}$.
:::

:::solution
1. $6n+4$: $\gcd(4,6)=2$, and every term is even and $>2$. Only finitely many primes — in fact none.
2. $10n+3$: $\gcd(3,10)=1$, so by the theorem *Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions* there are infinitely many: $3,13,23,43,53,\dots$
3. $15n+5$: $\gcd(5,15)=5$, so every term is a multiple of $5$; the only prime is $5$ itself, at $n=0$.
4. The rule is entirely captured by the gcd, and the four classes mod $8$ in the example *How many primes below $10^{5}$ should be $\equiv 1 \bmod 8$?* are each infinite for the same reason.
:::

## Orthogonality of characters

<!-- section: dirichlet-l-functions -->

The tool that makes the reduction work is a finite Fourier analysis on $(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, and it is entirely elementary.

:::theorem[The character group]{#character-group}
The Dirichlet characters modulo $q$ form a group $\widehat{G}$ under pointwise multiplication, with identity $\chi_{0}$ and inverse $\chi^{-1}=\overline\chi$, and

$$\left|\widehat{G}\right| = \varphi(q).$$

Moreover, for every $n \not\equiv 1 \bmod q$ with $\gcd(n,q)=1$ there is a character with $\chi(n)\neq1$.
:::

:::proof
That $\widehat G$ is a group is immediate: a product of homomorphisms $G=(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is a homomorphism, and $\overline\chi$ inverts $\chi$ by the proposition *Character values are roots of unity*.

For the order, write the finite abelian group $G$ as a direct product of cyclic groups $C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{r}$ with $|C_{i}|=d_{i}$ and $\prod d_{i} = |G| = \varphi(q)$. A homomorphism $G \to \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ is determined freely by its values on the generators $g_{i}$, and $\chi(g_{i})$ may be any $d_{i}$-th root of unity — any such choice does define a homomorphism, since the only relations are $g_{i}^{d_{i}}=1$. So there are exactly $\prod_{i}d_{i}=\varphi(q)$ characters.

For the separation property, let $n\not\equiv1$. In the decomposition, $n$ has some coordinate $n_{i}$ of order $e>1$ in $C_{i}$; define $\chi$ by $\chi(g_{i}) = e^{2\pi i/d_{i}}$ and $\chi(g_{j})=1$ for $j \ne i$. Then $\chi(n)=e^{2\pi i m/d_{i}}$ where $g_i^m$ is the $i$-th coordinate of $n$, and $m \not\equiv 0 \bmod d_i$, so $\chi(n)\neq1$.
:::

:::theorem[Orthogonality of characters]{#orthogonality}
Let $q \ge 1$. Then

$$\sum_{n \bmod q}\chi(n) = \begin{cases}\varphi(q) & \chi=\chi_{0}\\ 0 & \chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{cases} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{\chi \bmod q}\chi(n) = \begin{cases}\varphi(q) & n\equiv1\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

for $\gcd(n,q)=1$, the first sum over any complete residue system, the second over all characters.
:::

:::proof
*First relation.* If $\chi=\chi_{0}$ the sum counts the $\varphi(q)$ residues coprime to $q$. If $\chi\neq\chi_{0}$, pick $b$ coprime to $q$ with $\chi(b)\neq1$, and write $S=\sum_{n}\chi(n)$. As $n$ runs over the units mod $q$ so does $bn$, so

$$\chi(b)S = \sum_{n}\chi(bn) = S .$$

Hence $(\chi(b)-1)S=0$ and $S=0$.

*Second relation.* If $n\equiv1$ then every $\chi(n)=1$ and the sum is $|\widehat G| = \varphi(q)$ by the theorem *The character group*. If $n\not\equiv1$, that same theorem supplies $\psi$ with $\psi(n)\neq1$. As $\chi$ runs over the group $\widehat G$ so does $\psi\chi$, so with $T = \sum_{\chi}\chi(n)$,

$$\psi(n)T = \sum_{\chi}(\psi\chi)(n) = T,$$

and again $T=0$.
:::

:::corollary[Characters detect a residue class]{#char-detect}
For $\gcd(a,q)=1$ and any integer $n$,

$$\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi \bmod q}\overline{\chi(a)}\,\chi(n) = \begin{cases}1 & n\equiv a \bmod q\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
:::

:::proof
If $\gcd(n,q)>1$ every term vanishes, and $n\not\equiv a$ since $a$ is coprime to $q$; both sides are $0$. Otherwise let $a^{*}$ be an inverse of $a$ mod $q$. Then $\overline{\chi(a)} = \chi(a)^{-1} = \chi(a^{*})$ by the proposition *Character values are roots of unity*, so the sum is $\sum_{\chi}\chi(a^{*}n)$, which by the theorem *Orthogonality of characters* is $\varphi(q)$ if $a^{*}n\equiv1$, i.e. $n\equiv a$, and $0$ otherwise.
:::

:::example[Counting characters and evaluating a detector]
How many Dirichlet characters are there modulo $12$? Verify the corollary *Characters detect a residue class* at $q=4$, $a=1$, $n=5$.
:::

:::solution
1. $\varphi(12) = 12\left(1-\tfrac12\right)\left(1-\tfrac13\right) = 4$, so there are four characters mod $12$.
2. For $q=4$ there are two characters, $\chi_{0}$ and $\chi_{1}$, from the example *The characters modulo $4$*.
3. $\tfrac12\left(\overline{\chi_{0}(1)}\chi_{0}(5) + \overline{\chi_{1}(1)}\chi_{1}(5)\right) = \tfrac12(1\cdot1 + 1\cdot1) = 1$, and indeed $5\equiv1 \bmod 4$.
4. With $n=7$ instead: $\tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$, and $7\equiv3\not\equiv1$. The two characters are exactly enough to separate the two classes.
:::

## Reducing the theorem to one non-vanishing

<!-- section: dirichlet-nonvanishing -->

We can now prove Dirichlet's theorem. The structure is Euler's proof that $\sum1/p$ diverges, run one residue class at a time; the character detector is what isolates the class, and the non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$ is what stops the non-principal characters from cancelling the divergence.

:::theorem[Density of primes in a residue class]{#density-residue}
Let $\gcd(a,q)=1$. Then, as $\sigma\downarrow1$ through real values,

$$\sum_{p\equiv a \ (q)}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\log\frac{1}{\sigma-1} + O(1).$$
:::

:::proof
By the corollary *Characters detect a residue class*,

$$\sum_{p\equiv a\ (q)}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi}\overline{\chi(a)}\sum_{p}\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{\sigma}} . \tag{1}$$

So everything reduces to the behaviour of $P(\sigma,\chi):=\sum_{p}\chi(p)p^{-\sigma}$ as $\sigma\downarrow1$. Exactly as in the proof of the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals*, take the logarithm of the theorem *Euler product for $L(s,\chi)$* — using the branch of $\log$ obtained by summing the series, which is legitimate for $\sigma>1$ since $|\chi(p)p^{-\sigma}|<1$:

$$\log L(\sigma,\chi) = \sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\chi(p)^{k}}{k\,p^{k\sigma}} = P(\sigma,\chi) + R(\sigma,\chi), \qquad |R(\sigma,\chi)| \le \sum_{p}\frac{1}{p(p-1)} \le 1 .$$

So $P(\sigma,\chi) = \log L(\sigma,\chi) + O(1)$, uniformly in $\sigma>1$. Two cases.

*The principal character.* By the proposition *The principal character gives back zeta*, $L(\sigma,\chi_{0}) = \zeta(\sigma)\prod_{p\mid q}(1-p^{-\sigma})$, and the finite product tends to the nonzero constant $\varphi(q)/q$. With the corollary *Simple pole at $s=1$ with residue $1$* this gives

$$\log L(\sigma,\chi_{0}) = \log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+O(1).$$

*Non-principal characters.* Here is where the hard input is used. By the theorem *Non-principal L-series converge for $\sigma>0$*, $L(s,\chi)$ is holomorphic at $s=1$, so $L(\sigma,\chi)\to L(1,\chi)$ as $\sigma\downarrow1$; and by the theorem *Non-vanishing of $L(1,\chi)$*, that limit is **not zero**. Therefore $\log L(\sigma,\chi)$ stays bounded, i.e. $P(\sigma,\chi) = O(1)$.

(A word on branches: $\log L(\sigma,\chi)$ here means the value of the convergent double series, which is a continuous function of $\sigma$ on $(1,\infty)$; it differs from any fixed branch of the logarithm by a constant multiple of $2\pi i$, and boundedness of its real part — which is all we use, since the left side of (1) is real — follows from $|L(\sigma,\chi)|$ being bounded above and away from $0$.)

Substituting into (1): the $\chi_{0}$ term contributes $\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\overline{\chi_{0}(a)}\log\frac{1}{\sigma-1} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}$, and each of the other $\varphi(q)-1$ terms contributes $O(1)$. Since there are finitely many characters, the total error is $O(1)$.
:::

:::corollary[Dirichlet's theorem, proved]{#dirichlet-proved}
For $\gcd(a,q)=1$ the progression $a \bmod q$ contains infinitely many primes, and $\sum_{p\equiv a\ (q)}1/p$ diverges.
:::

:::proof
By the theorem *Density of primes in a residue class* the sum $\sum_{p\equiv a}p^{-\sigma}$ tends to $+\infty$ as $\sigma\downarrow1$. A finite set of primes would make it bounded, so the set is infinite. And if $\sum_{p\equiv a}1/p$ converged to $S$, then $\sum_{p\equiv a}p^{-\sigma}\le S$ for every $\sigma>1$, again contradicting the divergence — the same final step as in the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals*.
:::

:::remark
Notice how little of the argument is analytic. Orthogonality is finite group theory; the Euler product is unique factorization; the pole at $s=1$ is an integral comparison. The single imported fact is $L(1,\chi)\neq0$, and the proof would collapse without it: if some $L(1,\chi)$ vanished, that character's term in (1) would contribute $-\log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}$ times a root of unity and could cancel the principal term exactly. A vanishing $L(1,\chi)$ would not merely break the proof — it would make the progression finite.
:::

:::example[Summing the density over all classes]
Check that the theorem *Density of primes in a residue class* is consistent with the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals* for $q=5$, and compare with the counts of primes below $10^{4}$ in each class.
:::

:::solution
1. Summing the theorem over the $\varphi(5)=4$ admissible classes $a=1,2,3,4$ gives $\sum_{p \neq 5}p^{-\sigma} = 4\cdot\tfrac14\log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+O(1) = \log\frac{1}{\sigma-1}+O(1)$.
2. That is exactly the theorem *Divergence of the sum of prime reciprocals*, up to the single omitted prime $p=5$, which contributes $O(1)$. The two results agree.
3. Empirically below $10^{4}$ there are $1229$ primes, of which $1226$ are coprime to $5$; the four classes $1,2,3,4$ hold $306, 308, 309, 303$ primes.
4. The prediction $1226/4 = 306.5$ is within $3$ of every one of them — equidistribution is already sharp at this height, even though the theorem only claims a statement about densities in the limit.
:::

## Equidistribution and prime races

<!-- section: dirichlet-pnt-ap -->

Dirichlet's theorem says each admissible class is infinite. The quantitative theorem says more: they are infinite in equal proportions.

:::theorem[Equidistribution among residue classes]{#equidistribution}
For $\gcd(a,q)=\gcd(b,q)=1$,

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\pi(x;q,a)}{\pi(x;q,b)} = 1, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\pi(x;q,a)}{\pi(x)} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)} .$$
:::

:::proof
Both statements are immediate from the theorem *Prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions*: each of $\pi(x;q,a)$ and $\pi(x;q,b)$ is $\sim x/(\varphi(q)\log x)$, so their ratio tends to $1$, and dividing by $\pi(x)\sim x/\log x$ gives $1/\varphi(q)$. The content is entirely in the theorem quoted; what is worth noting is that the theorem *Density of primes in a residue class* already gives the *analytic density* version — the $\varphi(q)$ classes have equal Dirichlet density $1/\varphi(q)$ — with only $L(1,\chi)\neq0$ as input, whereas natural density needs the full non-vanishing on the line $\sigma=1$.
:::

:::remark
Equal in the limit is not equal at any finite height. Chebyshev observed in 1853 that $\pi(x;4,3)$ exceeds $\pi(x;4,1)$ for almost every $x$ one checks — the figure *Primes below $10^{k}$ split between the classes $1$ and $3$ modulo $4$* shows the lead persisting across four orders of magnitude. Littlewood proved the lead changes hands infinitely often, and the first crossing is at $x = 26861$. The bias is real but it is a bias in *logarithmic density*, not a violation of equidistribution: the class $3 \bmod 4$ leads about $99.6\%$ of the time, yet the ratio still tends to $1$.
:::

:::example[Density in a class modulo $10$]
What proportion of primes ends in the digit $7$? What proportion of the first $10^{5}$ integers' primes should that predict, given $\pi(10^{5})=9592$?
:::

:::solution
1. Ending in $7$ means $p\equiv7\bmod10$, and $\gcd(7,10)=1$, so the class is admissible.
2. $\varphi(10)=4$ (classes $1,3,7,9$), so the density is $1/4$ by the theorem *Equidistribution among residue classes*.
3. Prediction: $9592/4 = 2398$ primes below $10^{5}$ ending in $7$. The true count is $2402$ — an error of $0.17\%$.
4. The digits $0,2,4,5,6,8$ get no primes at all beyond $2$ and $5$: they are the six inadmissible classes, with $\gcd>1$.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating $\zeta(s)=\sum n^{-s}$ as valid everywhere.** The series converges only for $\operatorname{Re}(s)>1$. Statements like "$\zeta(-1)=1+2+3+\cdots=-1/12$" are shorthand for the value of the *continuation*, not for a sum. The series has nothing to say at $s=-1$.
- **Concluding $L(1,\chi)\neq0$ from the Euler product.** The Euler product holds only where the series converges absolutely, i.e. $\sigma>1$. At $s=1$ it is not available, and the non-vanishing there is a theorem with a genuinely hard proof for real characters.
- **Thinking the prime number theorem is about $x/\log x$ specifically.** It is an asymptotic statement; $\operatorname{li}(x)$ is asymptotically the same and numerically far better. At $x=10^{6}$ the error of $x/\log x$ is fifty times that of $\operatorname{li}(x)$, and the ratio grows.
- **Believing RH has been proved because zeros have been checked.** Over $10^{13}$ zeros have been verified to lie on the critical line. That is evidence, not proof, and comparable evidence has misled before — Skewes' number shows $\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}(x)$ changes sign despite being negative for every computed $x$.
- **Forgetting the coprimality hypothesis in Dirichlet's theorem.** The progression $6n+4$ contains no primes at all. Infinitude needs $\gcd(a,q)=1$, and the theorem gives nothing without it.
- **Confusing $\pi(x)\sim\operatorname{li}(x)$ with $\pi(x)-\operatorname{li}(x)\to0$.** An asymptotic equivalence controls the *ratio*. The difference is unbounded; RH asserts only that it is $O(\sqrt x\log x)$.
- **Assuming $\theta(x)$, $\psi(x)$ and $\pi(x)\log x$ are interchangeable in error terms.** They are interchangeable in *asymptotics*, by the proposition *Chebyshev functions are interchangeable*, but $\psi-\theta$ is of size $\sqrt x$ — the same order as the error term RH predicts, so the distinction matters exactly when the error term does.
- **Reading Chebyshev's bias as a failure of equidistribution.** $\pi(x;4,3)$ leads almost always, but the ratio of the counts still tends to $1$, and the lead changes hands infinitely often.
