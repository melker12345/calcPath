# Classical Theorems

Fermat's and Wilson's theorems, quadratic reciprocity, and Gauss sums.

Fermat's Little Theorem is one of the most useful and beautiful results in elementary number theory. It provides a powerful necessary condition for primality and a practical way to reduce exponents when working modulo a prime.

Stated simply: if $p$ is prime and $a$ is not divisible by $p$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. Equivalently $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ (this form holds even if $p$ divides $a$).

The theorem is the foundation for the Fermat primality test (probabilistic), underlies RSA and Diffie-Hellman exponentiation, and is a special case of Euler's theorem. Its proof can be given via group theory (order of multiplicative group) or combinatorially (necklaces) or by induction using binomial theorem.

## Statement in Two Forms

<!-- section: fermat-statement -->

:::theorem[Fermat's little theorem]{#fermat-little-theorem}
If $p$ is a prime and $\gcd(a,p) = 1$, then

$$
a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}.
$$

In the second form, sometimes more convenient,

$$
a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}
$$

for any integer $a$ (no coprimeness needed).
:::

:::proof
The second form follows from the first: when $p \nmid a$, multiply $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$ by $a$ to get $a^p \equiv a$; and when $p \mid a$, both $a^p$ and $a$ are $\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, so it holds trivially.
:::

The coprimeness hypothesis $\gcd(a,p)=1$ is essential for the first form — without it the statement is simply false (e.g. $5^4 = 625 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, not $1$).

:::intuition
Picture a clock with $p-1$ hours. Any starting number that is not a multiple of $p$ walks around this clock as you take powers, and after exactly $p-1$ steps (or a divisor of that) it lands back on $1$. Raising to the $(p-1)$ power always returns you to noon.

The $a^p \equiv a$ form says: a prime exponent hands the number back unchanged. It's like the number goes on a round trip and comes home to itself.
:::

:::example[Verify both forms for $p=5$, $a=3$.]
1. First form, $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$. Compute $3^4 = 81$. Divide by $5$: $81 = 16 \cdot 5 + 1$, so the remainder is $1$. Hence $3^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$. ✓ (And $\gcd(3,5)=1$, so the first form applies.)
2. Second form, $a^p \equiv a$. Compute $3^5 = 243$. Divide by $5$: $243 = 48 \cdot 5 + 3$, remainder $3$. Hence $3^5 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$. ✓
3. Sanity check that the two forms are consistent: multiplying Step 1's result by $a=3$ gives $3^5 = 3 \cdot 3^4 \equiv 3 \cdot 1 = 3 \pmod 5$, matching Step 2.
:::

## Group-Theoretic Proof (Lagrange)

<!-- section: group-proof -->

The multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ of nonzero residues mod $p$ has exactly $p-1$ elements and is a group under multiplication mod $p$. (It is a group precisely because $p$ is prime: every nonzero residue then has a multiplicative inverse.)

:::definition[Order of an element]{#element-order}
The **order** of an element $a$ is the smallest positive integer $d$ with $a^d \equiv 1$.
:::

By Lagrange's theorem, the order of any element divides the group order $p-1$, and that is all Fermat's little theorem needs.

:::proof
Write $p-1 = d \cdot k$ where $d$ is the order of $a$. Then

$$
a^{p-1} = a^{dk} = (a^{d})^{k} \equiv 1^k = 1 \pmod{p},
$$

which is exactly Fermat's little theorem.
:::

This is the shortest modern proof once one knows basic group theory. A purely elementary proof avoids Lagrange.

:::proof
Consider the numbers $a, 2a, \dots, (p-1)a$. They are all nonzero mod $p$ (a product of two nonzero residues is nonzero since $p$ is prime) and pairwise distinct (if $ia \equiv ja$ then $i \equiv j$ because $a$ is invertible). So they are just a rearrangement of $1, 2, \dots, p-1$. Taking the product of each list and equating:
$$
a \cdot 2a \cdots (p-1)a = a^{p-1}\,(p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod{p}.
$$
Since $(p-1)!$ is not divisible by $p$ (none of its factors is), it is invertible mod $p$; cancel it from both sides to get $a^{p-1} \equiv 1$.
:::

:::intuition
The nonzero numbers mod $p$ form a "club" of $p-1$ members closed under multiplication (everyone has an inverse). Raising any member to the power of the club size always brings it back to the "identity" $1$.

The permutation proof: multiplying the whole set by $a$ just shuffles it into a new order, so the product of the set gets multiplied by $a^{p-1}$ — but a reshuffled set has the same product, so that extra factor $a^{p-1}$ must be $1$.
:::

:::example[Using the permutation argument for $p=7$, $a=3$.]
1. Multiply the set $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ by $3$ mod $7$:
2. $$3\cdot1=3,\quad 3\cdot2=6,\quad 3\cdot3=9\equiv2,\quad 3\cdot4=12\equiv5,\quad 3\cdot5=15\equiv1,\quad 3\cdot6=18\equiv4.$$
3. The results are $\{3,6,2,5,1,4\}$ — the same six residues $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ in a new order.
4. Equate the two products. The reshuffled product equals the original: $6! = 720$. But it also equals $3^6 \cdot 6!$ (one factor of $3$ pulled from each of the six terms). So $3^6 \cdot 720 \equiv 720 \pmod 7$, and cancelling $720$ (which is coprime to $7$) gives $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.
5. Sanity check directly: $3^6 = 729 = 104 \cdot 7 + 1$, so $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$. ✓
:::

## Fermat Primality Test and Pseudoprimes

<!-- section: fermat-test -->

If $p$ is prime, then for any $a$ coprime to $p$ we have $a^{p-1}\equiv 1 \pmod p$. Read this as a contrapositive.

:::corollary[Fermat compositeness test]
If for some base $a$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$ we find

$$a^{n-1} \not\equiv 1 \pmod n,$$

then $n$ cannot be prime — it is **definitely composite**, and $a$ is called a *witness* to compositeness.
:::

This is the Fermat test. Its weakness is the other direction: $a^{n-1}\equiv 1$ does *not* prove primality. If $n$ passes the test for several randomly chosen bases we only say $n$ is a *probable prime*.

Worse, there exist composite numbers that pass the test for **every** base $a$ coprime to them.

:::definition[Carmichael number]{#carmichael}
A **Carmichael number** is a composite $n$ with $a^{n-1}\equiv 1 \pmod n$ for every $a$ coprime to $n$, so Fermat's test never catches it.
:::

:::theorem[Korselt's criterion]
The Carmichael numbers are exactly the square-free composites $n$ such that for every prime $p \mid n$, the divisibility $p-1 \mid n-1$ holds.
:::

The smallest Carmichael number is $561 = 3 \cdot 11 \cdot 17$.

:::intuition
Think of the Fermat test as a lie detector for "I am prime." If a number fails the $a^{n-1}\equiv 1$ test for even one $a$, it was caught lying and is certainly composite. Passing many tests only means "no lie detected yet" — good enough for practical crypto, but not a proof.

Carmichael numbers are master liars that pass the detector for every legal base. They are rare but must be guarded against with stronger tests such as Miller–Rabin.
:::

:::example[Why $561$ fools the Fermat test.]
1. Factor: $561 = 3 \cdot 11 \cdot 17$, which is square-free.
2. Check Korselt's criterion. Here $n-1 = 560$. For each prime factor $p$, we need $p-1 \mid 560$:
3. $$3-1 = 2 \mid 560,\qquad 11-1 = 10 \mid 560,\qquad 17-1 = 16 \mid 560.$$
4. Indeed $560 = 2\cdot 280 = 10\cdot 56 = 16\cdot 35$, so all three divide $560$. ✓
5. Conclusion. By Korselt's criterion $561$ is a Carmichael number: for every $a$ coprime to $561$ we get $a^{560}\equiv 1 \pmod{561}$, so the Fermat test declares "probably prime" for every legal base — yet $561$ is composite. This is why real primality checking uses Miller–Rabin, which no composite can fool for more than a quarter of the bases.
:::

## Applications to Exponentiation

<!-- section: fermat-exponentiation -->

:::proposition[Reducing the exponent mod $p-1$]
When computing $a^k \bmod p$ with $p$ prime and $\gcd(a,p)=1$, reduce the exponent $k$ modulo $p-1$:

$$
a^k \equiv a^{\,k \bmod (p-1)} \pmod p.
$$
:::

:::proof
Write $k = q(p-1) + r$. By Fermat's little theorem $a^{p-1}\equiv 1$, so full "laps" of size $p-1$ in the exponent do nothing: $a^k = (a^{p-1})^q a^r \equiv a^r \pmod p$.
:::

Caution: you reduce the *exponent* mod $p-1$, but the *base* mod $p$ — mixing these up is a common slip.

This makes enormous exponents feasible. Combined with repeated squaring (binary exponentiation), even $2048$-bit exponents mod a $2048$-bit prime are fast.

In RSA the private exponent $d$ satisfies $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$, and by Euler's theorem (the composite-modulus generalization of Fermat) the powers cycle with period dividing $\varphi(n)$.

:::intuition
The exponents live on their own smaller clock of size $p-1$. To know which power you land on, you only need the time on that little clock — the big exponent's full laps don't matter.

Binary exponentiation is like reading the (reduced) exponent in binary and only ever squaring or multiplying, roughly $\log_2 k$ times, instead of multiplying $k$ times.
:::

:::example[Reduce $3^{1000} \bmod 7$.]
1. Check the hypothesis: $\gcd(3,7)=1$, so we may reduce the exponent mod $p-1 = 6$.
2. Reduce the exponent: $1000 = 166 \cdot 6 + 4$, so $1000 \equiv 4 \pmod 6$.
3. Replace the exponent: $3^{1000} \equiv 3^{4} \pmod 7$.
4. Evaluate the small power: $3^4 = 81 = 11 \cdot 7 + 4$, so $3^4 \equiv 4$. Therefore $3^{1000} \equiv 4 \pmod 7$.
5. Sanity check: $3^{1000} = 3^{6\cdot 166}\cdot 3^4 = (3^6)^{166}\cdot 3^4 \equiv 1^{166}\cdot 81 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying without the coprimeness check**: If $p \mid a$ then $a^{p-1}\equiv 0 \not\equiv 1$. Always verify $\gcd(a,p)=1$ before using the $a^{p-1}$ form.
- **Using it for composite $n$ as a primality proof**: Passing the Fermat test for a few bases does not prove primality (Carmichael counterexamples). Use Miller–Rabin or full factorization for a proof.
- **Forgetting to reduce the exponent correctly**: The reduction $a^k = a^{q(p-1)+r} = (a^{p-1})^q\, a^r \equiv 1^q\, a^r$ only holds when $\gcd(a,p)=1$. And reduce the exponent mod $p-1$, not mod $p$.
- **Confusing it with Euler's theorem**: Fermat is the special case of Euler when the modulus is a prime $p$ (there $\varphi(p)=p-1$). Euler's theorem $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1$ works for composite moduli too, but $\varphi(n)$ is harder to compute.
- **The $p=2$ case**: For $p=2$ and $a$ odd, $a^{1}\equiv 1 \pmod 2$, which holds.

Wilson's Theorem gives a beautiful characterization of prime numbers using factorials: $(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$ if and only if $p$ is prime.

It is less computationally useful than Fermat's Little Theorem for primality testing (factorials are expensive), but it is theoretically elegant, has nice proofs, and appears in the study of the multiplicative group mod p and in certain combinatorial number theory results.

The theorem is named after John Wilson (though known earlier to Ibn al-Haytham). It provides an if-and-only-if criterion unlike the one-way Fermat test.

## Statement of Wilson's Theorem

<!-- section: wilson-statement -->

:::theorem[Wilson's theorem]{#wilson}
A positive integer $p > 1$ is prime if and only if

$$
(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{p},
$$

equivalently, if and only if $(p-1)! + 1$ is divisible by $p$.
:::

For composite $n > 4$ the congruence fails — instead $(n-1)! \equiv 0$. We prove the two directions separately in the sections below. The lone composite exception to the "$\equiv 0$" behavior is $n=4$, which must be checked by hand: $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$ (neither $0$ nor $-1\equiv 3$). The small primes are also direct: for $p=2$, $1! = 1 \equiv -1 \pmod 2$ (since $-1\equiv 1 \pmod 2$); for $p=3$, $2! = 2 \equiv -1 \pmod 3$.

:::intuition
For a prime $p$, when you multiply all the numbers from $1$ to $p-1$ together, the result lands exactly one short of a multiple of $p$. It's a property of the entire set $\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}$ acting together, not of any single number.

For a composite modulus the same product instead swallows all of $n$'s factors, so it lands squarely on a multiple of $n$ (remainder $0$), not one short.
:::

:::example[Check both directions for $p=7$ (prime) and $n=9$ (composite).]
1. Prime case $p=7$. Compute $6! = 720$. Then $720 + 1 = 721 = 7 \cdot 103$ exactly, so $7 \mid 6!+1$, i.e. $6! \equiv -1 \pmod 7$. ✓ (Matches Wilson: $7$ is prime.)
2. Composite case $n=9$. Compute $8! = 40320$. The factors $3$ and $6$ both appear in $8!$, contributing two factors of $3$, so $9 \mid 8!$. Confirm: the digit sum $4+0+3+2+0 = 9$ is divisible by $9$, so $8! \equiv 0 \pmod 9$.
3. Since $-1 \equiv 8 \pmod 9$ and $0 \neq 8$, we have $8! \not\equiv -1 \pmod 9$. ✓ (Matches Wilson: $9$ is composite.)
:::

## Proof that Prime Implies the Congruence

<!-- section: prime-implies-wilson -->

Assume $p$ is prime, so $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field (every nonzero residue has an inverse, and $x^2 \equiv 1$ has at most two solutions). The cleanest argument is the **inverse-pairing** proof.

:::proof
We want the product $(p-1)! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdots (p-1)$, i.e. the product of all nonzero residues. Pair each residue $a$ with its multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$. Two elements pair with themselves rather than a distinct partner exactly when they are their **own** inverse:
$$
a \equiv a^{-1} \iff a^2 \equiv 1 \iff (a-1)(a+1) \equiv 0 \pmod p.
$$
Because $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field, a product is $0$ only if a factor is $0$, so $a \equiv 1$ or $a \equiv -1 \equiv p-1$. These are the only two self-inverse elements. Every other residue pairs off with a *distinct* partner, and each such pair multiplies to $1$.

Grouping the product accordingly:
$$
(p-1)! \equiv \underbrace{1}_{\text{self-inv}} \cdot \underbrace{(p-1)}_{\;\equiv\, -1} \cdot \underbrace{(\text{product of pairs})}_{\text{each} = 1} \equiv 1 \cdot (-1) \cdot 1 \equiv -1 \pmod p.
$$
:::

:::intuition
In the "club" of $1$ to $p-1$, everyone has a dance partner they multiply to $1$ with (their inverse), except the two loners $1$ and $p-1 \equiv -1$, who partner with themselves. All the dancing pairs cancel to $1$, leaving just $1 \cdot (-1) = -1$.

This works only because $x^2 = 1$ has *exactly* two solutions in a field — in a composite modulus there can be more self-inverse elements, and the tidy cancellation breaks.
:::

:::example[Pairing for $p=5$.]
1. List the nonzero residues: $1, 2, 3, 4$.
2. Find the self-inverse ones. $1\cdot 1 = 1$, so $1$ is self-inverse. $4\cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $4 \equiv -1$ is self-inverse. These are the expected two.
3. Pair the rest: $2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, so $2$ and $3$ form a partner pair multiplying to $1$.
4. Multiply it all: $4! = 1 \cdot 4 \cdot (2\cdot 3) \equiv 1 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = 4 \equiv -1 \pmod 5$. ✓
5. Sanity check: directly, $4! = 24 = 5\cdot 5 - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod 5$. ✓
:::

## Proof that the Congruence Implies Prime (contrapositive)

<!-- section: composite-implies-not-wilson -->

:::proposition[Factorials of composite moduli]
If $n > 1$ is composite then $(n-1)! \not\equiv -1 \pmod n$; and for composite $n > 4$ the sharper statement

$$(n-1)! \equiv 0 \pmod n$$

holds.
:::

This is the contrapositive of the remaining half of Wilson's theorem. The strategy is to exhibit factors of $n$ hiding inside the product $1 \cdot 2 \cdots (n-1)$.

:::proof
**Case 1: $n = ab$ with distinct factors $1 < a < b < n$.** Both $a$ and $b$ are among $2, \dots, n-1$, so both appear as separate terms in $(n-1)!$. Their product $ab = n$ therefore divides $(n-1)!$, giving $(n-1)! \equiv 0 \pmod n$.

**Case 2: $n = p^2$ for a prime $p$ (a perfect square, so we cannot pick two distinct factors as above).** As long as $p^2 > 4$, i.e. $p \geq 3$, we have $2p < p^2 = n$, so both $p$ and $2p$ lie in $\{2, \dots, n-1\}$ as *distinct* terms of the factorial. Together they supply two factors of $p$, so $p^2 = n$ divides $(n-1)!$, again $(n-1)! \equiv 0 \pmod n$. (Higher powers $p^k$ with $k \geq 2$ fall under Case 1 with $a=p^{k-1}, b=p$ once $n>4$.)

**Case 3: $n = 4$.** This is the single exception the two cases above miss (here $p=2$, and $2p = 4 = n$ is *not* less than $n$). Check by hand: $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, which is neither $0$ nor $-1 \equiv 3$. So Wilson still fails, just not by giving $0$.

In every composite case the congruence $(n-1)! \equiv -1$ fails, completing the proof.
:::

:::intuition
Once a composite is "big enough," its prime factors already appear as separate numbers in the product $1 \cdot 2 \cdots (n-1)$, long before you reach $n$ itself. So the whole product is divisible by $n$ and ends in remainder $0$ — never one short.

Only a prime has no smaller building blocks sneaking into the product ahead of it, which is why primes are the sole numbers that hit the "$-1$" value.
:::

:::example[$n = 9 = 3^2$ (Case 2).]
1. We need two factors of $3$ inside $8!$. The terms $3$ and $6 = 2\cdot 3$ both appear and each carries a $3$, so $8!$ is divisible by $3 \cdot 3 = 9$.
2. Confirm numerically: $8! = 40320$, and its digit sum $4+0+3+2+0 = 9$ is divisible by $9$, so $8! \equiv 0 \pmod 9$.
3. Compare with $-1 \equiv 8 \pmod 9$. Since $0 \neq 8$, indeed $8! \not\equiv -1 \pmod 9$. ✓
:::

## Relation to Other Theorems

<!-- section: relations-other-theorems -->

Wilson's Theorem is equivalent to the statement that the product of all units in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ equals $-1$. It mainly stands as a clean characterization of primality rather than a computational tool.

There is a natural strengthening.

:::definition[Wilson prime]
A **Wilson prime** is a prime $p$ for which the congruence holds even modulo $p^2$:

$$(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{p^2},$$

equivalently $p^2 \mid (p-1)! + 1$.
:::

Only three are known: $5$, $13$, and $563$; whether there are infinitely many is open.

Where Fermat describes each individual member's behavior ("each unit raised to $p-1$ is $1$"), Wilson describes the whole set at once ("the product of all units is $-1$"). The two together pin down structure of the multiplicative group mod $p$.

:::intuition
Fermat is a statement about each club member on its own; Wilson is a statement about the whole club multiplied together. One is local, the other global.

A Wilson prime is a rare coincidence where that global product is $-1$ not just on the ordinary clock of size $p$, but on the much finer clock of size $p^2$.
:::

:::example[Is $p = 5$ a Wilson prime?]
1. Ordinary Wilson first: $(p-1)! = 4! = 24$, and $24 + 1 = 25$. Since $5 \mid 25$, ordinary Wilson holds. ✓
2. Strengthen to mod $p^2 = 25$: we need $25 \mid (p-1)! + 1 = 25$. Indeed $25 = 1 \cdot 25$, so $25 \mid 25$. ✓
3. Conclusion: $(5-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{25}$, so $5$ is a Wilson prime. (Contrast $p=7$: $6!+1 = 721 = 7\cdot 103$, and $721 / 49 = 14.7\ldots$ is not an integer, so $7$ is *not* a Wilson prime.)
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Using it for primality testing in practice**: Computing $(p-1)! \bmod p$ for large $p$ takes about $p$ multiplications — far slower than Fermat or Miller–Rabin. Wilson's is theoretical, not practical.
- **Forgetting the composite direction has an exception at $n=4$**: $4$ is the one composite where $(n-1)!\not\equiv 0$; instead $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$. It still fails Wilson (since $2 \neq -1 \equiv 3$), but must be handled separately.
- **Sign error**: it's $\equiv -1$, not $+1$. Equivalently, $(p-1)! + 1$ is divisible by $p$.
- **Applying it to $n = 1$ or $n = 0$**: the statement is for integers $p > 1$; $0!=1$ by convention but is not in scope.
- **Confusing $(p-1)!$ with $p!$ or $(p+1)!$**: the product runs exactly up to $p-1$, excluding $p$ itself (including $p$ would force the product to $0 \bmod p$).

The law of quadratic reciprocity is one of the deepest and most beautiful theorems in elementary number theory. It relates the Legendre symbols $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)$ and $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ for distinct odd primes $p$ and $q$ by a simple sign that depends only on their residues mod $4$.

(Recall the **Legendre symbol** $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$, for an odd prime $p \nmid a$, equals $+1$ if $a$ is a nonzero square mod $p$ — a *quadratic residue* — and $-1$ if it is a non-residue; it is $0$ when $p \mid a$.)

Together with the supplementary laws for -1 and 2, it gives a complete algorithm to compute any Legendre or Jacobi symbol in time O(log n), the same as computing gcd.

The theorem has many proofs (Gauss gave 8), using Gauss sums, cyclotomy, geometry, etc. It is the prototype for class field theory reciprocity laws.

## Statement

<!-- section: reciprocity-statement -->

:::theorem[Law of quadratic reciprocity]{#quadratic-reciprocity}
For distinct odd primes $p$ and $q$,

$$
\left( \frac{p}{q} \right) \left( \frac{q}{p} \right) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2 \cdot (q-1)/2}.
$$
:::

Equivalently: $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ if at least one of $p, q$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$; and $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) = -\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$ if **both** are $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$. The sign flips precisely when both $(p-1)/2$ and $(q-1)/2$ are odd, which happens exactly when both primes are $3 \pmod 4$.

:::intuition
Whether $p$ is a square mod $q$ is almost the same question as whether $q$ is a square mod $p$ — you get the same answer, except in the one case where both primes are $3 \bmod 4$, when the answer flips sign.

The flip happens exactly when both "clocks" have an odd "half-size" $(p-1)/2$.
:::

:::example[Relate $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)$ and $\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)$, then evaluate them.]
1. Determine the sign. Here $(3-1)/2 = 1$ (odd) and $(5-1)/2 = 2$ (even); their product is even, so $(-1)^{1\cdot 2} = +1$. Note $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so we are in the "no flip" case: $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{5}{3}\right)$.
2. Evaluate the easier side. Reduce $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, so $\left(\frac{5}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)$. By the supplementary law for $2$ (below), $\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = -1$ because $3 \equiv 3 \pmod 8$.
3. Transfer via reciprocity: $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = \left(\frac{5}{3}\right) = -1$, so $3$ is a non-residue mod $5$.
4. Sanity check: the nonzero squares mod $5$ are $1^2=1$, $2^2=4$, $3^2\equiv4$, $4^2\equiv1$, i.e. $\{1,4\}$. Since $3 \notin \{1,4\}$, indeed $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = -1$. ✓
:::

## Supplementary Laws for -1 and 2

<!-- section: supplementary-laws -->

:::theorem[Supplementary laws for $-1$ and $2$]{#supplementary-laws}
For an odd prime $p$,

$$
\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2} = \begin{cases} +1 & p \equiv 1 \pmod 4,\\ -1 & p \equiv 3 \pmod 4,\end{cases}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{2}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p^2 - 1)/8} = \begin{cases} +1 & p \equiv 1 \text{ or } 7 \pmod 8,\\ -1 & p \equiv 3 \text{ or } 5 \pmod 8.\end{cases}
$$
:::

These are exactly what the main reciprocity law cannot handle, because reciprocity is stated for two *odd primes*. Together, main law plus supplements let you evaluate any $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$: reduce $a$ mod $p$, split off the sign $-1$ and the factors of $2$ with the rules above, and flip the remaining odd prime factors using reciprocity — an Euclid-style descent.

:::intuition
These are the two "cheat sheet" cases the flipping rule leaves out: is $-1$ a square (look at $p \bmod 4$) and is $2$ a square (look at $p \bmod 8$).

With them you can handle any $a$ by first stripping off its sign and any factors of $2$, leaving an odd positive number to flip.
:::

:::example[Compute $\left(\frac{-3}{7}\right)$.]
1. Factor the top and split the symbol: $\left(\frac{-3}{7}\right) = \left(\frac{-1}{7}\right)\left(\frac{3}{7}\right)$.
2. The $-1$ part: $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so $\left(\frac{-1}{7}\right) = -1$.
3. The $3$ part via reciprocity: $3$ and $7$ are both $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so we flip with a sign: $\left(\frac{3}{7}\right) = -\left(\frac{7}{3}\right)$. Reduce $7 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so $\left(\frac{7}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 1$. Hence $\left(\frac{3}{7}\right) = -1$.
4. Multiply: $\left(\frac{-3}{7}\right) = (-1)(-1) = +1$.
5. Sanity check: is $-3 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$ a square? Yes, $2^2 = 4$. So $\left(\frac{-3}{7}\right) = +1$. ✓
:::

## The Algorithm to Compute the Symbol

<!-- section: reciprocity-algorithm -->

The reciprocity law turns evaluating a symbol into an Euclid-style descent. To compute $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)$ with $n$ an odd positive integer (Legendre if $n$ prime, Jacobi if $n$ merely odd — the reciprocity and supplementary rules hold for the Jacobi symbol too), repeat:

:::recipe[Evaluating a Legendre or Jacobi symbol]
1. **Reduce** $a$ modulo $n$ so that $0 \le a < n$.
2. **Strip the sign and the $2$s**: pull each factor $-1$ out using the $\left(\frac{-1}{n}\right)$ rule and each factor $2$ out using the $\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)$ rule, leaving an odd positive $a$.
3. If $a = 1$, the remaining symbol is $1$; stop.
4. **Flip** by reciprocity: swap to $\left(\frac{n}{a}\right)$, inserting a sign of $-1$ iff both $a \equiv 3$ and $n \equiv 3 \pmod 4$; then go back to step 1 with the roles of $a$ and $n$ swapped.
:::

The moduli shrink just as in $\gcd(a,n)$, so the loop runs in $O(\log n)$ steps. (If at any point $\gcd(a,n) > 1$, the Legendre symbol is $0$.)

:::intuition
The computation follows the same swaps and reductions as computing $\gcd(a,n)$, but at each swap you may flip the sign (when both current numbers are $3 \bmod 4$) and you have special rules for the factor $2$.

The accumulated sign at the end tells you residue ($+1$) or non-residue ($-1$); a leftover common factor means the symbol is $0$.
:::

:::example[Compute $\left(\frac{17}{41}\right)$.]
1. Flip. Both $17 \equiv 1$ and $41 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so no sign: $\left(\frac{17}{41}\right) = \left(\frac{41}{17}\right)$.
2. Reduce: $41 = 2\cdot 17 + 7$, so $\left(\frac{41}{17}\right) = \left(\frac{7}{17}\right)$.
3. Flip. Here $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ but $17 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$; not both $3$, so no sign: $\left(\frac{7}{17}\right) = \left(\frac{17}{7}\right)$.
4. Reduce: $17 = 2\cdot 7 + 3$, so $\left(\frac{17}{7}\right) = \left(\frac{3}{7}\right)$.
5. Flip. Now $3 \equiv 3$ and $7 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, both $3$, so insert a $-1$: $\left(\frac{3}{7}\right) = -\left(\frac{7}{3}\right)$. Reduce $7 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$: $\left(\frac{7}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = 1$. So $\left(\frac{3}{7}\right) = -1$.
6. Back-substitute through Steps 1–4 (all sign-free): $\left(\frac{17}{41}\right) = -1$, so $17$ is a non-residue mod $41$. (A direct check: no $x$ in $1,\dots,40$ satisfies $x^2 \equiv 17 \pmod{41}$.)
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Flipping the sign in the wrong case**: the extra $-1$ appears *only* when both primes are $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$. If either is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, there is no sign change.
- **Reading the Jacobi symbol as a residue test**: for composite $n$, $\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) = 1$ does **not** imply $a$ is a square mod $n$. Only the Legendre symbol (prime $n$) tells you residue status directly.
- **Forgetting to peel off $2$ and $-1$ first**: reciprocity applies only to odd primes, so you must strip the factor $2$ (via the $\left(\frac{2}{\cdot}\right)$ rule) and the sign before flipping.
- **Applying reciprocity when the arguments are not coprime**: check $\gcd$ first; if it exceeds $1$ the Legendre symbol is $0$.
- **Confusing the symbol with a square root**: the algorithm tells you *whether* $a$ is a residue, not *what* its square root is. Extracting the root needs Tonelli–Shanks or Cipolla.

The quadratic Gauss sum is the sum $\tau = \sum_{k=0}^{p-1} \left(\frac{k}{p}\right) e^{2\pi i k / p}$, where $\left(\frac{\cdot}{p}\right)$ is the Legendre symbol, for an odd prime $p$.

Its magnitude is $\sqrt{p}$, and its exact value (real or purely imaginary depending on $p \bmod 4$) is used in Gauss's proof of quadratic reciprocity and in evaluating character sums, class numbers, and more.

Gauss sums are the prototype for more general exponential sums in analytic number theory and have applications in signal processing and coding theory (discrete Fourier).

## Definition and Magnitude

<!-- section: gauss-def -->

:::definition[Quadratic Gauss sum]{#gauss-sum}
For an odd prime $p$, the **quadratic Gauss sum** is

$$
\tau = \sum_{k=0}^{p-1} \left( \frac{k}{p} \right) e^{2\pi i k / p}.
$$
:::

:::theorem[Magnitude and value of the Gauss sum]
It can be shown that $|\tau| = \sqrt{p}$. The exact value depends on $p \bmod 4$: with the standard $+$ sign in the exponent,

$$\tau = \sqrt{p} \ \text{ if } p \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \qquad \tau = i\sqrt{p} \ \text{ if } p \equiv 3 \pmod 4.$$
:::

:::intuition
Think of each term as a little arrow (a $p$-th root of unity) pointing in some direction, weighted $+1$ or $-1$ by whether $k$ is a square mod $p$. The Gauss sum adds all these arrows tip to tail.

Astonishingly, they don't cancel to nothing and they don't add up to $p$ either: the resultant arrow has length exactly $\sqrt{p}$ every time. That precise partial cancellation is the surprise.
:::

:::example[Verify $|\tau| = \sqrt 5$ for $p = 5$.]
1. Legendre values. The nonzero squares mod $5$ are $\{1,4\}$, so $\left(\frac{1}{5}\right)=\left(\frac{4}{5}\right)=1$ and $\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)=\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)=-1$; the $k=0$ term is $0$.
2. Write the sum. Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i / 5}$. Then
3. $$
4. \tau = \omega^1 - \omega^2 - \omega^3 + \omega^4.
5. $$
6. Use the symmetry $\omega^4 = \overline{\omega}$ and $\omega^3 = \overline{\omega^2}$ (conjugates), so $\tau = (\omega + \omega^4) - (\omega^2 + \omega^3) = 2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{5} - 2\cos\tfrac{4\pi}{5}$. Numerically $2\cos 72^\circ \approx 0.618$ and $2\cos 144^\circ \approx -1.618$, giving $\tau \approx 0.618 - (-1.618) = 2.236$.
7. Compare: $\sqrt 5 \approx 2.236$. So $\tau = \sqrt 5$ (real and positive, consistent with $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$), and $|\tau| = \sqrt 5$. ✓
:::

## Evaluation and Sign

<!-- section: gauss-evaluation -->

The exact evaluation of the Gauss sum is a deep result of Gauss: the sum is $\sqrt p$ when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $i\sqrt p$ when $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

The easy half is the *square*.

:::proposition[The square of the Gauss sum]
One shows directly that

$$
\tau^2 = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\, p = \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) p.
$$
:::

So $\tau^2 = p$ when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $\tau^2 = -p$ when $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. This already gives $|\tau| = \sqrt p$ and pins $\tau$ down to one of two candidates ($\pm\sqrt p$ or $\pm i\sqrt p$). The hard half — choosing the correct sign — needs a finer argument (Gauss used a careful count; later proofs use theta functions). Determining that sign is essentially equivalent in depth to quadratic reciprocity, which is why Gauss's sixth proof runs through the Gauss sum.

:::intuition
Squaring the sum is the cheap trick that gives its length; getting the exact *direction* (the sign or the $i$) is the genuinely hard part.

Once you know that direction — real and positive for $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, purely imaginary for $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ — a cascade of explicit formulas opens up.
:::

:::example[Evaluate $\tau$ and verify $\tau^2 = -p$ for $p = 3$ (so $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$).]
1. Legendre values: $\left(\frac{0}{3}\right)=0$, $\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)=1$, $\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)=-1$ (since $2$ is not a square mod $3$; the only nonzero square is $1$).
2. With $\omega = e^{2\pi i / 3}$, the sum is
3. $$
4. \tau = 1\cdot\omega^1 + (-1)\cdot\omega^2 = \omega - \omega^2.
5. $$
6. Since $\omega = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ and $\omega^2 = \overline{\omega} = -\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$, we get $\tau = \omega - \omega^2 = \sqrt3\, i$. This matches the rule $\tau = i\sqrt p$ for $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.
7. Sanity check the square: $\tau^2 = (i\sqrt3)^2 = -3 = -p$, agreeing with $\tau^2 = (-1)^{(3-1)/2}\,p = (-1)^1\cdot 3 = -3$. ✓
:::

## Use in Proof of Reciprocity

<!-- section: gauss-reciprocity-proof -->

Gauss's sixth proof of quadratic reciprocity works inside the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ of the cyclotomic field, where $\zeta = e^{2\pi i / p}$. The idea is to compute the $q$-th power of the Gauss sum $\tau$ in two different ways modulo $q$ (for the other prime $q$), and compare. One route gives $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)$; the other, using that raising to the $q$-th power is a ring homomorphism mod $q$ (the "freshman's dream" $(x+y)^q \equiv x^q + y^q$), gives $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)$ times the sign from $\tau^2 = \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)p$. Equating the two yields the reciprocity relation.

:::intuition
The Gauss sum packs "which numbers are squares mod $p$" into a single complex number. Doing algebra with that number — raising it to the power $q$ — forces a relationship between squares-mod-$p$ and squares-mod-$q$.

It converts a counting statement about squares into an identity in a cyclotomic field, where ordinary algebra does the work.
:::

:::example[Sketch, made concrete with $p=5$, $q=3$.]
1. We know $\tau^2 = \left(\frac{-1}{5}\right)5 = 5$ (since $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$), so $\tau^2 = p^* := \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)p = 5$.
2. Compute $\tau^{q} = \tau^{3} = \tau\cdot\tau^2 = 5\tau$. Working modulo $q=3$, $5 \equiv 2 \equiv \left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right)$-type factor; the general identity is $\tau^{q-1} = (p^*)^{(q-1)/2} \equiv \left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right) \pmod q$, and here $(p^*)^{(q-1)/2} = 5^{1} \equiv 2 \equiv -1 \pmod 3$.
3. The other evaluation of $\tau^q$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ (permuting the roots of unity) gives $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)\tau$. Matching the two expressions for $\tau^{q}/\tau$ yields $\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{p^*}{q}\right)$, which unwinds to the reciprocity law $\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q-1}{2}}$. This is the algebraic engine; the full proof fills in the ring-theoretic details.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **The $k=0$ term**: it contributes $0$ because $\left(\frac{0}{p}\right) = 0$; the sum effectively runs over the nonzero residues.
- **The exact constant (real vs. $i$)**: the phase depends on the normalization (sign in the exponent, whether you use $e^{2\pi i k/p}$), so different textbooks may report $\tau$ up to a unit factor. State your convention.
- **Gauss sums for composite moduli**: the clean $|\tau|=\sqrt p$ result is for a *prime* $p$. More general Gauss and Jacobi sums exist for Dirichlet characters, but they behave differently.
- **Confusing magnitude with the full evaluation**: proving $|\tau| = \sqrt p$ (equivalently $\tau^2 = \pm p$) is the easy part; determining the exact sign/phase is the deep step, comparable to reciprocity itself.
