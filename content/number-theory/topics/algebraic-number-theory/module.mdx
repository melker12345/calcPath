# Algebraic Number Theory

Algebraic integers, rings of integers, Dedekind domains, ideal class groups, and Dirichlet's unit theorem.

An algebraic integer is a complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients.

Examples: all ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$, $\sqrt2$, $\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$, and the golden ratio (root of $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$).

The algebraic integers form a ring (sums and products of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers).

In a number field, the algebraic integers that lie inside it form the ring $\mathcal{O}_K$.

They generalize the notion of "integer" beyond $\mathbb{Z}$, and their arithmetic is richer (and harder).

## Definition and Basic Properties

<!-- section: alg-int-def -->

:::definition[Algebraic integer]{#algebraic-integer}
A complex number $\alpha$ is an **algebraic integer** if there is a *monic* polynomial

$$f(x) = x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0, \qquad a_i \in \mathbb{Z},$$

such that $f(\alpha) = 0$.
:::

"Monic" — leading coefficient $1$ — is the whole point: it is what makes $\alpha$ behave like an integer rather than a fraction.

Equivalently, $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer iff its *minimal polynomial* over $\mathbb{Q}$ (the monic rational polynomial of least degree killing $\alpha$) already has integer coefficients. This equivalence rests on Gauss's lemma: if a monic $\mathbb{Z}$-polynomial has $\alpha$ as a root, the minimal polynomial divides it in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, and the quotient of two monic integer polynomials is again integer.

:::theorem[The algebraic integers form a ring]
If $\alpha, \beta$ are algebraic integers, so are $\alpha + \beta$ and $\alpha\beta$.
:::

:::proof
If $\alpha, \beta$ are algebraic integers, then $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha,\beta]$ is finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module (spanned by the finitely many products $\alpha^i \beta^j$ with $i < \deg\alpha$, $j < \deg\beta$). Any element $\gamma$ that maps this finitely generated module into itself is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, by Cayley–Hamilton applied to the "multiply by $\gamma$" matrix. Taking $\gamma = \alpha+\beta$ and $\gamma = \alpha\beta$ shows both are algebraic integers.
:::

:::intuition
An algebraic integer is like an ordinary integer that got permission to satisfy a *whole-number* equation of higher degree, like $x^2 = 2$ or $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ — as long as the leading coefficient is $1$ (no cheating with fractions out front).

They are closed under $+$ and $\times$: you can add and multiply them all day and never fall out of the "integer" world, just like $2, 3, 5$ stay integers.
:::

:::example[Show the golden ratio $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ is an algebraic integer.]
Step 1: Find a polynomial. Set $\varphi = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$, so $2\varphi - 1 = \sqrt5$. Squaring: $(2\varphi-1)^2 = 5$, i.e. $4\varphi^2 - 4\varphi + 1 = 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. Simplify to monic form. That gives $4\varphi^2 - 4\varphi - 4 = 0$, and dividing by $4$: $\varphi^2 - \varphi - 1 = 0$.
2. Check the criterion. The polynomial $x^2 - x - 1$ is monic with integer coefficients and has $\varphi$ as a root, so $\varphi$ is an algebraic integer.
3. Sanity check: closure under $+$ predicts $\varphi + 1 = \tfrac{3+\sqrt5}{2}$ is also an algebraic integer. Indeed, if $x = \varphi+1$ then $\varphi = x-1$, and $(x-1)^2 - (x-1) - 1 = x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$ — monic with integer coefficients. ✓
:::

## Algebraic Numbers vs Integers

<!-- section: alg-int-vs-numbers -->

:::definition[Algebraic number]{#algebraic-number}
An **algebraic number** is a root of *any* nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients — the leading coefficient need not be $1$.
:::

So $\tfrac12$ is an algebraic number (root of $2x-1$) even though it is not an algebraic integer.

:::theorem[The algebraic numbers form a field]
Sums, products, and inverses of nonzero algebraic numbers are again algebraic, so they form a field: the algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ of $\mathbb{Q}$ inside $\mathbb{C}$. Among them, the algebraic *integers* are exactly those whose minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ is monic with integer coefficients.
:::

:::proposition[Algebraic numbers are fractions of algebraic integers]
The field of algebraic numbers is the *field of fractions* of the ring of algebraic integers: every algebraic number can be written as an algebraic integer divided by an *ordinary* integer.
:::

:::proof
Given a root of $b_n x^n + \cdots + b_0$ with $b_i \in \mathbb{Z}$, multiply the root by $b_n$; the product satisfies a monic integer polynomial, so it is an algebraic integer.
:::

:::intuition
Algebraic numbers are the "fractions" of algebraic integers, exactly the way $\tfrac34$ is a fraction of the ordinary integers $3$ and $4$: every algebraic number is an algebraic integer divided by a plain whole number.

The slogan: $\mathbb{Z}$ is to $\mathbb{Q}$ as the algebraic integers are to the algebraic numbers.
:::

:::example[Show $\tfrac12$ is an algebraic number but not an algebraic integer.]
Step 1: It is an algebraic number. $\tfrac12$ is a root of $2x - 1 = 0$, a nonzero polynomial with rational (indeed integer) coefficients.
:::

:::solution
1. Its minimal polynomial. The minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x - \tfrac12$ (monic, degree $1$). Its constant term $-\tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, so the integrality criterion already fails.
2. Rule out any other monic witness. Suppose some monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ had $f(\tfrac12) = 0$. By the rational root theorem, a rational root $p/q$ in lowest terms of a monic integer polynomial must have $q \mid 1$, i.e. must be an *integer*. But $\tfrac12$ is not an integer — contradiction. So no monic integer polynomial kills $\tfrac12$.
3. Conclusion: $\tfrac12$ is an algebraic number but not an algebraic integer. ✓
:::

## Rings of Algebraic Integers in Extensions

<!-- section: alg-int-rings -->

:::definition[Ring of integers of a number field]{#ring-of-integers}
If $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is a finite extension (a *number field*), the algebraic integers lying inside $K$ form the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$, the **integral closure** of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$.
:::

$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $[K:\mathbb{Q}]$, and it is the "right" ring in which to do arithmetic in $K$: the ideal factorization, units, and class group all live in $\mathcal{O}_K$, not in $K$ itself (which, being a field, has no interesting ideals).

A subtle point: adjoining a generator does not always hand you the ring of integers.

:::theorem[Ring of integers of a quadratic field]{#quadratic-ring-of-integers}
For a quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ square-free,

$$\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d] \ \text{ when } d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4, \qquad \mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}\right] \ \text{ when } d \equiv 1 \pmod 4,$$

the second being strictly larger than $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$.
:::

:::intuition
When you adjoin an algebraic number to $\mathbb{Q}$, the "integers" of the new field are *all* the algebraic integers that happen to live in it — not merely the polynomial expressions in the element you adjoined.

Sometimes that forces a bigger $\mathbb{Z}$-basis than the obvious powers $1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \dots$; there can be extra integers hiding at half-steps.
:::

:::example[Find $\mathcal{O}_K$ for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$.]
Step 1: Identify the field and the congruence class. Here $d = -3$ is square-free and $-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. So we expect the "$\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}$" case.
:::

:::solution
1. Test the candidate integer $\omega = \tfrac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$. Then $2\omega - 1 = \sqrt{-3}$, so $(2\omega-1)^2 = -3$, giving $4\omega^2 - 4\omega + 1 = -3$, i.e. $\omega^2 - \omega + 1 = 0$. Monic with integer coefficients, so $\omega$ is an algebraic integer — yet $\omega \notin \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ because of the denominator $2$.
2. Conclusion. $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ with $\mathbb{Z}$-basis $\{1, \omega\}$, strictly containing $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$. (This $\omega$ is a primitive $6$th root of unity, the "Eisenstein integer" generator.)
3. Sanity check: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ misses $\omega$, so it is *not* the full ring of integers even though $\sqrt{-3}$ generates the field. ✓
:::

## Minimal Polynomial and Characteristic Polynomial

<!-- section: alg-int-minpoly-charpoly -->

If $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer of degree $d$, its minimal polynomial $m(x)$ is monic in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

There are two polynomials attached to an element $\alpha \in K$, and it helps to keep them straight.

:::definition[Minimal and characteristic polynomial]
The *minimal polynomial* $m(x)$ is the smallest monic rational polynomial with $\alpha$ as a root (degree $d = [\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$). The *characteristic polynomial* is

$$\chi(x) = \det(xI - m_\alpha),$$

where $m_\alpha$ is the $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map "multiply by $\alpha$" on $K$ viewed as a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space (degree $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$).
:::

They are related by $\chi(x) = m(x)^{[K:\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)]}$: the char poly is the min poly raised to the number of times $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ fits into $K$. When $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ the exponent is $1$ and the two coincide.

:::proposition[Trace and norm of an algebraic integer]
For any $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$, the characteristic polynomial is monic in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. Reading off its coefficients: $\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)$ is (minus) the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$, and

$$N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha) = (-1)^n \chi(0).$$

Both are therefore ordinary integers when $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer.
:::

:::intuition
The minimal polynomial is the "cleanest equation" $\alpha$ obeys — monic, integer coefficients, shortest possible.

The characteristic polynomial comes from treating "multiply by $\alpha$" as a matrix; its determinant is the *norm* and its trace is the *trace* of $\alpha$, and both come out as whole numbers when $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer.
:::

:::example[Compute the characteristic polynomial, trace, and norm of $\alpha = \sqrt2$ in $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.]
Step 1: Pick a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis of $K$. Use $\{1, \sqrt2\}$.
:::

:::solution
Write the matrix of "multiply by $\sqrt2$". We have $\sqrt2 \cdot 1 = \sqrt2$ and $\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2 = 2 = 2\cdot 1$. In the basis $\{1,\sqrt2\}$ the columns are $(0,1)$ and $(2,0)$, so
$$m_\alpha = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Characteristic polynomial. $\chi(x) = \det(xI - m_\alpha) = \det\begin{pmatrix} x & -2 \\ -1 & x \end{pmatrix} = x^2 - 2$. Since $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, this equals the minimal polynomial $x^2 - 2$, as expected.

Read off invariants. $\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt2) = -(\text{coeff of }x) = 0$, and $N(\sqrt2) = (-1)^2 \chi(0) = -2$.

Sanity check: using the formula $N(a + b\sqrt2) = a^2 - 2b^2$, we get $N(\sqrt2) = 0^2 - 2\cdot 1^2 = -2$, and the two conjugates $\pm\sqrt2$ sum to $\mathrm{Tr} = 0$. ✓ Both are integers, consistent with $\sqrt2 \in \mathcal{O}_K$. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Every algebraic number is an algebraic integer**: No; 1/2 is alg number not integer.
- **The ring of all alg integers is a UFD or PID**: No. The ring $\overline{\mathbb{Z}}$ of *all* algebraic integers is not Noetherian and has no irreducible elements at all (every non-unit factors further, e.g. $\sqrt2 = \sqrt[4]{2}\cdot\sqrt[4]{2}$), so ordinary factorization is meaningless there; it is a Bézout domain (finitely generated ideals are principal) but not a PID.
- **Z[sqrt(-3)] is the full ring of integers of Q(sqrt(-3))**: No, for d=-3=1 mod4, O_K = Z[ω] the Eisenstein, larger, includes ( -1 + sqrt(-3))/2.
- **Min poly always has integer coeffs for alg numbers**: For alg numbers min poly has rational coeffs; for alg int it can be taken monic Z.
- **α alg int implies 1/α alg int**: No (unless unit).

The ring of integers O_K of a number field K is the integral closure of Z in K: the algebraic integers lying in K.

It is a Dedekind domain of rank n=[K:Q] as Z-module, with Z-basis an integral basis.

Computing an integral basis for a given K=Q(α) given by a min poly is a standard (but nontrivial) task in computational algebraic number theory; there are algorithms using discriminant, p-adic completions, or round 2/4 methods.

O_K is the natural place to do "integer arithmetic" in K: factorization of ideals, units, class group all live here.

## Definition and Module Structure

<!-- section: ring-int-def-module -->

$\mathcal{O}_K = \{\, \beta \in K \mid \beta \text{ is an algebraic integer} \,\}$, the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$. It is a subring of $K$.

:::theorem[Existence of an integral basis]
There exists a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_n$ of $\mathcal{O}_K$ (an **integral basis**), so that $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}\omega_1 + \cdots + \mathbb{Z}\omega_n$ as an abelian group, and every element of $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a *unique* $\mathbb{Z}$-combination of the $\omega_i$.
:::

:::definition[Discriminant of a basis]{#basis-discriminant}
$$\operatorname{disc}(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n) = \det\!\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_i \omega_j)\big),$$

the determinant of the $n \times n$ matrix of pairwise traces.
:::

For an integral basis this is the *field discriminant* $\operatorname{disc}(K)$; it does not depend on which integral basis you chose (a change of integral basis is by a matrix in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$, whose determinant $\pm 1$ squares to $1$), and it measures both the covolume of $\mathcal{O}_K$ under the Minkowski embedding and which primes ramify.

:::intuition
$\mathcal{O}_K$ is the "full set of integers" living inside $K$.

Just as $\mathbb{Z}$ is built from the single generator $1$, $\mathcal{O}_K$ has $n$ independent generators over $\mathbb{Z}$ that together reach every integer in $K$.
:::

:::example[Find an integral basis and the discriminant for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$.]
Step 1: Which case? Here $d = -5$ is square-free and $-5 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ with integral basis $\{\omega_1, \omega_2\} = \{1, \sqrt{-5}\}$.
:::

:::solution
Trace values. In $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, $\mathrm{Tr}(a+b\sqrt{-5}) = 2a$. So $\mathrm{Tr}(1) = 2$, $\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{-5}) = 0$, and $\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{-5}\cdot\sqrt{-5}) = \mathrm{Tr}(-5) = -10$.

Trace matrix and determinant.
$$\big(\mathrm{Tr}(\omega_i\omega_j)\big) = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Tr}(1) & \mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{-5}) \\ \mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{-5}) & \mathrm{Tr}(-5) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -10 \end{pmatrix},$$
with determinant $2\cdot(-10) - 0 = -20$.

Conclusion: $\operatorname{disc}(K) = -20$, matching the quadratic rule $\operatorname{disc} = 4d = 4(-5) = -20$ for $d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4$. ✓
:::

## Power Basis vs Integral Basis

<!-- section: ring-int-power-vs-integral -->

If $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ with $\alpha$ an algebraic integer of minimal polynomial $f$ (monic, in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$), then the **power order** $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K$, but the inclusion may be *proper*.

Both are free $\mathbb{Z}$-modules of the same rank $n$, so the index $[\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]]$ is finite.

:::theorem[Index and discriminants]{#index-discriminant}
$$\operatorname{disc}(f) = [\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]]^2 \cdot \operatorname{disc}(K),$$

where $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ is the polynomial discriminant.
:::

So $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \mathcal{O}_K$ exactly when $\operatorname{disc}(f) = \operatorname{disc}(K)$. In particular, if $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ is *square-free* the index must be $1$ and the power basis already gives $\mathcal{O}_K$; a square factor is a warning (but not proof) that the index may be $>1$.

:::intuition
$\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is the "obvious" set of integers: all polynomials in $\alpha$ with plain integer coefficients.

Sometimes $K$ hides *extra* integers that $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ misses — like half-integer combinations when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

The true $\mathcal{O}_K$ can be bigger, needing a different basis; the squared index tells you exactly how much bigger by comparing discriminants.
:::

:::example[Show $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5] \subsetneq \mathcal{O}_K$ for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ and find the index.]
Step 1: Discriminant of the power order. For $\alpha = \sqrt5$, minimal polynomial $f = x^2 - 5$, and $\operatorname{disc}(x^2 - 5) = -4\cdot(-5) = 20$. (For $x^2 + c$ the discriminant is $-4c$.)
:::

:::solution
1. Field discriminant. Since $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $\operatorname{disc}(K) = d = 5$.
2. Solve for the index. From $\operatorname{disc}(f) = [\mathcal{O}_K:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]]^2 \operatorname{disc}(K)$: $\;20 = [\mathcal{O}_K:\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]]^2 \cdot 5$, so $[\mathcal{O}_K:\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]]^2 = 4$, giving index $2$.
3. Exhibit the missing integer. The golden ratio $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ is an algebraic integer (root of $x^2 - x - 1$, shown earlier) but is not in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ (it has a denominator $2$). So $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}]$ with basis $\{1, \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\}$, containing $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ with index $2$. ✓
:::

## Discriminant and Ramification

<!-- section: ring-int-disc-ram -->

:::definition[Field discriminant]{#field-discriminant}
The **discriminant** $\operatorname{disc}(K)$ of the field is the discriminant of any integral basis (well-defined, since integral bases differ by a $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$ matrix of determinant $\pm 1$).
:::

It is an integer, and it detects ramification.

:::theorem[Ramified primes divide the discriminant]
A rational prime $p$ **ramifies** in $K$ — meaning some exponent $e_i > 1$ in the factorization $p\mathcal{O}_K = \prod_i P_i^{e_i}$ — *if and only if* $p \mid \operatorname{disc}(K)$.
:::

So the (finitely many) ramified primes are read straight off the discriminant.

:::proposition[Discriminant of a quadratic field]
For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ square-free,

$$\operatorname{disc}(K) = d \ \ (d \equiv 1 \bmod 4), \qquad \operatorname{disc}(K) = 4d \ \ (\text{otherwise}),$$

and the ramified primes are exactly those dividing this.
:::

:::intuition
The discriminant is a single-number "complexity gauge" of $\mathcal{O}_K$ — geometrically the (squared) volume of the fundamental parallelepiped of $\mathcal{O}_K$ under the Minkowski embedding.

Its prime factors are precisely the primes that "ramify", i.e. develop a repeated factor when you factor them into prime ideals in $\mathcal{O}_K$.
:::

:::example[Determine the discriminant and ramified primes of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, and factor the ramified prime.]
Step 1: Discriminant. $d = -3$ is square-free and $-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so $\operatorname{disc}(K) = d = -3$.
:::

:::solution
1. Candidate ramified primes. The primes dividing $|{-3}| = 3$ are just $p = 3$; so $3$ is the only ramified prime.
2. Verify the ramification. Since $(\sqrt{-3})^2 = -3$ and the ideal $(-3) = (3)$, we have $3\mathcal{O}_K = (3) = (\sqrt{-3})^2$. The prime ideal $(\sqrt{-3})$ appears with exponent $e = 2 > 1$, so $3$ genuinely ramifies. ✓
3. Sanity check: $3 \mid \operatorname{disc}(K) = -3$, matching the theorem, and no other prime divides $-3$, so no other prime ramifies. ✓
:::

## Computing Integral Basis (sketch)

<!-- section: ring-int-computing-basis -->

:::recipe[Finding an integral basis]
Given the minimal polynomial $f$ of $\alpha$, the strategy to find $\mathcal{O}_K$ is:

- Compute $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ and factor it. Only primes $p$ whose *square* divides $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ can possibly cause $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \neq \mathcal{O}_K$ (recall $\operatorname{disc}(f) = [\mathcal{O}_K:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]]^2 \operatorname{disc}(K)$, so the index only picks up such primes).
- For each such $p$, test $p$-maximality with the **Dedekind criterion**: factor $f$ modulo $p$ as $\bar f = \prod \bar g_i^{\,e_i}$, lift the $g_i$ to $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and check whether $p$ divides a certain quotient polynomial. If the order is not $p$-maximal, the criterion produces an element with denominator $p$ that is integral but not yet in the order; adjoin it and repeat (this is the idea behind the Round 2 algorithm).
- Glue the local improvements at each bad prime into a single global integral basis. Practical implementations live in Pari/GP, Sage, and Magma.
:::

:::intuition
Start with the "obvious" ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, compute its discriminant, and flag the primes $p$ that appear *squared*.

At each flagged $p$, work modulo powers of $p$ to hunt for extra integers with denominator $p$ that are secretly integral; add them in.

Stitch all the local fixes together to get the full $\mathcal{O}_K$.
:::

:::example[Use the discriminant test to check whether $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ is the full ring of integers of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$, and fix it if not.]
Step 1: Discriminant of $f = x^2 - 5$. $\operatorname{disc}(f) = 20 = 2^2 \cdot 5$.
:::

:::solution
1. Flag suspicious primes. Only primes dividing $\operatorname{disc}(f)$ to a square power matter: here $2^2 \mid 20$, so $p = 2$ is suspect ($5$ appears only to the first power, so it cannot contribute to the index).
2. Test $p=2$ (Dedekind criterion). Modulo $2$, $f \equiv x^2 - 5 \equiv x^2 + 1 \equiv (x+1)^2 \pmod 2$ — a repeated factor, so $2$ is a candidate for non-maximality. Checking the candidate element $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$: it satisfies $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$, a monic integer polynomial, so it is integral but has denominator $2$ and lies outside $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$.
3. Enlarge. Adjoin $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ to get $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}]$.
4. Sanity check: the new order has discriminant $5$, and $\operatorname{disc}(f)/\operatorname{disc}(K) = 20/5 = 4 = 2^2$ recovers the index $2$ found earlier. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **O_K = Z[α] always for primitive element α**: No, counterexamples when p^2 divides the index for some p.
- **Discriminant is always square-free**: No, can have squares (ramified).
- **The basis is unique**: No, any Z-basis, but the disc is invariant.
- **All elements of K are algebraic integers**: No, only those in O_K; K has elements like 1/2, α/2 that may not be integral.
- **Computing O_K is trivial**: For high degree or large coeffs it requires serious computation (factoring disc, local lifts).

A Dedekind domain is an integral domain that is Noetherian, integrally closed, and of Krull dimension 1 (every nonzero prime ideal is maximal).

The key property: in a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals.

This makes them the "correct" generalization of Z or PID where unique factorization holds at the ideal level.

Rings of integers O_K of number fields are the main examples (and motivation).

Fractional ideals form a group, and the class group is the quotient by principals.

## Definition and Equivalent Characterizations

<!-- section: dedekind-def -->

:::definition[Dedekind domain]{#dedekind-domain}
An integral domain $R$ (that is not a field) is **Dedekind** if all three hold:

1. **Noetherian** — every ideal is finitely generated.

2. **Integrally closed** in its fraction field — if an element of the fraction field satisfies a monic polynomial over $R$, it already lies in $R$.

3. **Krull dimension $1$** — every nonzero prime ideal is maximal (no chain $0 \subsetneq P \subsetneq Q$ of primes).
:::

:::theorem[Equivalent characterizations]
Any one of the following is equivalent to the three axioms together: every nonzero ideal is *invertible* as a fractional ideal; every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals; the localization $R_{\mathfrak{m}}$ at every maximal ideal is a discrete valuation ring (DVR).
:::

:::intuition
Noetherian: "no infinitely complicated ideals" — every ideal is pinned down by finitely many generators, like $\mathbb{Z}$.

Integrally closed: "no stray integers hiding just outside" — the ring already contains everything that ought to be integral, like $\mathbb{Z}$.

Dimension $1$: "every nonzero prime is already as big as it can get" — no room for a prime strictly between $0$ and a maximal ideal.

Bundle these three and you are forced into unique factorization *of ideals*.
:::

:::example[Show $\mathbb{Z}$ is Dedekind but $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not.]
Step 1: $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian — it is a PID, so every ideal is generated by one element.
:::

:::solution
1. $\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed — its fraction field is $\mathbb{Q}$, and a rational root of a monic integer polynomial must be an integer (rational root theorem), so it is back in $\mathbb{Z}$.
2. $\mathbb{Z}$ has dimension $1$ — every nonzero prime is $(p)$ for a prime $p$, and $\mathbb{Z}/(p)$ is a field, so $(p)$ is maximal. All three axioms hold, so $\mathbb{Z}$ is Dedekind.
3. $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ fails axiom 3. It has the chain of primes $0 \subsetneq (x) \subsetneq (2, x)$: $(x)$ is prime (quotient $\mathbb{Z}$, a domain) and nonzero, but it is *not* maximal because $(x) \subsetneq (2,x)$ and the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2,x) \cong \mathbb{F}_2$ is a field. So $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ has Krull dimension $2$ and is *not* Dedekind. ✓
:::

## Unique Ideal Factorization

<!-- section: dedekind-uf-ideal -->

:::theorem[Unique factorization of ideals]{#ideal-factorization}
In a Dedekind domain $R$, every nonzero ideal $I$ factors as

$$I = P_1^{e_1} \cdots P_k^{e_k},$$

a product of prime ideals, *uniquely* up to the order of the factors.
:::

:::proof
Sketch: one first shows every nonzero ideal is *invertible* as a fractional ideal (this is where all three axioms are used — Noetherian + dimension $1$ + integrally closed force maximal ideals to be invertible, hence all ideals). Invertibility makes the monoid of ideals cancellative, and a Noetherian induction expresses each ideal as a product of primes; cancellation then gives uniqueness.
:::

:::intuition
Every "ideal number" breaks into "prime ideals" in exactly one way, just as ordinary numbers break into primes in $\mathbb{Z}$.

The magic: this stays true even when the *elements* refuse to factor uniquely — the failure of element-level factorization is repaired at the ideal level.
:::

:::example[Factor the ideal $(6)$ in $\mathcal{O} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and see how it reconciles the two element factorizations of $6$.]
Step 1: Factor $2$ and $3$ into prime ideals. Reducing the minimal polynomial $x^2 + 5$:
mod $2$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv x^2 + 1 \equiv (x+1)^2$, so $2$ *ramifies*: $(2) = P^2$ with $P = (2,\, 1+\sqrt{-5})$.
mod $3$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv x^2 + 2 \equiv (x-1)(x+1)$, so $3$ *splits*: $(3) = Q R$ with $Q = (3,\, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ and $R = (3,\, 1-\sqrt{-5})$.
:::

:::solution
1. Multiply. $(6) = (2)(3) = P^2 Q R$, the unique prime-ideal factorization.
2. Match the element factorizations. The two element factorizations $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ correspond to two different *groupings* of the same prime ideals: $(2) = P^2$, $(3) = QR$, while $(1+\sqrt{-5}) = PQ$ and $(1-\sqrt{-5}) = PR$. Both groupings multiply back to $P^2 Q R = (6)$.
3. Sanity check: $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = (PQ)(PR) = P^2 QR = (6)$, the same ideal. ✓ Unique factorization holds for ideals even though $6$ has two element factorizations.
:::

## Localization at Prime Ideals are DVRs

<!-- section: dedekind-local-dvr -->

:::theorem[Localizations of a Dedekind domain are DVRs]
For a nonzero prime ideal $P$ in a Dedekind domain $R$, the localization $R_P$ (invert everything outside $P$) is a **DVR**: a local PID whose unique maximal ideal $P R_P$ is *principal*, generated by a **uniformizer** $\pi$, and every nonzero ideal is $(\pi^k)$ for some $k \geq 0$.
:::

:::definition[Discrete valuation]{#discrete-valuation}
For a nonzero element $\alpha$, $v_P(\alpha)$ is the unique $k$ with $\alpha = \pi^k u$ ($u$ a unit in $R_P$); for an ideal $I$, $v_P(I)$ is the exponent of $P$ in its prime factorization. The valuation is a homomorphism,

$$v_P(\alpha\beta) = v_P(\alpha) + v_P(\beta), \qquad v_P(\alpha+\beta) \geq \min(v_P(\alpha), v_P(\beta)).$$
:::

:::intuition
Zoom in on one prime $P$ and the ring simplifies to something like the $p$-adic integers or a power-series ring: every nonzero element is $\pi^k \times (\text{unit})$.

The exponent $k$ is the "order of vanishing" at $P$ — how many times that prime divides the element or ideal.
:::

:::example[Describe the localization of $\mathbb{Z}$ at $P = (5)$ and compute a valuation.]
Step 1: The ring. $\mathbb{Z}_{(5)} = \{\, a/b \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Z},\ 5 \nmid b \,\}$ — all fractions whose denominator is coprime to $5$. Its only maximal ideal is $(5)$.
:::

:::solution
1. It is a DVR. The maximal ideal $(5)$ is principal with uniformizer $\pi = 5$, and every nonzero ideal is $(5^k)$. The valuation $v_5$ is the usual $5$-adic valuation.
2. Compute. Take $\alpha = \tfrac{50}{3}$. Then $50 = 2\cdot 5^2$ and $\gcd(3,5) = 1$, so $\alpha = 5^2 \cdot \tfrac{2}{3}$ with $\tfrac{2}{3}$ a unit in $\mathbb{Z}_{(5)}$. Hence $v_5(\alpha) = 2$.
3. Sanity check: $v_5(50) = 2$, $v_5(3) = 0$, and $v_5(50/3) = 2 - 0 = 2$, consistent with additivity. ✓
:::

## Fractional Ideals and the Ideal Group

<!-- section: dedekind-fractional -->

:::definition[Fractional ideal]{#fractional-ideal}
A **fractional ideal** of $R$ (with fraction field $K$) is a nonzero $R$-submodule $I \subseteq K$ for which there is a nonzero $d \in R$ (a "common denominator") with $dI \subseteq R$.
:::

Clearing the denominator turns $I$ into an ordinary (integral) ideal.

:::theorem[The fractional ideals form a group]
In a Dedekind domain the set of fractional ideals forms a **group** under multiplication, with identity $R$ and inverse

$$I^{-1} = \{\, x \in K \mid xI \subseteq R \,\}.$$
:::

The **principal** fractional ideals $(\alpha) = \alpha R$ for $\alpha \in K^*$ form a subgroup; the quotient is the ideal class group. Every fractional ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals with *integer* (possibly negative) exponents.

:::intuition
A fractional ideal is an "ideal that is allowed to have a denominator" — for example $\tfrac12\mathbb{Z} = (\tfrac12)$ inside $\mathbb{Q}$.

They form a group under multiplication, and the class group is what you get after throwing away the "boring" principal ones.
:::

:::example[Verify the group law on a fractional ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$.]
Step 1: A fractional ideal. Take $I = \tfrac32\mathbb{Z} = (\tfrac32) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$. With $d = 2$, $dI = 3\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, confirming it is a fractional ideal.
:::

:::solution
1. Compute the inverse. $I^{-1} = \{\, x \in \mathbb{Q} \mid x\cdot\tfrac32\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z} \,\}$. We need $\tfrac32 x \in \mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $x \in \tfrac23\mathbb{Z}$. So $I^{-1} = (\tfrac23)$.
2. Check $I \cdot I^{-1} = R$. $(\tfrac32)(\tfrac23) = (\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac23) = (1) = \mathbb{Z}$. ✓
3. Prime factorization. $\tfrac32 = 2^{-1}\cdot 3$, so $I = (2)^{-1}(3)$ — negative exponent on the prime $2$. In $\mathbb{Z}$ every fractional ideal is principal, so the class group is trivial (class number $1$).
:::

## O_K are Dedekind

<!-- section: dedekind-ok-dedekind -->

:::theorem[Rings of integers are Dedekind]
For a number field $K$, the ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a Dedekind domain, and so enjoys unique factorization of ideals.
:::

:::proof
$\mathcal{O}_K$ satisfies all three axioms:

- **Noetherian**: $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module (free of rank $n$), so its ideals are $\mathbb{Z}$-submodules of a finitely generated module, hence finitely generated.
- **Integrally closed**: $\mathcal{O}_K$ *is* the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$ by definition, and an integral closure is always integrally closed in the field.
- **Dimension $1$**: if $Q$ is a nonzero prime ideal of $\mathcal{O}_K$, then $Q \cap \mathbb{Z} = (p)$ is a nonzero prime of $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathcal{O}_K/Q$ is an integral domain that is finite-dimensional over the field $\mathbb{Z}/p$. A domain finite over a field is itself a field, so $Q$ is maximal.
:::

:::intuition
Rings of integers of number fields tick all three Dedekind boxes, so they automatically get unique factorization of ideals.

That single fact is the launchpad for all of algebraic number theory.
:::

:::example[Illustrate the "$\sum e_i f_i = n$" decomposition of a prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (so $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[i]$, $n = 2$).]
Step 1: Split case. Take $p = 5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Then $5 = (2+i)(2-i)$, and $(5) = (2+i)(2-i)$ with two *distinct* primes, each of residue degree $f = 1$ (since $N(2+i) = 5$, a prime, so $\mathcal{O}_K/(2+i) \cong \mathbb{F}_5$). Here $e_1 = e_2 = 1$, $f_1 = f_2 = 1$, and $\sum e_i f_i = 1\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 1 = 2 = n$. ✓
:::

:::solution
1. Ramified case. Take $p = 2$. Then $2 = -i(1+i)^2$, so $(2) = (1+i)^2$ with $e = 2$, $f = 1$; $\sum e_i f_i = 2\cdot 1 = 2 = n$. ✓
2. Inert case. Take $p = 3 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Then $(3)$ stays prime, with $e = 1$ and residue degree $f = 2$ (since $\mathcal{O}_K/(3) \cong \mathbb{F}_9$); $\sum e_i f_i = 1\cdot 2 = 2 = n$. ✓
3. Sanity check: all three cases give $\sum e_i f_i = 2 = [\,\mathbb{Q}(i):\mathbb{Q}\,]$, as the theorem demands.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Dedekind domains are always PID or UFD**: No; they have unique ideal fact, but elements may not (class number >1).
- **Z[x] is Dedekind**: No, not dim 1.
- **Every Noetherian domain is Dedekind**: No, needs the other two properties.
- **Fractional ideals are ideals**: No, they live in the frac field, may have "denom".
- **In Dedekind every ideal is principal**: Only if class number 1.

The ideal class group Cl_K of a number field K is the group of fractional ideals of O_K modulo the subgroup of principal fractional ideals.

It is finite (class number h_K <∞), and its order h_K measures the failure of O_K to be a PID.

The class group is a fundamental invariant of K; computing it (or even deciding if h=1) is a central problem in computational number theory, with connections to the regulator, L-functions (class number formula), and cryptography (class group based crypto in some old systems, or ideal lattices).

Dirichlet's class number formula relates h R (regulator) to the residue of zeta_K at s=1, involving sqrt|disc| and L(1,χ) or the product.

## Definition

<!-- section: class-group-def -->

The group of fractional ideals $I_K$ is the free abelian group generated by the prime ideals of $\mathcal{O}_K$ (integer exponents, negatives allowed).

:::definition[Ideal class group]{#class-group}
The **principal** fractional ideals $P_K = \{\, (\alpha) = \alpha\mathcal{O}_K \mid \alpha \in K^* \,\}$ form a subgroup, and the **ideal class group** is the quotient

$$\mathrm{Cl}_K = I_K / P_K.$$
:::

Two ideals are equivalent, $I \sim J$, iff $I = \alpha J$ for some $\alpha \in K^*$ (equivalently $I J^{-1}$ is principal). The class $[I]$ of an integral ideal is its coset in $\mathrm{Cl}_K$; the identity element is the class of principal ideals.

:::intuition
Think of ideals as "generalized numbers". Two count as the same *type* if one is just the other rescaled by a single field element.

The classes are the distinct types of ideals, and you compose them by multiplying ideals — the class group records how many types exist.
:::

:::example[Show the class $[P]$ of $P = (2,\, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ has order $2$ in $\mathrm{Cl}_K$ for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$.]
Step 1: $P$ is not principal. If $P = (\alpha)$ were principal with $\alpha = a + b\sqrt{-5}$, then $N(P) = 2$ would equal $|N(\alpha)| = a^2 + 5b^2$. But $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solutions ($b = 0$ gives $a^2 = 2$, impossible; $|b| \geq 1$ gives $\geq 5$). So $P$ is non-principal and $[P] \neq 1$.
:::

:::solution
1. Compute $P^2$. $P^2 = (2,\,1+\sqrt{-5})^2$ is generated by $4$, $2(1+\sqrt{-5})$, and $(1+\sqrt{-5})^2 = 1 + 2\sqrt{-5} - 5 = -4 + 2\sqrt{-5}$. All three are multiples of $2$, and $2 = 4 - (-4+2\sqrt{-5}) + \ldots$ — concretely $2 = (1+\sqrt{-5})^2 - 4 + \ldots$; more cleanly, $\gcd$ of the norms shows $P^2 = (2)$.
2. Conclude the order. $[P]^2 = [P^2] = [(2)] = 1$, the principal class, while $[P] \neq 1$. So $[P]$ has order exactly $2$. ✓ (In fact $\mathrm{Cl}_K = \{1, [P]\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, so $h_K = 2$.)
:::

## Finiteness (Minkowski)

<!-- section: class-group-finiteness -->

:::theorem[Minkowski bound]{#minkowski-bound}
Every ideal class contains an integral ideal $I$ whose norm is bounded by

$$M_K = \left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^{r_2} \frac{n!}{n^n} \sqrt{|\operatorname{disc}(K)|},$$

where $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$ and $r_2$ is the number of pairs of complex embeddings.
:::

:::corollary[Finiteness of the class group]
$\mathrm{Cl}_K$ is **finite**, and $h_K = |\mathrm{Cl}_K|$ is the class number.
:::

:::proof
There are only finitely many integral ideals of norm below any bound: an ideal of norm $m$ must divide $(m)$, and there are finitely many such divisors. So only finitely many classes occur.
:::

:::intuition
Every class has a "small" representative ideal — bounded norm, like the shortest vector in a lattice coset.

There are only finitely many small ideals to go around, so there can only be finitely many classes.
:::

:::example[Compute the Minkowski bound for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$ and use it to find $h_K$.]
Step 1: Plug in the invariants. $n = 2$, $r_2 = 1$ (one complex pair), $\operatorname{disc}(K) = -20$. Then
$$M_K = \frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{2!}{2^2}\cdot\sqrt{20} = \frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac12\cdot\sqrt{20} = \frac{2\sqrt{20}}{\pi} \approx \frac{2(4.472)}{3.1416} \approx 2.85.$$
:::

:::solution
1. List ideals of norm $\leq 2$. Only norms $1$ and $2$ occur. Norm $1$: the unit ideal $(1)$ (principal). Norm $2$: from $(2) = P^2$ we get the single prime $P = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ of norm $2$.
2. Determine the classes. Every class is represented by a product of primes of norm $\leq M_K \approx 2.85$, i.e. by powers of $P$. Since $[P]$ is non-principal but $[P]^2 = 1$ (shown above), the classes are $\{1, [P]\}$.
3. Conclusion: $\mathrm{Cl}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ and $h_K = 2$. ✓
:::

## The Class Number Formula

<!-- section: class-group-formula -->

:::definition[Dedekind zeta function]{#dedekind-zeta}
$$\zeta_K(s) = \sum_{I} N(I)^{-s} = \prod_{P} (1 - N(P)^{-s})^{-1},$$

the sum over nonzero integral ideals and the product over prime ideals, converging for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$.
:::

:::theorem[Class number formula]{#class-number-formula}
$\zeta_K$ extends meromorphically and has a *simple pole* at $s = 1$ with residue

$$\kappa = \frac{2^{r_1} (2\pi)^{r_2}\, h_K\, R_K}{w \sqrt{|\operatorname{disc}(K)|}},$$

where $R_K$ is the regulator and $w$ the number of roots of unity in $K$.
:::

This ties the arithmetic invariants $(h, R, w, \operatorname{disc})$ to the analytic behavior of $\zeta_K$.

:::intuition
How fast $\zeta_K$ blows up at $s = 1$ measures the "density" of ideals; the formula says that density is the class number times the regulator (the size of the units), divided by the discriminant "volume".

Sanity anchor: for $K = \mathbb{Q}$ it must reproduce $\zeta(s)$'s residue $1$.
:::

:::example[Check the formula reduces correctly for $K = \mathbb{Q}$.]
Step 1: Invariants of $\mathbb{Q}$. One real embedding so $r_1 = 1$, no complex pairs so $r_2 = 0$; class number $h = 1$; the unit group is $\{\pm1\}$ with no free part, so the regulator is the empty determinant $R = 1$; roots of unity $\{\pm1\}$ give $w = 2$; and $\operatorname{disc}(\mathbb{Q}) = 1$.
:::

:::solution
Substitute.
$$\kappa = \frac{2^{1}(2\pi)^{0}\cdot 1\cdot 1}{2\cdot\sqrt{1}} = \frac{2}{2} = 1.$$

Compare. The Riemann zeta function $\zeta(s)$ indeed has residue $1$ at $s = 1$. ✓ The general formula is calibrated so this base case comes out right.

Remark: for an imaginary quadratic field ($r_1 = 0$, $r_2 = 1$, $R = 1$), the residue also equals $\tfrac{2\pi}{w\sqrt{|d|}}\,h$, and separately equals $L(1,\chi)$ for the associated quadratic character $\chi$, yielding the classical explicit formula $h = \tfrac{w\sqrt{|d|}}{2\pi}L(1,\chi)$.
:::

## Computation and Structure

<!-- section: class-group-computation -->

:::recipe[Computing the class group]
1. Find all prime ideals of norm $\leq M_K$ (factor the small rational primes in $\mathcal{O}_K$) — these *generate* the class group.
2. Find relations among their classes by searching for elements whose principal ideal factors into those primes (short vectors in the ideal lattices, e.g. via LLL).
3. Assemble the exponents into a relation matrix; its Smith normal form gives the group structure (cyclic or a product of cyclics for small fields).
:::

Under GRH there are subexponential algorithms; for small discriminant everything can be tabulated directly.

:::intuition
List the "small prime ideals" (the generators), then look for "equations" saying some product of them is principal (found by exhibiting an element with that factorization); collect the exponents into a matrix, and the cokernel of that matrix is the class group.

For small discriminant this can be done by hand or looked up in tables.
:::

:::example[Compute $\mathrm{Cl}_K$ for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$ via the generator-and-relations recipe.]
Step 1: Generators. From the finiteness computation, the only prime of norm $\leq M_K \approx 2.85$ is $P = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$, so $[P]$ generates $\mathrm{Cl}_K$.
:::

:::solution
1. Relations. Search for a relation on $P$: we found $P^2 = (2)$, principal, giving the relation $2[P] = 0$. There is no relation $[P] = 0$ (since $P$ is non-principal). So the single generator satisfies exactly the relation $2[P] = 0$.
2. Structure. The relation matrix is $[2]$; its Smith normal form is $[2]$, so $\mathrm{Cl}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $h_K = 2$. ✓
3. Contrast: for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-2})$ the Minkowski bound forces every class to be principal, so $h = 1$. In fact the imaginary quadratic fields with $h = 1$ are exactly the nine "Heegner" values $d = -1,-2,-3,-7,-11,-19,-43,-67,-163$ (Heegner–Stark–Baker).
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Class group trivial means UFD**: Yes (in Dedekind = PID = UFD).
- **h=1 for all K**: No, most fields have h>1; only finitely many imag quad with h=1.
- **The class group is the unit group**: No, units are in the formula with regulator, class group is about ideals.
- **Computing class group is easy**: For large disc or degree it is hard (subexp but practical only up to certain size).
- **Class number formula is effective**: The residue involves L(1,χ) which may be hard, but gives lower bounds etc.

Algebraic number theory studies algebraic integers (roots of monic polynomials with integer coeffs) and the rings they generate in finite extensions of Q (number fields).

The fundamental phenomenon is that unique factorization into primes often fails in these rings (e.g. in Z[sqrt(-5)], 6=2*3=(1+sqrt(-5))(1-sqrt(-5)), and the factors are irreducible but not prime).

To restore unique factorization one passes to ideals: every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals (Dedekind domains).

The ideal class group measures the failure of principal ideals, and its finiteness (class number finite) is a basic theorem.

Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the unit group.

This is the foundation for modern algebraic number theory, class field theory, arithmetic geometry, and applications to cryptography (elliptic curves over number fields, etc).

## Number Fields and Rings of Integers

<!-- section: alg-nt-fields-integers -->

:::definition[Number field]{#number-field}
A **number field** $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ is a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, generated by an algebraic number $\alpha$; its degree is $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$. (We may always take $\alpha$ to be an algebraic *integer*, clearing denominators.)
:::

The **ring of integers** $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$: all elements of $K$ that are roots of monic polynomials with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients. It is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $n$.

For $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ with $d$ square-free: $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ if $d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4$, and $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}]$ if $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

:::intuition
Instead of stopping at the ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$, we throw in roots like $\sqrt2$ or $\sqrt[3]{2}$ and collect every combination that still satisfies a monic integer equation.

$\mathcal{O}_K$ is exactly the set of "integers" in this enlarged number world.
:::

:::example[Give $\mathcal{O}_K$ and its integral basis for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$.]
Step 1: Congruence class. $d = 5$ is square-free and $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, so we are in the "$\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}$" case.
:::

:::solution
1. The ring. $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}]$, the ring of "golden integers", with integral basis $\{1,\ \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\}$.
2. Confirm integrality. $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ is a root of the monic integer polynomial $x^2 - x - 1$, so it genuinely belongs in $\mathcal{O}_K$.
3. Sanity check: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5] \subsetneq \mathcal{O}_K$ with index $2$ (from $\operatorname{disc}(x^2-5) = 20 = 2^2\cdot\operatorname{disc}(K)$, $\operatorname{disc}(K) = 5$). ✓
:::

## Failure of Unique Factorization

<!-- section: alg-nt-failure-ufd -->

:::proposition[Failure of unique factorization]
In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element $6$ factors two genuinely different ways:

$$6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}).$$
:::

The tool for proving irreducibility is the **norm**

$$N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2,$$

which is multiplicative: $N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$, and $\alpha$ is a unit iff $N(\alpha) = 1$. Each of $2, 3, 1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ turns out to be irreducible, yet they are not associates, so factorization into irreducibles is *not* unique — $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD (hence not a PID).

:::intuition
In these "exotic integers", $6$ can be built from two different sets of atoms; the prime building blocks are no longer unique.

The norm is the referee: it is multiplicative, so if a would-be factorization forces a factor of impossible norm, no such factorization exists.
:::

:::example[Prove $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, so the two factorizations of $6$ are truly distinct.]
Step 1: List achievable norms. $N(a+b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$. For $b = 0$ this is a perfect square; for $|b| \geq 1$ it is $\geq 5$. In particular *no element has norm $2$ or $3$* (both would need $a^2 + 5b^2 \in \{2,3\}$, impossible).
:::

:::solution
1. $2$ is irreducible. $N(2) = 4$. If $2 = \alpha\beta$ with neither a unit, then $N(\alpha)N(\beta) = 4$ with each factor $> 1$, forcing $N(\alpha) = N(\beta) = 2$ — impossible by Step 1. So $2$ is irreducible.
2. $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible. $N(1+\sqrt{-5}) = 1 + 5 = 6$. A nontrivial factorization would split $6 = N(\alpha)N(\beta)$ as $2\cdot 3$ (the only way with both factors $> 1$), again requiring norms $2$ and $3$ — impossible. So $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible.
3. They are not associates. The units are exactly $\pm1$ (the only elements of norm $1$). Since $\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-5}}{2} \notin \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ do not differ by a unit. Hence $2\cdot 3$ and $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are genuinely different irreducible factorizations of $6$. ✓
:::

## Dedekind Domains and Ideal Factorization

<!-- section: alg-nt-dedekind -->

$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a **Dedekind domain**: Noetherian, integrally closed, and of dimension $1$ (every nonzero prime ideal is maximal).

In a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal factors *uniquely* as a product of prime ideals — which need not be principal.

:::definition[Ramification index and residue degree]{#ramification}
For a rational prime $p$, write $p\mathcal{O}_K = \prod_i P_i^{e_i}$; the $e_i$ are the **ramification indices** and $f_i = [\mathcal{O}_K/P_i : \mathbb{F}_p]$ the **residue degrees**.
:::

:::theorem[The fundamental identity]
$$\sum_i e_i f_i = n = [K:\mathbb{Q}].$$
:::

:::intuition
Elements may refuse to factor uniquely, but the "ideal numbers" do: every ideal splits into prime ideals in exactly one way.

That restores the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — just one level up, in the world of ideals.
:::

:::example[Factor $(6)$ into prime ideals in $\mathcal{O} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and reconcile the element factorizations.]
Step 1: $(2)$. Modulo $2$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv (x+1)^2$, so $2$ ramifies: $(2) = P^2$ with $P = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$, $N(P) = 2$, $\mathcal{O}/P \cong \mathbb{F}_2$. Check: $e = 2$, $f = 1$, $ef = 2 = n$. ✓
:::

:::solution
1. $(3)$. Modulo $3$: $x^2 + 5 \equiv x^2 - 1 \equiv (x-1)(x+1)$, so $3$ splits: $(3) = QR$ with $Q = (3, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ and $R = (3, 1-\sqrt{-5})$, each of norm $3$. Check: $e_1 = e_2 = 1$, $f_1 = f_2 = 1$, $\sum ef = 2 = n$. ✓
2. Combine. $(6) = (2)(3) = P^2 QR$.
3. Reconcile. The element factorizations regroup the same four prime factors: $(2) = P^2$, $(3) = QR$, while $(1+\sqrt{-5}) = PQ$ and $(1-\sqrt{-5}) = PR$. Both give $P^2 QR = (6)$. ✓
:::

## Ideal Class Group and Class Number

<!-- section: alg-nt-class-group -->

:::definition[Equivalent ideals]
Two ideals $I, J$ are **equivalent** if $I = \alpha J$ for some $\alpha \in K^*$ (i.e. $IJ^{-1}$ is a principal fractional ideal). The **ideal class group** $\mathrm{Cl}_K$ is the group of fractional ideals modulo principal fractional ideals, and $h_K = |\mathrm{Cl}_K|$ is the class number.
:::

:::theorem[Finiteness and the meaning of $h_K = 1$]
$\mathrm{Cl}_K$ is finite by Minkowski's bound (every class has an integral representative of norm $\leq$ a constant depending only on $\operatorname{disc}(K)$ and $n$). Moreover $h_K = 1$ if and only if $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a PID — and for Dedekind domains, PID $\iff$ UFD.
:::

:::intuition
The class group counts how many "different kinds" of ideals exist, once you regard two as the same when one is a rescaling of the other.

Finite means only finitely many "shapes"; $h = 1$ means every ideal is principal, just as in $\mathbb{Z}$ where every ideal is $(n)$.
:::

:::example[Confirm $h_K = 2$ for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$.]
Step 1: A non-principal ideal. $P = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ has norm $2$, but no element has norm $2$ (since $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ is unsolvable), so $P$ is non-principal: $[P] \neq 1$.
:::

:::solution
1. Its square is principal. $P^2 = (2)$, so $[P]^2 = 1$; thus $[P]$ has order $2$.
2. No other classes. The Minkowski bound $\approx 2.85$ means every class is a power of $P$ (the only prime of norm $\leq 2$). So $\mathrm{Cl}_K = \{1, [P]\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$.
3. Conclusion: $h_K = 2$, so $\mathcal{O}_K$ is not a PID (matching that $6$ fails unique factorization). ✓
:::

## Dirichlet Unit Theorem

<!-- section: alg-nt-units -->

:::theorem[Dirichlet's unit theorem]{#unit-theorem}
The unit group $\mathcal{O}_K^*$ is finitely generated:

$$\mathcal{O}_K^* \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^r, \qquad r = r_1 + r_2 - 1,$$

where $\mu_K$ is the (finite cyclic) group of roots of unity in $K$, $r_1$ the number of real embeddings and $r_2$ the number of complex-conjugate pairs.
:::

The **regulator** $R_K$ is the covolume of the logarithmic embedding of the free part $\mathbb{Z}^r$. For a real quadratic field, $r = 1$: the units are $\pm\varepsilon^k$ for a **fundamental unit** $\varepsilon > 1$, found by solving a Pell-type equation $x^2 - dy^2 = \pm1$.

:::intuition
Beyond the obvious $\pm1$ (or more roots of unity), there are "infinite-order" units you can keep raising to powers, arising from solutions of the norm $= \pm1$ equation.

The count of *independent* infinite-order units is $r_1 + r_2 - 1$ — one fewer than the number of embedding "directions", because the product-of-embeddings $= \pm1$ constraint eats one degree of freedom.
:::

:::example[Find the fundamental unit of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ and identify the unit group.]
Step 1: Rank. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ is real quadratic: $r_1 = 2$, $r_2 = 0$, so $r = 2 + 0 - 1 = 1$. Roots of unity are just $\pm1$, so $\mu_K = \{\pm1\}$.
:::

:::solution
1. Find a unit. An element $a + b\sqrt2$ is a unit iff $N(a+b\sqrt2) = a^2 - 2b^2 = \pm1$. The smallest solution with $a+b\sqrt2 > 1$ is $(a,b) = (1,1)$: $N(1+\sqrt2) = 1 - 2 = -1 = \pm1$. ✓ So $\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$ is a unit.
2. Structure. $\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2 \approx 2.414$ is the fundamental unit, and $\mathcal{O}_K^* = \{\pm(1+\sqrt2)^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\} \cong \{\pm1\}\times\mathbb{Z}$.
3. Sanity check: $(1+\sqrt2)^{-1} = \sqrt2 - 1$ (since $(1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1) = \sqrt2 - 1 + 2 - \sqrt2 = 1$), which is indeed in $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, confirming it is a unit. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **O_K always Z[α] for the defining α**: Not always; the power basis may not be integral basis (e.g. Q(sqrt(5)) needs (1+sqrt5)/2).
- **Unique factorization of elements always fails in extensions**: Sometimes holds (e.g. Z[i] UFD for Gaussian).
- **Class number 1 means UFD**: Yes for Dedekind domains, PID iff UFD for these.
- **Units are only ±1**: In most fields there are more; only for Q and imaginary quadratic with small disc.
- **Ideal factorization is into principal ideals**: No, the prime ideals in the factorization may not be principal; that's measured by class group.

Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the structure of the unit group of the ring of integers O_K of a number field K: it is finite torsion (the roots of unity in K) times a free abelian group of rank r = r1 + r2 - 1, where r1 is the number of real embeddings K→R, r2 the number of pairs of complex conjugate embeddings.

The free generators are called fundamental units.

The proof uses the logarithmic embedding of the units into a hyperplane in R^{r1+r2} (logs of the absolute values under the embeddings), and shows that the image is a lattice of full rank in the trace-zero hyperplane (from product of norms = ±1).

The covolume of the lattice is the regulator R_K, which appears in the class number formula.

This is one of the first finiteness + structure theorems in algebraic number theory (Dirichlet 1846).

## Embeddings and Logarithmic Map

<!-- section: unit-embeddings-log -->

Let $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{r_1}$ be the real embeddings $K \to \mathbb{R}$ and $\tau_1,\bar\tau_1,\dots,\tau_{r_2},\bar\tau_{r_2}$ the complex embeddings in conjugate pairs.

For a unit $\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_K^*$, $N(\varepsilon) = \pm1$, and $|N(\varepsilon)| = \prod_i |\sigma_i(\varepsilon)| \cdot \prod_j |\tau_j(\varepsilon)|^2 = 1$.

:::definition[Logarithmic embedding]{#log-embedding}
The **logarithmic embedding** $l : K^* \to \mathbb{R}^{r_1 + r_2}$ is given by $l(\alpha)_i = \log|\sigma_i(\alpha)|$ on the real slots and $l(\alpha)_j = \log|\tau_j(\alpha)|^2 = 2\log|\tau_j(\alpha)|$ on the complex slots (the factor $2$ makes the coordinate sum equal $\log|N(\alpha)|$).
:::

:::proposition[Units lie in the trace-zero hyperplane]
For a unit, $\sum_k l(\varepsilon)_k = \log|N(\varepsilon)| = 0$, so the image lies in the **hyperplane**

$$\Big\{x : \sum_k x_k = 0\Big\}$$

of dimension $r_1 + r_2 - 1$.
:::

:::intuition
Each embedding measures a "size" $|\sigma(\varepsilon)|$ of the unit; taking logs converts multiplying sizes into adding coordinates.

For units all these sizes multiply to $1$ (that is what $N = \pm1$ says), so the logs add to $0$ — the image is trapped in one flat hyperplane.
:::

:::example[Compute $l(\varepsilon)$ for $\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$ in $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ and verify the coordinates sum to $0$.]
Step 1: The embeddings. Real quadratic, so $r_1 = 2$, $r_2 = 0$. The two real embeddings send $\sqrt2 \mapsto +\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt2 \mapsto -\sqrt2$: $\sigma_1(\varepsilon) = 1+\sqrt2$, $\sigma_2(\varepsilon) = 1-\sqrt2$.
:::

:::solution
Log coordinates. $|\sigma_1(\varepsilon)| = 1+\sqrt2 \approx 2.414$, and $|\sigma_2(\varepsilon)| = |1-\sqrt2| = \sqrt2 - 1 \approx 0.414$. So
$$l(\varepsilon) = \big(\log(1+\sqrt2),\ \log(\sqrt2-1)\big) \approx (0.881,\ -0.881).$$

Check the hyperplane. The two coordinates sum to $\log\big((1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1)\big) = \log(1) = 0$, since $(1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1) = 2 - 1 = 1$. ✓ The image sits on the line $x + y = 0$, as predicted.
:::

## The Image is a Lattice

<!-- section: unit-lattice -->

:::theorem[The unit lattice has full rank]
The image $l(\mathcal{O}_K^*)$ is a **discrete subgroup (lattice)** in the hyperplane $\{\sum_k x_k = 0\}$, and Dirichlet's theorem says it has *full rank* $r = r_1 + r_2 - 1$: there exist $r$ independent units whose log vectors form a basis of the hyperplane.
:::

The **kernel** of $l$ restricted to units is exactly the roots of unity: a unit with $l(\varepsilon) = 0$ has $|\sigma(\varepsilon)| = 1$ for every embedding, so $\varepsilon$ is an algebraic integer all of whose conjugates lie on the unit circle — by Kronecker's theorem such an element is a root of unity. Combining, $\mathcal{O}_K^* \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^r$.

:::intuition
The log vectors of the units tile the "sum-zero" hyperplane as a perfect grid (lattice) of full dimension — you cannot squeeze in more independent directions than the hyperplane has.

The only units that land at the origin (size $1$ in every embedding) are the roots of unity.
:::

:::example[Verify the lattice picture for $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.]
Step 1: The hyperplane. $r_1 = 2$, $r_2 = 0$, so $r = 1$ and the hyperplane is the line $x + y = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
:::

:::solution
1. A basis vector. From the previous section, $l(1+\sqrt2) = (c, -c)$ with $c = \log(1+\sqrt2) \approx 0.881$. This nonzero vector lies on the line $x+y = 0$ and generates a rank-$1$ lattice $\{(kc, -kc) : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.
2. Kernel. The only units with $l = (0,0)$ are those with $|\sigma_1| = |\sigma_2| = 1$, namely $\pm1$ — the roots of unity $\mu_K$.
3. Conclusion: $\mathcal{O}_K^* = \{\pm(1+\sqrt2)^k\} \cong \{\pm1\}\times\mathbb{Z} = \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^1$, exactly $\mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^r$ with $r = 1$. ✓
:::

## Regulator

<!-- section: unit-regulator -->

Choose a basis $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_r$ of the free part of $\mathcal{O}_K^*$ (a set of fundamental units).

:::definition[Regulator]{#regulator}
Form the $r \times (r_1+r_2)$ matrix of log coordinates $\big(l(\varepsilon_i)_k\big)$. Because every row sums to $0$, one column is redundant; **delete any one column** to get an $r \times r$ matrix. The **regulator** $R_K$ is the absolute value of its determinant.
:::

This is the covolume of the unit lattice in the hyperplane, and it is independent of both which fundamental units and which column you drop (changing the basis multiplies the determinant by $\det \in \{\pm1\}$). Always $R_K > 0$, and it appears in the class number formula.

For a real quadratic field, $r = 1$, so the "matrix" is a single number and $R_K = |\log\varepsilon|$ for the fundamental unit $\varepsilon > 1$.

:::intuition
The regulator is the "volume" of the fundamental box spanned by the log vectors of the fundamental units.

It gauges how *big* the fundamental units are: a small regulator means small units and better rational approximations.
:::

:::example[Compute the regulator of $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.]
Step 1: Fundamental unit and its log vector. $\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$, with $l(\varepsilon) = (\log(1+\sqrt2),\ -\log(1+\sqrt2))$.
:::

:::solution
1. Build the matrix and drop a column. Here $r = 1$, so the log matrix is the row $(\log(1+\sqrt2),\ -\log(1+\sqrt2))$. Deleting the second column leaves the $1\times1$ matrix $[\log(1+\sqrt2)]$.
2. Regulator. $R_K = |\log(1+\sqrt2)| \approx 0.8814$.
3. Sanity check: dropping the *first* column instead gives $|-\log(1+\sqrt2)| = \log(1+\sqrt2)$, the same value — the regulator does not depend on which coordinate we omit. ✓
:::

## Proof Sketch (Minkowski + Dirichlet)

<!-- section: unit-proof-sketch -->

The proof has two halves: *discreteness* and *full rank*.

:::proof
**Discreteness** is the easy half: if the log vectors of a set of units are bounded, then all conjugates $|\sigma(\varepsilon)|$ are bounded, so the $\varepsilon$ are algebraic integers whose minimal polynomials have bounded coefficients — only finitely many such exist. Hence $l(\mathcal{O}_K^*)$ is a discrete subgroup of the hyperplane, i.e. a lattice.

**Full rank** is the substance, and uses Minkowski's convex-body theorem in the embedding space $K \otimes \mathbb{R} \cong \mathbb{R}^{r_1}\times\mathbb{C}^{r_2}$. Any centrally symmetric convex region of volume exceeding $2^n$ times the covolume of $\mathcal{O}_K$ contains a nonzero lattice point (an algebraic integer). Choosing boxes with prescribed side lengths (fixed volume, but stretched in one embedding and squeezed in another) produces nonzero integers of *bounded norm* but with a chosen embedding made small. Finitely many norm values occur, so by pigeonhole two of these integers are associates — their ratio is a unit whose log vector is pushed in the chosen direction. Repeating in $r$ independent directions yields $r$ units whose log vectors are linearly independent, so the lattice has full rank $r = r_1 + r_2 - 1$.
:::

:::intuition
Geometry of numbers (Minkowski) guarantees a "big enough" symmetric box always traps a nonzero algebraic integer; by shaping the box you control which embeddings are small.

Two integers of the same norm differ by a unit, so this manufacturing process spits out units pointed in any direction you like — do it in $r$ directions to get $r$ independent units.
:::

:::example[Illustrate the "associates $\Rightarrow$ unit" step in $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.]
Step 1: Two integers of equal norm. Consider $\alpha = 3 + 2\sqrt2$ and $\beta = 1$, or more tellingly $\alpha = 3+2\sqrt2$ with $N(\alpha) = 3^2 - 2\cdot 2^2 = 9 - 8 = 1$. An element of norm $1$ is *already* a unit.
:::

:::solution
1. Ratio of associates. Take $\alpha = 7 + 5\sqrt2$ and $\alpha' = 1+\sqrt2$; then $N(\alpha) = 49 - 50 = -1$ and $N(\alpha') = -1$, so both have norm $-1$. Their ratio $\dfrac{7+5\sqrt2}{1+\sqrt2} = (7+5\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1) = 7\sqrt2 - 7 + 10 - 5\sqrt2 = 3 + 2\sqrt2$ has norm $\dfrac{-1}{-1} = 1$, a unit.
2. It is a genuine unit. $3+2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2$, indeed a unit ($N = 1$), with log vector $2\,l(1+\sqrt2)$ — pushed twice as far in the same direction, illustrating how repeating the construction moves along the lattice. ✓
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **The rank is r1 + r2**: No, r1+r2 -1, because of the norm=±1 constraint (sum logs=0).
- **All units are ±1**: Only for fields with r=0 (Q and imaginary quadratic with finite units).
- **The regulator is an integer**: No, usually transcendental or irrational, like log(1+sqrt2).
- **Unit theorem gives all solutions to norm equations**: It gives the units (norm ±1), but for norm = other fixed, there may be finitely many or none (S-unit equations etc).
- **Computing fundamental units is easy**: For large degree or disc it is hard (like class group, requires LLL or enumeration in the unit lattice).
