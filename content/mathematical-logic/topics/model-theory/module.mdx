# Model Theory

Model theory studies the relationship between formal languages (syntax) and the mathematical structures that interpret them (semantics). It answers questions such as: when do two structures satisfy exactly the same sentences? How can we build new models from old ones? What properties are preserved under various constructions?

It is the branch of logic that most directly connects syntax to "what the world could be like."

## Structures and Satisfaction

<!-- section: structures -->

Model theory lives at the interface between the purely syntactic world of formal languages and the semantic world of concrete mathematical structures that "make the sentences true." A structure (or model) supplies a universe of objects together with interpretations of the symbols in the language; satisfaction tells you whether a given sentence comes out true in that universe.

The satisfaction relation is defined recursively, mirroring the inductive construction of formulas: atomic formulas are checked directly against the interpretations, Boolean combinations follow truth tables, and quantifiers range over the domain of the structure. Two structures are elementarily equivalent when they satisfy exactly the same first-order sentences, even if they are not isomorphic.

In this section you will learn the precise definition of a structure, how satisfaction works, the distinction between theories and models, and the basic constructions (elementary submodels, ultrapowers) that let you build new models with prescribed properties.

A **structure** (or model) $\mathcal{M}$ for a first-order language consists of a domain plus interpretations for all the constant, function, and relation symbols.

The **satisfaction relation** $\mathcal{M} \models \phi[\mathbf{a}]$ (read: $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies formula $\phi$ under assignment $\mathbf{a}$) is defined recursively on formula complexity:

- Atomic: by the interpretation of the predicate/relation.
- Boolean connectives: classical truth tables.
- Quantifiers: $\mathcal{M} \models \forall x \phi[x,\mathbf{a}]$ iff for every $b$ in the domain, $\mathcal{M} \models \phi[b,\mathbf{a}]$.

For sentences (no free variables) we simply write $\mathcal{M} \models \phi$.

:::intuition
A structure is a concrete "world" together with names for its important objects, functions, and relations. The natural numbers with + and < is one structure for the language of arithmetic.

Satisfaction is "the sentence comes out true when you plug in the real meanings in this world."
:::

:::example[evaluating satisfaction step by step.]
Take the structure $\mathcal{M}$ with domain $\{0,1,2\}$, constant symbol $c$ interpreted as $c^{\mathcal{M}} = 0$, and unary function symbol $S$ interpreted as successor mod $3$, so $S^{\mathcal{M}}(0)=1$, $S^{\mathcal{M}}(1)=2$, $S^{\mathcal{M}}(2)=0$. Does $\mathcal{M} \models \forall x\,(S(S(S(x))) = x)$?
:::

:::solution
**Step 1 (unfold the quantifier).** By the recursive clause for $\forall$, this holds iff for *every* $b \in \{0,1,2\}$ we have $\mathcal{M} \models (S(S(S(x))) = x)[b]$.
**Step 2 (evaluate the term for each $b$).** Applying $S^{\mathcal{M}}$ three times cycles all the way around: $b=0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 0$; $b=1 \to 2 \to 0 \to 1$; $b=2 \to 0 \to 1 \to 2$. In every case the result equals $b$.
**Step 3 (check the atomic formula).** The atomic formula $t = x$ is true under the interpretation of $=$ exactly when both sides denote the same domain element, which we just verified for all three $b$.
**Sanity check.** $S$ is a bijection of period $3$, so $S^3$ is the identity — cubing a $3$-cycle gives the identity, matching Step 2. Hence $\mathcal{M} \models \forall x\,(S(S(S(x))) = x)$. ✓

Note $\mathcal{M}$ is *not* the standard naturals: it satisfies the sentence above, which is false in $(\mathbb{N}, S)$, so satisfaction genuinely depends on the world you plug into.
:::

:::example[counting structures on a fixed domain.]
How many distinct structures are there for a language with exactly one constant symbol $c$ and one unary function symbol $f$, on a fixed domain of size $3$? A structure is determined by *choosing the interpretations of all symbols*, so we count independently and multiply:
:::

:::solution
**Step 1 (interpret $c$).** The constant can be any of the $3$ domain elements: $3$ choices.
**Step 2 (interpret $f$).** A unary function is any map from the $3$-element domain to itself; there are $3^3 = 27$ such maps.
**Step 3 (multiply, since choices are independent).** $3 \times 27 = 81$ distinct structures.

Sanity check: if we drop $f$ and keep only $c$ there would be $3$ structures, and each is refined by one of $27$ function choices, giving $3 \times 27 = 81$. ✓
:::

## Elementary Equivalence and Elementary Substructures

<!-- section: elementary -->

Two structures $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ are **elementarily equivalent** ($\mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{N}$) if they satisfy exactly the same first-order sentences: for every sentence $\phi$, $\mathcal{M} \models \phi \iff \mathcal{N} \models \phi$.

They may be non-isomorphic yet elementarily equivalent (e.g., $(\mathbb{Q},<)$ and $(\mathbb{R},<)$ are both dense linear orders without endpoints, hence elementarily equivalent by quantifier elimination).

A substructure $\mathcal{N} \subseteq \mathcal{M}$ is an **elementary substructure** ($\mathcal{N} \preceq \mathcal{M}$) if for every formula $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ and tuple $\mathbf{a}$ from $\mathcal{N}$, $\mathcal{N} \models \phi[\mathbf{a}] \iff \mathcal{M} \models \phi[\mathbf{a}]$. (Recall a *substructure* means $\mathcal{N}$'s domain sits inside $\mathcal{M}$'s, contains all interpreted constants, and is closed under all interpreted functions, with relations and functions inherited by restriction. "Elementary" is the much stronger demand that $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{M}$ agree on *all* formulas with parameters from $\mathcal{N}$, not just the quantifier-free ones.)

Checking the full definition directly is impractical, so the standard tool is the **Tarski–Vaught test**: a substructure $\mathcal{N} \subseteq \mathcal{M}$ satisfies $\mathcal{N} \preceq \mathcal{M}$ if and only if for every formula $\psi(x, \mathbf{y})$ and every tuple $\mathbf{a}$ from $\mathcal{N}$, *whenever there is a witness $b \in \mathcal{M}$ with $\mathcal{M} \models \psi[b, \mathbf{a}]$, there is already a witness $b' \in \mathcal{N}$ with $\mathcal{M} \models \psi[b', \mathbf{a}]$.* In words: $\mathcal{N}$ never has to leave home to satisfy an existential it can see in $\mathcal{M}$.

:::intuition
Elementary equivalence: the two worlds answer "yes" or "no" to exactly the same first-order questions, even if one is "bigger" or has extra hidden structure that first-order logic cannot see.

Elementary submodel: when you zoom in on a sub-universe, every first-order fact that was true there remains true in the big universe (and vice versa for the sub-universe).
:::

:::example[elementary equivalence without isomorphism.]
Claim: $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$ and $(\mathbb{R}, <)$ are elementarily equivalent but not isomorphic (in the language with just the order symbol $<$).
:::

:::solution
**Step 1 (they cannot be isomorphic).** An isomorphism is in particular a bijection of the domains, but $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable and $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. So no isomorphism exists.
**Step 2 (both are dense linear orders without endpoints, DLO).** Each is linearly ordered; between any two elements lies a third ($\tfrac{p+q}{2}$ works in both); and neither has a least or greatest element. These are exactly the axioms of DLO.
**Step 3 (DLO is complete).** The theory DLO admits quantifier elimination, and a quantifier-free sentence in the pure language of $<$ (no constants) is either a tautology or a contradiction, decided identically in every DLO. Hence any two DLOs satisfy exactly the same sentences.
**Conclusion.** Therefore $(\mathbb{Q},<) \equiv (\mathbb{R},<)$ even though they are non-isomorphic — first-order logic over $<$ simply cannot detect the cardinality gap.
:::

:::example[a substructure that is *not* elementary.]
Consider $(\mathbb{Z}, <)$ as a substructure of $(\mathbb{Q}, <)$. It is a genuine substructure (integers are closed under nothing extra, order inherited), but it is *not* elementary. Apply the Tarski–Vaught test with the formula $\psi(x, a_1, a_2) \equiv (a_1 < x \wedge x < a_2)$ and parameters $a_1 = 0,\ a_2 = 1$ from $\mathbb{Z}$: in $\mathbb{Q}$ the witness $x = \tfrac12$ satisfies $0 < x < 1$, yet *no integer* lies strictly between $0$ and $1$. The test fails, so $(\mathbb{Z},<) \not\preceq (\mathbb{Q},<)$. Concretely, the sentence "between any two ordered points there is a third" (density) is true in $\mathbb{Q}$ and false in $\mathbb{Z}$, so they are not even elementarily equivalent. This is exactly why "substructure" and "elementary substructure" must be kept apart.
:::

## Ultraproducts and Łoś's Theorem

<!-- section: ultraproducts -->

First, an **ultrafilter** $U$ on an index set $I$ is a collection of "large" subsets of $I$ that is closed under supersets and finite intersections, contains $I$, excludes $\emptyset$, and — the defining maximality property — for *every* $A \subseteq I$ contains exactly one of $A$ or its complement $I \setminus A$. A *principal* ultrafilter is one of the form $\{A : i_0 \in A\}$ for a fixed point $i_0$ ("large" means "contains $i_0$"); any other is *non-principal*, and on an infinite $I$ a non-principal ultrafilter contains all cofinite sets.

Given a family of structures $\{\mathcal{M}_i\}_{i\in I}$ and an ultrafilter $U$ on $I$, the **ultraproduct** $\prod_U \mathcal{M}_i$ has as its domain the set of equivalence classes of tuples $(a_i)_{i \in I}$ from the direct product $\prod_i \mathcal{M}_i$, under the relation "$(a_i) \sim (b_i)$ iff $\{i : a_i = b_i\} \in U$" (equal on a $U$-large set). Functions and relations are computed coordinatewise and then read off "on a large set." When all the factors are the same structure $\mathcal{M}$, the ultraproduct is called the **ultrapower** $\mathcal{M}^I / U$.

**Łoś's theorem**: For any formula $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ and classes $[\mathbf{a}]$ in the ultraproduct, $\prod_U \mathcal{M}_i \models \phi[\,[\mathbf{a}]\,]$ if and only if $\{i \in I : \mathcal{M}_i \models \phi[\mathbf{a}_i]\} \in U$. In the sentence case: $\phi$ holds in the ultraproduct iff it holds in a $U$-large ("almost all") set of factors.

This is a powerful model-building tool: you can take ultraproducts of finite structures to obtain infinite ones that still satisfy the same first-order sentences.

:::intuition
Ultraproduct is a "majority vote" construction across many worlds. A property holds in the big new world precisely when it holds in a "large" (according to the ultrafilter) number of the original worlds.

It lets logicians manufacture exotic models while preserving all first-order truths.
:::

:::example[building an infinite natural number.]
Take countably many copies of the standard naturals, i.e. the ultrapower $\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}} / U$ where $U$ is a *non-principal* ultrafilter on $\mathbb{N}$. Let $\alpha = [(0,1,2,3,\dots)]$, the class of the identity sequence, and for a fixed standard $n$ let $\underline{n} = [(n,n,n,\dots)]$, the class of the constant sequence. Claim: $\alpha > \underline{n}$ in the ultrapower for every standard $n$, so $\alpha$ is an "infinite" element.
:::

:::solution
**Step 1 (translate via Łoś).** By Łoś's theorem, $\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}/U \models \underline{n} < \alpha$ iff $\{i \in \mathbb{N} : \mathbb{N} \models n < i\} \in U$, i.e. iff $\{i : i > n\} \in U$.
**Step 2 (the index set is large).** The set $\{i : i > n\} = \{n+1, n+2, \dots\}$ is *cofinite* (it omits only the finitely many $0,\dots,n$).
**Step 3 (non-principal $\Rightarrow$ contains all cofinite sets).** A non-principal ultrafilter contains every cofinite set: it contains no finite set (a finite set in $U$ would force a single point into every member, making $U$ principal), hence by the one-of-$A$-or-complement law it contains the complement of each finite set. So $\{i : i > n\} \in U$.
**Conclusion.** Thus $\underline{n} < \alpha$ for every standard $n$: the class $\alpha$ exceeds all standard naturals. Yet by Łoś every first-order sentence true in $\mathbb{N}$ is true in the ultrapower, so this nonstandard model is elementarily equivalent to $\mathbb{N}$ — an infinite "natural number" obeying all the same first-order arithmetic. ✓

Contrast: if $U$ were *principal*, say generated by $\{i_0\}$, then Łoś reduces every sentence to its truth at coordinate $i_0$, and the ultrapower is just $\mathbb{N}$ again with no new elements — no infinite number appears.
:::

## The Completeness Theorem Revisited

<!-- section: completeness -->

Gödel's completeness theorem can be stated model-theoretically: a set of sentences $\Gamma$ is consistent (no proof of contradiction) if and only if it has a model.

The proof usually proceeds by building a term model (Henkin construction) or via compactness + ultraproducts. The Henkin idea is to make the *syntax itself* into a model: elements of the model are (equivalence classes of) closed terms of the language, so that a term literally names the object it describes. The one obstacle is existentials — a consistent theory may assert $\exists x\,\psi(x)$ without any term that provably witnesses it — so the construction first adds, for each existential formula $\exists x\,\psi(x)$, a fresh **witness constant** $c_\psi$ together with the axiom $\exists x\,\psi(x) \to \psi(c_\psi)$, then extends to a maximal consistent theory. On the closed terms of the enlarged language, the canonical structure then satisfies exactly the sentences in that maximal theory.

This gives a tight link: syntactic consistency ⇔ semantic satisfiability.

:::intuition
Think of your axioms as a wish-list for a world. Completeness promises that if the wish-list never contradicts itself (the proof machine can never derive $\bot$), then some real world granting *all* the wishes actually exists — possibly huge and weird, but real. "No contradiction on paper" is upgraded to "an actual model out there."

This is why we can confidently study "the theory of groups" — consistency (easy to see: exhibit one group) guarantees the whole space of models exists.
:::

:::example[consistency gives a model, via Henkin witnessing.]
Consider the theory $T = \{\exists x\,P(x),\ \exists x\,\neg P(x)\}$ in a language with one unary predicate $P$. Does it have a model? The completeness theorem says yes as soon as $T$ is consistent — let us trace the Henkin construction.
:::

:::solution
**Step 1 (add witness constants).** $T$ has two existentials, so add fresh constants $c_1, c_2$ and the Henkin axioms $\exists x\,P(x) \to P(c_1)$ and $\exists x\,\neg P(x) \to \neg P(c_2)$.
**Step 2 (extend to a maximal consistent set).** Adjoining these keeps the theory consistent (the constants are new, so they cannot create a contradiction); extend to a complete, maximal consistent theory $T^*$ deciding every sentence. Since $\exists x\,P(x) \in T^*$, modus ponens forces $P(c_1) \in T^*$; likewise $\neg P(c_2) \in T^*$.
**Step 3 (read off the term model).** The domain is the closed terms modulo provable equality — here $\{c_1, c_2\}$ (distinct, since nothing forces $c_1 = c_2$). Interpret $P$ by "$P(t) \in T^*$", giving $P^{\mathcal{M}} = \{c_1\}$.
**Sanity check.** In $\mathcal{M}$, $c_1$ satisfies $P$ so $\exists x\,P(x)$ holds; $c_2$ satisfies $\neg P$ so $\exists x\,\neg P(x)$ holds. Both axioms are true, so $\mathcal{M} \models T$. ✓ Consistency did indeed produce a concrete model.
:::

:::example[completeness of a theory forces elementary equivalence.]
The theory ACF$_0$ of algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero admits quantifier elimination, and adding the axioms (field axioms, "every nonconstant polynomial has a root", $\operatorname{char} = 0$) fixes the truth value of every quantifier-free sentence — hence ACF$_0$ is *complete*: it decides every sentence. Therefore any two of its models — e.g. $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ (the algebraic numbers) and $\mathbb{C}$ — satisfy exactly the same first-order sentences and are elementarily equivalent, even though $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is countable and $\mathbb{C}$ is uncountable, so they are certainly not isomorphic. This is the completeness-theorem principle "complete theory $\Rightarrow$ all models elementarily equivalent" in action.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Elementary equivalence is not isomorphism:** Equating elementary equivalence with isomorphism is a mistake. First-order logic cannot distinguish all structures; cardinality, higher-order properties, and "standardness" often require second-order or infinitary logic.

- **Same sentences does not mean identical:** Thinking that if two structures satisfy the same sentences they are "the same" is incorrect. They can differ wildly (different cardinalities, non-isomorphic).

- **Domains are non-empty:** Forgetting that the domain of a structure must be non-empty leads to errors. Empty structures are usually disallowed in classical FOL.

- **Substructure versus elementary substructure:** Confusing substructure with elementary substructure is common. A subring need not be an elementary subring.

- **Principal ultrafilters give ordinary products:** Believing ultraproducts always produce "non-standard" models is wrong. If the ultrafilter is principal the ultraproduct is just one of the factors.

- **Categoricity is rare:** Assuming every complete theory has a unique model up to isomorphism (categoricity) is false for most. $\aleph_0$-categoricity is rare and characterized by the Ryll-Nardzewski theorem.
