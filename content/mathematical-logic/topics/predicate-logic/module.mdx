# Predicate Logic

Predicate logic, or first-order logic (FOL), extends propositional logic by allowing us to talk about objects and their properties and relations. Instead of treating "Socrates is mortal" as an atomic proposition, we decompose it as $M(s)$ where $s$ names Socrates and $M$ is the predicate "is mortal".

We also add quantifiers: "for all" ($\forall$) and "there exists" ($\exists$). This lets us express statements such as "every natural number has a successor" or "there exists a prime larger than 100". FOL is expressive enough for most of mathematics yet still decidable in important fragments and has a complete proof system (Gödel's completeness theorem).

## Predicates, Constants, Functions, and Variables

<!-- section: predicates -->

A **predicate** (or relation symbol) of arity $k$ takes $k$ arguments and yields a truth value. $P(x)$ is a unary predicate (property); $R(x,y)$ is binary (relation).

**Constants** (names) denote specific objects: $a, b, c, \ldots$ or $0, \pi, \text{Socrates}$.

**Function symbols** produce new objects from old: $f(x)$, $+(x,y)$, $\text{successor}(n)$.

**Variables** $x,y,z$ stand for arbitrary objects; they are not yet assigned meaning until we interpret or quantify.

A **term** is anything that names an object: a constant, a variable, or a function symbol applied to other terms. So $x$, $a$, $f(x)$, and $f(f(x,a),y)$ are all terms. A **term is not a formula** — it has no truth value; it just picks out an object.

An **atomic formula** is a predicate symbol applied to terms (constants, variables, or function applications), e.g. $R(f(a), x)$ applies the binary predicate $R$ to the term $f(a)$ and the variable $x$. Atomic formulas are the smallest pieces that carry a truth value; everything else is built from them with connectives ($\land, \lor, \neg, \to$) and quantifiers.

**ELI5**

- Predicates are like adjectives or verbs with blanks: "__ is even", "__ loves __". Fill the blanks with names or variables and you get a claim that is true or false of those particular things.
- Constants are proper names. Functions are "machines" that take input objects and output a new object (successor function turns 3 into 4).

**Worked Example:**

Work in the language of arithmetic: constant $0$, unary function $S$ (successor), binary functions $+$ and $\times$, and predicate $=$ (equality, usually built-in).

Classify each expression.

1. $S(S(0))$ — a **term**. It is the function $S$ applied to the term $S(0)$, which is $S$ applied to the constant $0$. Under the standard interpretation it names the number $2$. Being a term, it has no truth value on its own.
2. $S(0) = S(S(0))$ — an **atomic formula** (predicate $=$ applied to two terms). Under the standard interpretation it says $1 = 2$, so it is **false**.
3. $x = 0$ — an **atomic formula** too, but it is **open**: the free variable $x$ means it is neither true nor false until we assign $x$ a value.

Now count function-symbol occurrences in the term $f(f(x, a), y)$ (in a language with a binary function $f$). Read the term as a tree: the outer $f$ is one occurrence, and its first argument $f(x,a)$ is a second occurrence. The leaves $x$, $a$, $y$ are a variable, a constant, and a variable — none is a function symbol. **Total: $2$ occurrences of $f$.** Sanity check: a function symbol occurrence is exactly one "call," and there are two calls to $f$ nested here.

## Quantifiers and Their Meaning

<!-- section: quantifiers -->

The **universal quantifier** $\forall x \, \phi(x)$ means "for every object $x$ in the domain, $\phi(x)$ holds".

The **existential quantifier** $\exists x \, \phi(x)$ means "there is at least one object $x$ in the domain such that $\phi(x)$ holds".

Quantifiers range over the **domain** (universe) of the interpretation — the set of objects we are talking about. Changing the domain changes truth.

Quantifiers can nest: $\forall x \exists y \, (y > x)$ ("there is no largest number").

**Negating quantifiers.** To push a negation past a quantifier, flip the quantifier and negate the body (the quantifier version of De Morgan's laws):

$$\neg \forall x \, \phi \;\equiv\; \exists x \, \neg\phi, \qquad \neg \exists x \, \phi \;\equiv\; \forall x \, \neg\phi.$$

Intuition: "not everyone passed" means "someone failed"; "no one passed" means "everyone failed". You apply this repeatedly to move a negation all the way inside a formula, flipping every quantifier it crosses.

**ELI5**

- $\forall$ is "no matter which one you pick, the claim is still true". Like a universal warranty: every customer gets a refund.
- $\exists$ is "I can point to one that works". Like "there exists a winning move" — you don't need to list all, just show one example suffices.
- Order matters: $\forall x \exists y$ (for every x I can find a y) is weaker than $\exists y \forall x$ (there is one y that works for every x).

**Worked Example:**

"Every person has a mother": $\forall x \exists y \, \text{Mother}(y, x)$.

"There is someone who is everyone's mother": $\exists y \forall x \, \text{Mother}(y, x)$.

These are not equivalent; the second is much stronger (and false in normal human domains). In general $\exists y \forall x \, \phi \;\to\; \forall x \exists y \, \phi$ is valid, but the converse is not: one witness that works for all is a special case of each object getting its own witness.

**Worked Example (negating a quantified sentence):**

Negate $\forall x \, (P(x) \to \exists y \, Q(x,y))$ and simplify until no negation sits in front of a quantifier.

- Step 1 — flip the outer $\forall$: $\;\neg\forall x\,\psi \equiv \exists x\,\neg\psi$, giving $\exists x \, \neg(P(x) \to \exists y \, Q(x,y))$.
- Step 2 — negate the implication: $\;\neg(A \to B) \equiv A \land \neg B$, giving $\exists x \, (P(x) \land \neg\exists y \, Q(x,y))$.
- Step 3 — flip the inner $\exists$: $\;\neg\exists y\,\chi \equiv \forall y\,\neg\chi$, giving $\exists x \, (P(x) \land \forall y \, \neg Q(x,y))$.

Sanity check: the original says "every $P$ has some $Q$-partner"; its negation should say "some $P$ has no $Q$-partner at all" — which is exactly $\exists x \, (P(x) \land \forall y \, \neg Q(x,y))$. ✓

**Worked Example (moving a quantifier past a subformula):**

The rule $\forall x \, (P(x) \to Q) \equiv (\forall x \, P(x) \to Q)$ holds **only when $x$ does not occur free in $Q$**. Here is why the condition matters.

- If $x$ is *not* free in $Q$: the value of $Q$ never depends on which $x$ we look at, so pulling the universal in front of only $P$ is safe. (This is the standard "quantifier can leave a subformula it doesn't affect" rule.)
- If $x$ *is* free in $Q$, say $Q = Q(x)$: take domain $\{1,2\}$ with $P^I = \{1\}$ and $Q^I = \{2\}$. The left side $\forall x\,(P(x)\to Q(x))$ checks $x=1$ ($P$ true, $Q$ false → implication false), so the left side is **false**. But on the right, $\forall x\,P(x)$ is false (2 is not in $P$), so $(\forall x\,P(x)) \to Q(x)$ is **true**. Different truth values, so the equivalence fails. ✓

## Scope, Free and Bound Variables

<!-- section: scope -->

The **scope** of a quantifier is the subformula it governs, usually indicated by parentheses or convention.

A variable occurrence is **bound** if it lies inside the scope of a quantifier that uses the same variable name; otherwise it is **free**.

A formula with no free variables is a **sentence** (closed formula) and can be true or false in an interpretation. Open formulas (with free vars) express properties or relations.

Example: $\forall x (P(x) \to Q(x))$ is closed. $P(x) \to Q(y)$ has free $x$ and $y$.

Renaming bound variables is safe (alpha-equivalence): $\forall x \phi(x) \equiv \forall y \phi(y)$.

**ELI5**

- Free variables are like pronouns without antecedent in the sentence: "He is tall" — who? You need context (an assignment of values).
- Bound variables are dummy: "For every person x, if x is a student then x studies" — the 'x' is just a placeholder; you can rename it to 'z' without changing meaning.
- Scope is the "jurisdiction" of the quantifier — like parentheses in arithmetic. $\exists x (P(x) \land Q(x))$ vs $(\exists x P(x)) \land Q(x)$ — different meanings.

**Worked Example (which quantifier binds which occurrence):**

In $\exists x (P(x) \land \forall x \, Q(x))$, the $x$ in $Q(x)$ sits inside the inner $\forall x$, so it is bound by that $\forall$; the $x$ in $P(x)$ is bound by the outer $\exists$. When two quantifiers reuse the same name, an occurrence is captured by the **innermost** enclosing quantifier of that name. To avoid this confusion, rename the inner one: the formula is equivalent to $\exists x (P(x) \land \forall y \, Q(y))$, which makes the two roles of the variable visible.

**Worked Example (counting free occurrences):**

Count the free variable occurrences (with multiplicity) in $\forall x \, (P(x,y) \to \exists y \, Q(y,z))$. Go occurrence by occurrence, checking whether each sits inside a quantifier of its own name.

- $x$ in $P(x,y)$: inside $\forall x$ → **bound**.
- $y$ in $P(x,y)$: the only $y$-quantifier is $\exists y$, whose scope is just $Q(y,z)$; this $y$ is in the antecedent, **outside** that scope → **free** (occurrence 1).
- $y$ in $Q(y,z)$: inside $\exists y$ → **bound**.
- $z$ in $Q(y,z)$: no quantifier for $z$ anywhere → **free** (occurrence 2).

**Total: $2$ free occurrences** (one $y$, one $z$). Sanity check: the distinct free *variables* are $\{y, z\}$, and here each appears free exactly once, so the occurrence count matches the variable count.

## Interpretations and Satisfaction

<!-- section: interpretations -->

An **interpretation** (or structure, model) for a first-order language consists of:

- A non-empty domain $D$.
- For each constant $c$, an element $c^I \in D$.
- For each $k$-ary function $f$, a function $f^I : D^k \to D$.
- For each $k$-ary predicate $P$, a relation $P^I \subseteq D^k$.

An assignment of values to free variables, together with the interpretation, determines whether a formula is satisfied. The domain $D$ must be **non-empty** in classical FOL — otherwise $\exists x \, \phi$ could never be satisfied while $\forall x \, \phi$ would be vacuously true, breaking standard laws.

We write $\mathcal{M} \models \phi$ when sentence $\phi$ is true in model $\mathcal{M}$. A **sentence** has no free variables, so its truth depends only on $\mathcal{M}$, not on any variable assignment — this is why we can speak of $\phi$ being simply "true in $\mathcal{M}$".

**ELI5**

- An interpretation is "picking the actual universe and what the symbols mean in it". The same sentence "$\forall x (P(x) \to Q(x))$" can be true in one universe (all P things are also Q) and false in another.
- Satisfaction is "this particular assignment of objects to the free slots makes the claim hold in this model".

**Worked Example (checking a unary predicate):**

Language with unary $P$. Domain $D = \{1,2,3\}$. Let $P^I = \{1,2\}$.

- $\exists x \, P(x)$: is there some element in $P^I$? Yes ($1$ works) → **true**.
- $\forall x \, P(x)$: is *every* element in $P^I$? No, $3 \notin P^I$ → **false**.
- $\exists x \, \neg P(x)$: is there an element outside $P^I$? Yes ($3$) → **true**.

If we shrink the domain to $\{1,2\}$ and set $P^I = \{1,2\}$ (all of $D$), then $\forall x \, P(x)$ becomes **true**. Same symbols, different structure, different truth value — that is the whole point of an interpretation.

**Worked Example (nested quantifiers over a relation):**

Domain $D = \{a,b\}$, binary relation $R^I = \{(a,a),(a,b)\}$. Is $\exists x \, \forall y \, R(x,y)$ true?

- The claim asks for **one** $x$ that relates to **every** $y$. Test each candidate $x$.
- Try $x = a$: need $R(a,a)$ and $R(a,b)$. Both pairs are in $R^I$ → the inner $\forall y$ holds for $x=a$.
- Since a witness exists, the existential is satisfied → **true**.

(Had we asked $\forall x \, \exists y \, R(x,y)$ instead, we would also need $b$ to relate to *something*; but $R^I$ has no pair starting with $b$, so that different sentence would be false — a reminder that quantifier order changes the question.)

**Worked Example (counting interpretations):**

How many interpretations of a single binary relation $R$ exist over a domain of size $2$? An interpretation $R^I$ is *any* subset of $D \times D$. With $|D| = 2$ there are $2 \times 2 = 4$ ordered pairs, and a subset either includes or excludes each pair independently, so there are $2^4 = 16$ possible relations. Sanity check: the empty relation and the full relation $D\times D$ are two of these $16$ extremes.

## Common Mistakes

- **Quantifier scope and domain:** Treat quantifiers with care for scope and domain rather than like propositional connectives or English plurals. "All that glitters is not gold" is classically misparsed; the logical form is usually $\neg \forall x (G(x) \to \text{Gold}(x))$.

- **Quantifier order:** Swapping quantifier order without justification is invalid. $\forall x \exists y$ ("everyone has a mother") is not equivalent to $\exists y \forall x$ ("someone is mother of everyone").

- **Non-empty domains:** Forgetting that the domain must be non-empty leads to errors. Some texts allow empty domains; classical FOL usually requires non-empty.

- **Free versus bound:** Confusing free and bound variables is common. Writing proofs that treat a free $x$ as if universally quantified without the $\forall$ intro rule is incorrect.

- **Intended model versus validity:** Assuming that truth in the "intended" model implies validity (truth in all models) gets the direction wrong. Soundness goes the other way.

- **Nested quantifier capture:** Using the same variable name for nested quantifiers without renaming leads to accidental capture.
